# Newcomer read · g09 (V.7 The Mirror in the Grain · Book Six heading · VI.1 The Homecomings)

**How I read it.** I came in cold. I have never heard of this world and opened no other file. I read the tales top to bottom, once straight through and then a second time slowly, marking each stumble. The file ends with a WRITER'S NOTES section. I treated that as backstage material and did **not** let it answer any of my questions. Everything below comes from the tale text alone (lines 1–327).

`{{Orvenna}}`, `{{Enrella}}` and the rest show up with curly braces in the raw file. I took those as markup the reader won't see and ignored them, except where the *position* of the name trips the reader (see line 19).

Line numbers refer to `plain/g09.md`.

## V.7 · The Mirror in the Grain

### Title and when-line (l.3–5)
- **"The Mirror in the Grain"**: at the start this meant nothing to me ("grain" of what? wheat? wood?). By the end of Part III I understood it: the enemy copies us three times (the bond, the lie, the tomorrow). That's a good title in hindsight. It isn't a stumble, just a slow fuse.
- **"each on the night its carving was known"**: this stopped me. *What* carving? Known *by whom*? "Known" as a verb for reading something is strange English, and its meaning isn't given until line 63 ("We call that a knowing"), sixty lines later. On a first read it sounds like a typo for "made" or "shown."

### Headnote (l.7)
- **"Seren Two-Inks"**: I'm curious why "Two-Inks", but that's a pleasant curiosity, not a stumble.
- **"(We say a tale is laid, like a stone in a wall, because a laid tale stays.)"**: charming, and once is the right amount.
- **"It's this same lamp, here beside the old gate-stone that the teller holds."**: I had to read this twice. Where is "here"? Who is "the teller"? I thought Seren was the writer, so now there's a teller too, holding a stone, in the present. The frame doesn't come into focus.

### "Hold the stone, Seren." (l.11)
- **Who is speaking?** It reads like someone handing Seren the stone, but no one has been introduced. Or is Seren telling herself? I couldn't tell. Compare VI.1, where "Give me the stone. No, I'll stand." works at once because the headnote has just told me Kael is the teller. Here the headnote says Seren *wrote* it and *held the lamp*, not that she is telling it now.

### l.13 (the drums, the grey)
- Clear. "the grey, the fog bank at sea where the enemy's fleets come from" is a good, quick gloss. Small question: whose drums? I assumed the enemy's, and that was fine.

### l.15 (Enno and Della, the house of one door)
- **"They were the youngest bonded pair in our guild of builders, the Stonewrights: two people joined as one from birth."**: I read this twice. The colon makes "two people joined as one from birth" describe *the Stonewrights*, as if the whole guild were two people. It should hang off "bonded pair."
- **"joined as one from birth"**: literal or figurative? Twins? Betrothed as babies? Something magical? I didn't know yet. Line 25 later shows it's literal (one dies, so does the other), and that payoff is good. But at this point I didn't know how seriously to take "joined".
- This paragraph does a lot of defining in one go: bonded pair, guild, Stonewrights, mark, sealing, course. It holds together because the house is a lovely image, but it's the densest paragraph in the tale.
- **"Enno set the chisel, and Della struck it. The house stood one course high, one row of stones. It never stood any higher."**: beautiful. The last sentence gave me a chill.

### l.17 (I came in alone. Tarnel)
- **"Tarnel, the boy I'd shared a cradle with, had died of the cold on the road."**: on first read it sounded like a brother or twin. After the prayer at l.51–53 ("one cradle from you"), I realised cradle-sharing means a bonded pair, so **Seren was bonded and lost her other half.**
- **This raises the biggest question I carried out of the whole tale.** Line 25 says "when one of a pair dies, the other dies too." So why is Seren alive? Maybe the rule only applies after sealing, or maybe Seren is an exception that matters later. Either way the text doesn't say, and an attentive newcomer *will* notice. If it's a deliberate mystery, it needs a half-line from Seren that admits it ("I have never known why I lived"). As it stands, it reads like a continuity slip.
- **"reached it"**: reached the Keep from where? Why were they on a road in the cold? It's a hint of a flight or exodus. That's fine, and I didn't need it explained.

### l.19 (three roads, the breaches, Orvenna)
- **"came at us by three roads at once"**: ships on *roads*? I pictured land roads and got confused. If these are sea-lanes, one word ("by three sea-roads") would fix it.
- **"broke our wall in the east and in the north. There were more breaches than there were pairs to mend them."**: I read this twice. Only two breaches are named, so "more breaches than pairs" seems to mean fewer than two pairs were free, yet the tale is full of pairs. I think it means many breaches spread across east and north, but it reads as two.
- **"Orvenna, Ormund and Penna, our wall-masters"**: this reads as a list of **three people**. The pair-name idea (Ormund + Penna = Orvenna) isn't explained until l.63 ("Together they are Halyna"). Before that point Orvenna, Enrella and Idrenna have all appeared. I worked out the pattern on my own around Idrenna, but the first one tripped me. A small fix would help: "Ormund and Penna, our wall-masters, whom we call by one name, Orvenna."

### l.21 ("They sent a pair apart.")
- Strong one-line paragraph.

### l.23 (the old joke)
- **"It is like being one person." / "with four hands and no excuses."**: I understood the call-and-answer. But it reads as a motto more than a joke, and I didn't smile. The uncontracted "It is" stands out against the casual voice around it. I let that go because it's a set phrase.

### l.25 (the law of the bond)
- **"Harm to one is harm to both, the old masters said, and when one of a pair dies, the other dies too. A mile of wall is far."**: very clear, and it creates real dread. "A mile of wall is far" is the best sentence in the opening.
- I was a little unsure why *distance* matters if the rule is about death. Does being apart hurt them, or can a far-off partner just not help? I assumed the second.
- See the Tarnel problem above. This sentence is what makes it a contradiction.

### l.27 ("I didn't see it. Hale did.")
- **Hale**: who? He's explained at l.39 ("our fastest runner"). The forward reference is fine and resolves quickly.

### l.29–37 (Della's death)
- I followed every beat, and it's the most moving passage in the file. "She set the chisel herself, and struck it" echoes the first stone perfectly. "Then her mallet stopped in the air." "She looked east, toward Enno." "Lay down with her cheek against the stone." No notes.
- **"I pressed my thumb into the mortar, the way masons do to test it. It was still soft."**: I paused here. What is the soft mortar telling me? That only moments had passed? That the work was unfinished? I wasn't sure until the echo at l.82 ("On Halvard's trowel, the mortar was drying"), and then I liked both. A first-time reader may shrug at l.35.

### l.39 (Hale too late)
- **"Never before had a runner come too late."**: good. "Came at a run" plus "runner" in one sentence is a little heavy, but it's easy to forgive.

### l.41–47 (the mark; "You can't tell.")
- Very good. "You can't tell." landed.
- The image caption says **"the north stone"** before the text explains it (l.49). That's minor.

### l.49 (Stannard, the north stone, Idrenna's prayer)
- Three new names in one paragraph (Stannard, the True Men, the Captain), but each comes with a short tag, and I kept up.
- **The prayer**: clear and moving. "One cradle from you and one stone from us" is where I connected cradle to bond (see Tarnel).

### l.55 (the joke at the grave; the strip)
- **"Over the stone Ormund began Enno and Della's old joke, and Penna finished it."**: lovely.
- **"tied a strip on a pike over them, cut from the torn half of the cloak he wears."**: I read this twice. As written, "cut from the torn half..." attaches to *the pike*. Better: "tied a strip cut from the torn half of his cloak on a pike over them."
- **"the torn half of the cloak he wears"**: *why* is it torn? It's an intriguing mystery, and I was happy to carry it.
- **"this was his first for Stonewrights"**: so no builder had died in four years of siege? It surprised me a little, but it makes the moment heavier, so I'll take it.

### l.57–59 (Kael: "One.")
- Perfect. "Who counts everything" is just enough setup. I got it instantly and it hurt.

### l.61 (the plank, Rhyna, the gate-song)
- This paragraph does two different jobs. It starts with the plank and Rhyna going to the shingle, then cuts to Halvard on the wall and the song. The song is one of the best moments in the tale: "That night no one took the first line... On the second line everyone came in, as though the first had been sung." It deserves its own paragraph, and the plank deserves to start the next one.
- **Rhyna**: who? She's named before she's explained (l.63). "Her husband Halvard" helps, but I didn't know why Rhyna, of all people, goes down to a plank.
- **"a plank came in from the bearer"**: why does a plank matter? I didn't know yet that enemy wood carries messages.

### l.63 (Halyna and the knowing)
- This is the key world-rule, and it's explained well: "When one of them touches the enemy's carved wood, its message comes into both of them whole, like something remembered. We call that a knowing." The simultaneous going-pale on the shingle and on the wall is a great way to *show* the bond.
- **"Together they are Halyna, who lead our builders"**: but Orvenna were just called "our wall-masters." Who's in charge of what? It's minor, but I noticed.
- **Who carves the enemy's wood, and why are its orders cut into the ships themselves?** I didn't need an answer. I'm flagging it because it's the moment the world turns strange, and I enjoyed that.

### l.66 (the first carving)
- ***"The south waits at the dread's edge until the south's reach goes east, and then it goes."***: I read this three times and still don't fully have it. "The dread" gets glossed right after, which is good. But **"the south's reach"** is never explained. Is it *our* south section's people going east to help the breach? Then the third enemy fleet attacks the stripped south? If so, that's exactly how the enemy uses "they sent a pair apart" against us, which is the whole point, and I nearly missed it. The tale also never says whether the south was attacked or what happened there. This is the one carving whose *plot* meaning I needed and didn't get.

### l.68 (the dread)
- **"water where its ships have died"**: ships that *die*. That was my first real hint that the ships are alive. Good.

### l.74–76 (Halyna: "That's how we two work a wall.")
- This landed. The enemy fleets are now moving like a bonded pair. It's chilling, and the title starts to make sense.

### l.80 (Aelthae, Ael'thar, the Guest)
- **"We call each year's fleets a Tide"**: OK. But the tale also uses "When the tide turned" literally three times, plus "Tide-Heart" later. For a beat each time I wondered whether "tide" meant the sea or the fleet.
- **"Aelthae" / "Ael'thar" / "the Guest" / "the rite" / "our outpost" / "our grandparents' time"**: that's five new things in three sentences, at the emotional peak. I followed the *shape* of the hint: the enemy's name for this joined fleet shares a root with something a stranger once said to us, and Seren is afraid of what that means. My guess is that *Ael* means something like "joined" or "one." It was satisfying to make that guess, but I'm not sure I'm right, and a less patient reader would just feel lost.
- **Romanized words.** *Rhenear* (l.136) comes with its English ("the stone-deaf"), and I loved it. *Aelthae* and *Ael'thar* come with none. I understand the withholding is the hook. As a reader, the gap was noticeable *next to* a word that does get translated. Even a partial gloss on *-thae* or *-thar* (if those halves can be given without giving away *Ael*) would make the pair feel like real language instead of a locked box.
- **"I have written the name, and I won't write what I thought."**: a good line.

### l.82 ("the mortar was drying")
- Good echo. This retroactively explains l.35.

### l.84 ("I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.")
- Small stop: Seren was an eyewitness, so who "laid it for" her? I assume it's a fixed formula, and by Part II I accepted it as one.

### Part II, l.90–94 (the old way; Pellow)
- Clear. "The whole wall laughed" told me the "old way" was easy prey. Pellow gets a good, quick portrait. "Look close." works as his tag.

### l.96–100 (pale sails; "They are not green.")
- **"in it a sail went pale. Then a second. Then a third."**: good suspense.
- **"when one of their ships is struck, the sails of others far off go from grey-green to white, like a man's face."**: I followed this as a fact. But the **deduction** lost me. Why do pale sails mean "they are not green"? I *think* it's this: the sails that went pale were far-off ships hidden in the grey, which means more ships were waiting out there, and the loud ones weren't young after all. But "green" is doing two jobs, sail colour (grey-green) and age (young wood). Read literally, "They are not green" seems to say the sails turned white, which I already knew. I had to stop and work this out. The helper line ("He meant they weren't young wood") tells me *what* he meant, but not *how he knew*.

### l.102 (Pellow turns his guns, stands on the parapet)
- Clear, and it shows his nerve.

### l.104 (the Captain's calls)
- **"He gives his calls only in his own voice, at a True Man's side, so he can be with only one battery of guns at a time."**: this is the crux of Pellow's death, and the *why* is missing. Why only in his own voice? A vow, a law, a superstition, pride? Without a reason it reads like an arbitrary rule that gets a man killed, and I found myself angry at the Captain without knowing whether the tale wants me to be. If the reason is told elsewhere, half a clause here would help ("as he has sworn to," "because a call carried by another can be wrong," etc.).
- **"That means move your guns."**: helpful, slightly over-explained, and fine.
- **The lamplighter simile**: nice. It made me read the Captain as careful rather than cold, which complicated my anger in a good way.

### l.106–110 (the hunters; "One at a time is slow.")
- "Every battery he had reached lived. One at a time is slow." Excellent, cold, and clear.
- **Why didn't Pellow move his own guns?** At l.102 he acts "without waiting for orders," so he *can* act alone. Did he choose to stand and fight? Was he waiting for the call after all? The mechanism of his death is fuzzy, and that blunts the grief a little.

### l.112–114 (Pellow dead, glass unbroken)
- Very good.

### l.116 ("Halyna, Halvard and Rhyna, laid")
- Stray comma before "laid," and once again it reads like three people. A fix: "Halyna, Halvard and Rhyna together, laid..." or drop the second comma.

### l.119–124 (the second carving)
- **"they shoot first and far, and move any gun we find."**: I read this twice because the pronouns flip ("they" is us, "we" is the enemy). Line 124 sorts it out ("The line underneath was about us").
- **"The fire was our guns' fire, and the flashes were our guns' flashes"**: a bit of over-explaining here. I'd mostly got it from the carving itself.

### l.126 ("The wood can't lie")
- **"The wood can't lie," I said**: this is the first I've heard that the wood can't lie. It's presented as something everyone knows, but the tale never set it up. A half-line back at l.63 ("and the wood cannot lie") would make this moment pay off.
- **"The fleet that ran at us the old way was the lie, sent to make our guns fire and show the hunters where our guns stood."**: by now this is the third time I've been told it (carving, gloss, this line). It's the one spot in Part II where I felt talked down to.

### l.130–132 ("We taught them, with every gun we kept silent to fool them.")
- I've never seen us keep guns silent to fool anyone. This refers to history I don't have, so it lands as "OK, I'll trust you" rather than as a punch.

### l.136 (Rhenear)
- **"They'd had no word for a lie. We gave them one."**: great.
- **"Rhenear, the stone-deaf."**: I loved this, and the translation is exactly right. I worked out on my own that "stone-deaf" means we can't hear the wood (only Halyna can), and working that out felt good.

### l.138–144 (Pellow's cairn, Hale, Ruan)
- **"I'm not a True Man," Hale said. "I run."**: the best line in Part II.
- Ruan's "Look close" closes the part well. I was moved.

### Part III, l.152 (a whole tomorrow, the heart)
- **"a whole tomorrow carved into its heart, the core of its wood"**: poetic and clear enough. I read "tomorrow" as "a full day's plan."

### l.154–157 (the longest knowing; the carving)
- "Twice I filled the lamp" is a good way to show length. The carving itself is crystal clear.

### l.159 ("They had cut their own defeat into the plan...")
- Over-explaining. The carving one line up says exactly this, so the restatement bored me. Keep the first sentence ("They had cut their own defeat into the plan.") and cut the second.

### l.163–165 ("one hand... And the hand is young.")
- An intriguing hook. I don't know who, and I'm happy not to know yet.

### l.169 (home)
- **"To the young wood of the first fleets, home had only meant the place hurt things run to."**: this is how I learned the young ships ran home when hurt, which was new and quietly sad. It's clear.

### l.171 (wives and fever)
- **"the way wives look up when their men have lain a long time in fever and then ask for their boots."**: the best image in the file. I understood it as the first sign that the long sickness (the war) is breaking. One thing left me uncertain: is the "recovering man" *us*, *them*, or the war? I settled on "the war could end." That ambiguity is fine.

### l.175–181 (closing lines; the lamp)
- "Then let's build a tomorrow they didn't carve for." Good. "I didn't fill it a third time." A lovely close.
- **A gap**: the tale never tells me whether we *did* sink the bearer and its successor, or whether the fleet went home. The part's title ("The Plan That Carved Its Own Defeat") promises it, and Book Six opens with the war won, but I never see the hinge.

## Book Six heading (l.189–191)

## VI.1 · The Homecomings

### When-line (l.195)
- **"on the night its Throne fell"**: for twelve lines I pictured a king's seat or a ruler being overthrown. The gloss comes at l.201 and is good, but a word in the when-line ("its Throne, the enemy ship that held it") would stop the wrong picture forming.

### Headnote (l.197)
- **"(We say a tale is laid, like a stone in a wall, because a laid tale stays.)"** and **"the Captain's own company of twelve"**: both repeat V.7 word for word. If a reader meets these tales one at a time, that's fine. Read back to back, I noticed the repetition and skimmed.
- **"Before he'd begin, he counted my pages."**: delightful. It made Kael a person.

### l.199 ("Give me the stone. No, I'll stand.")
- Works perfectly, because the headnote just told me who's speaking. (This is the contrast with V.7's "Hold the stone, Seren.")

### l.201 (the going-down; the Thrones)
- **The jump.** V.7 ended with a plan in the sixth winter. Here, in the same sixth winter, we're marching down to take back the cities. What happened in between? Did the plan work? Did the enemy go home? I assume other tales cover it, but as a newcomer I felt a missing step.
- **"the ten great enemy ships that had come out of the fog that winter and settled in our ruined cities"**: a good gloss. But "ships" set up an expectation that the next sections break. A ship sits in a forest (Holtward), comes "down out of its cloud" (Highreach), "burst up" from underground (Sandreach), and sits in cellars (Glasspire). Each time, I briefly wondered how a ship gets there. I love the strangeness, but one word here ("ships, if they were ships" or "ships of living wood") would prepare me for it.
- **Why do the Thrones fall?** This is the biggest open question in VI.1. Almost none are fought: the Tide-Heart "sank," the Root-Heart "wouldn't rise," the Burning "went out," the Stone-Grain "crumbled." Only Tidesmeet involves striking. Something made them all die or give up, and the heading hints at "the war went home," but I never learn what happened.

### l.203–205 (oaths; "hold on to them")
- Clear, and it sets up the pattern well. "Hold on to them, because they're ours again" is a nice line.

### Eldhythe (l.207–217)
- **The Tide-Heart "sank into the sea it came from and never rose."**: how, or why? (See above.)
- Corlen's oath, the tiller, the wet finger and "Wait. The sea hasn't finished." all come through. Explaining the saying *after* Voss uses it ("That was his word to his gunners") is slightly backwards, but it works.
- **"the three grey stones... old carved stones from the edge of the fog that no one could read."**: this felt planted. Carved stones from the fog, before the war? Do they connect to the enemy's carving? I didn't get the hint, but I noticed it, and that's probably right. "*The* three grey stones" before they're explained made me feel I'd missed something.
- **"my grandmother's harbour-house"**: so Voss is also from Eldhythe? He's "speaking for Corlen," which made me think it was Corlen's city, not his. The fact that they share a city is never stated.
- This is the busiest haven (Corlen's oath, Voss's succession, the finger, the saying, the house, the stones). It's the one I'd trim.

### Tidesmeet (l.219–225)
- **"Bearer of New Branches, had three heads... none of them grew back"**: a vivid hydra picture, and clear.
- **"in a single holding, one stretch of fighting"**: "holding" is never used again, so why teach me the word? "We struck all three at once" would do. This is the clunkiest gloss in VI.1.
- **"One span is a plank. Three is a city."**: lovely.

### Fenholm (l.227–239)
- **"A town is just the last course on the old," we used to say. Tonight we said it again, and laughed.**: I didn't see why it's funny. Relief, maybe? The laugh confused me more than it moved me.
- **"the rot went out of our mortar"**: I inferred that the Throne's rot had infected the town. That's fine.
- **Old Ebba and "Perhaps." / "It hasn't sunk."**: very good.
- **l.237, the waited breath for Ebba**: one of the most moving lines in the file, and a lovely echo of the unsung first line in V.7.

### Holtward (l.241–247)
- **"a traveller could pass right under it and never see a wall"**: I read "under it" twice before I understood the town was up in the trees. "Aske of the canopy" helps once I connected it.
- **"These were trees once," I told my men. "Mind your hands."**: this is the moment the living-wood idea paid off for me completely. "It had come home." I got it, and it moved me.

### Carnhold (l.249–255)
- **"Those are the old stone grave-mounds our town was built among."**: a clunky after-the-fact gloss. Fold it into the sentence ("the cairns, the old grave-mounds of the first quarrymen, took it in").
- **"The first thing I make on it won't be a gun."**: good, and it's the short, quiet one I needed between Holtward and Emberhythe.

### Emberhythe (l.257–265)
- **"Emberhythe stood on the burning shore"** right after the header "Harl of the burning shore": the phrase appears twice in two lines.
- **"went out like a forge nobody tends"**: a forge simile right after Carnhold the forge-town. It's an accidental echo.
- **"Our mason"**: who? Unnamed. That's fine, but I wondered whether I should know him.
- **The plank that said "Burn" / "Of their wood, I always said one thing: 'Burn it.' / I didn't burn it."**: I followed it. The enemy's order and Harl's own words mirror each other, and his mercy closes it. It ties nicely to the "mirror" title. Moving.

### Sandreach (l.267–273)
- **"under the east keep"**: is that Sandreach's own east tower or *the* Keep? With "the Keep" used everywhere else, a lowercase "keep" made me look twice.
- The ear to the ground and "Tonight I sleep under my own roof of stone." are clear and good.

### Rimewatch (l.275–299)
- **"I don't tell other men's cities. This one I have to."**: good. *Why* he has to (he was the one counting) comes clear later.
- **"its great hall stood out on the ice"**: a building on a frozen lake. I wondered for a second, then pictured it on stilts over the water. That's fine.
- **"That whole morning"**: which morning? There's a small time jump, and it's easy to follow.
- **The flashback inside l.285** (the hall burning, the lamp carried across new ice) interrupts the walk, but "the way it had groaned on that night" brings me back. It works.
- **Why did Hesk walk out?** To break the ice under the Throne? To go to where his mother died? Did he know it would break? The tale won't say. I felt the grief, but I wanted *one* hint of his intent. Right now I can't tell whether it's a sacrifice, a homecoming, or an accident, and the line "He came home" works best if I can lean one way.
- **"found his lamp on the ice, still burning. I swear to that." / "He came home."**: this is the emotional peak of VI.1. It moved me a lot.
- **Seren's added line (the fisher-girl)**: lovely. The echo of "Someone has to keep a light" lands without being pointed out. One small stop: "back at the Keep," when Seren was supposedly beside each teller at his city's fire. Did she hear it later? It's minor.

### Highreach (l.301–307)
- **Highreach gets no "what it was" sentence.** Every other haven has one (forge-town, tree-town, canyon, and so on). I don't know what Highreach was beyond "crags" and a cloud. The Throne coming "down out of its cloud" is wonderful and strange, but there's no city around it.
- **"the wind came a day ahead of their sails... The wind came. It brought no sails."**: good.

### Glasspire (l.309–315)
- **"broke like a mirror, and every piece of it showed us our own lamps"**: a beautiful image, and it ties back to the title.
- Retelling Pellow's death is needed if this tale stands alone, but it's redundant right after V.7 Part II.
- **"I looked close, and I looked for him."**: moving.

### Kael's close (l.317–327)
- **The roll call**: I did the arithmetic (12 + 2 = 14, minus Corlen, Pellow and Hesk = 11), and it holds. That's good, because a counter's numbers had better be right.
- **"At Hesk's name a lamp was lit on the north wall, and no answer came down."**: very good.
- **"At the Naming, years ago, we gave the Keep's name to our whole land. Every haven is Rivenkeep."**: "the Naming" is a brand-new lore term dropped into the closing paragraph, and it's the first time the word "Rivenkeep" appears anywhere in the file. The *reveal* of the name worked for me (it felt like a title card). "The Naming" as an unexplained event didn't, because it pulled me out at the climax.
- **"Ten havens. I counted them twice, and it didn't take long."**: a perfect last line for this character.

## Tale by tale: did I follow, and did it move me?

**V.7 Part I · Three Roads, One Hour.** *Followed:* yes, almost all of it. I lost only the carving's "south's reach," which turns out to be the plot's key move. *Moved:* yes, very much, and it's the strongest thing in the file. Della finishing the mark, "You can't tell.", Kael's "One.", and the silent first line of the song all landed hard. Three things to fix: the Seren/Tarnel contradiction with the pair-death rule, Orvenna read as three people, and "Hold the stone, Seren" with no speaker.

**V.7 Part II · What We Taught Them.** *Followed:* mostly. I lost the deduction from pale sails to "not green," and I never learned *why* the Captain only gives calls in person, which is the hinge of Pellow's death. *Moved:* yes, but less than it could. Hale's "I run." and Ruan's "Look close." are excellent. Because the cause of Pellow's death is fuzzy, I couldn't settle on grief or anger. The "wood can't lie" premise needs planting earlier. ## The ten things I'd fix first (newcomer priority)

1. **Seren survived Tarnel, but "when one of a pair dies, the other dies too"** (l.17 vs l.25). Either acknowledge it or adjust the rule.
2. **"the south's reach goes east"** (l.66): the plot meaning of the first carving is lost on a newcomer.
3. **Why the Captain gives calls only in person** (l.104): Pellow's death hinges on it.
4. **Pair-names first appear as if they were a third person** ("Orvenna, Ormund and Penna," l.19; "Halyna, Halvard and Rhyna," l.116). Explain the convention at first use.
5. **"Hold the stone, Seren."** (l.11): give the reader a speaker or a frame.
6. **"The wood can't lie"** (l.126) is used before it's established. Plant it at l.63.
7. **Pale sails to "They are not green"** (l.96–100): show *how* Pellow knew, not just what he meant.
8. **Why the Thrones fall / the jump from V.7's plan to VI.1's march** (l.201): one bridging line.
9. **Over-explaining**: l.159 (restates the carving), l.124–126 (says the lie three times), l.223 ("a single holding, one stretch of fighting"), l.253 ("Those are the old stone grave-mounds...").
