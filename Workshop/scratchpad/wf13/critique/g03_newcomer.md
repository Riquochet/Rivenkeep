# g03 newcomer read: I.4 The Rite of the Cornerstone, I.5 The Naming of the Rivenmen

**Who's reading:** someone who has never heard of Rivenkeep. I read only `plain/g03.md`, from the I.4 heading to the second "We remember." I took the WRITER'S NOTES and the top comment to be backstage material, and I didn't use them to make sense of the tales. I assumed `{{Halyna}}` and the other braced names display as plain names, the `<!-- -->` markers don't display, and each `:::` block displays as a glyph with its caption, so I read the caption text as reader-facing.

**Line refs** are line numbers in g03.md.

**Short verdict:** I followed both tales, and both moved me, I.5 a bit more than I.4. Nothing lost me for long. The real stumbles are a few:
- some facts that seem to contradict each other: the husband and wife who shared a cradle, a "great" capstone I'm told the narrator holds in her hands, and "Rivenmen" against "we"
- a closing image that was never set up (Ebba's stone)
- three places where a glossing aside stops the story at the exact moment it should be building

---

## I.4 · The Rite of the Cornerstone, paragraph by paragraph

**Title.** "Rite of the *Cornerstone*", but the stone in the tale is a *capstone*, "the great stone across its top" (L13). I know a cornerstone sits at the bottom corner. Is the Captain the cornerstone? Is it just the rite's name? I never found out. It's a small itch, but it's the first word I read.

**L5 when-line.** It's clear. Two small notes: "the siege" is never explained anywhere in I.4 (whose siege, and of what?), and I only work out that the Fourteen Nights fell in winter from the snow later on. Not a blocker.

**L7 headnote.** This is the densest paragraph in the file, and I read it twice.
- "Laid by me": the first verb on the page reads like a typo or a burial. The meaning only arrives at L125, about 120 lines later. I liked the payoff when it came, but the first impression is "huh?".
- "Seren Two-Inks, who write this Book": the "who write" after "me" is grammatical, but it reads as a mistake to a modern ear. "Two-Inks" is never explained. I'm fine with that, because it made me curious.
- "I'm the third member of the household of {{Halyna}}, one name for the two of them, Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders": that's three layers of add-on in a row. *Third* of what? Am I their adopted child, an apprentice, a servant? I still don't know at the end of the tale.
- "nobody trusted me with anything but water": good, and the water bucket pays it off.
- "Here, whoever holds the old gate-stone by the fire is the one who tells": I didn't know what the gate-stone was until L30. That's fine, because it pulls me forward.

**L9.** "Hold the stone, Seren. I tell myself that every time." I liked it. I got the nerves.

**L11.**
- "a moving light woke me" is a great opening, but the very next sentence stops to explain "the cry". The suspense breaks before it starts. I'd rather have the light, then the watching, and then the gloss.
- "It raised our whole guild up out of the grass": why were they lying in grass, in winter? Were they exhausted, or asleep? It feels like a reference to a scene I missed.
- "They're a bonded pair, two of our builders joined as one from birth": this is where my biggest confusion in the file starts (see L13).
- "turn by turn": that means a spiral stair to me, but the next paragraphs describe a *rope* down a *shaft*. I couldn't picture the place.

**L13.** I read this twice.
- "Over the one cradle that she and Halvard shared as babies, Rhyna's mother used to sing...": so Halvard and Rhyna shared a cradle, were raised by Rhyna's mother, were "joined as one from birth", and are husband and wife. My first thought was *are they brother and sister?* Nothing tells me they aren't. A modern reader will stop hard here. One clause about how bonded pairs come to be would clear it up: paired by the guild at birth, raised together, not kin.
- "a rhyme of {{Aldwena}}'s line": "line" can mean a line of verse or a family line. Then "{{Aldwena}} *were*" reads as a typo for a second, until "They were the bonded pair" fixes it. By then I'd half-learned the pattern from Halyna, so it's minor.
- "Nobody had ever asked what the rhyme meant" is a great hook.

**L15–18, the rhyme.** It's clear and it's lovely. "the first two keep what the first two know" gave me a shiver.

**L20.** "Rhyna knew where to look." *Why now?* The rhyme had been sung for generations, and they've been at the Keep three nights. I assume seeing the place made the rhyme make sense, but the text doesn't say so. The rest of the paragraph (the stair that goes no more, the rope, the silence) is good.

**L22–24.** It's clean. "dust a finger deep" sticks, and pays off at L113.

**L26–28, glyph caption.** It says "the bottom of the *tale-stone*". That's a new name. The prose calls it "the old gate-stone" and "the stone in my hands". Is it a third stone? Use one name.

**L30.** "The same mark is cut under the stone in my hands, the old capstone they laid". But L13 called the capstone "the great stone across its top" of a gate, and later (L111) True Men are needed to raise the new one. How does a girl hold a gate's capstone in her hands by the fire? I read this twice and still can't picture it. Either give its size or explain it.

**L32.**
- "Inside the chest lay the Rite": what Rite? It's only explained at L42. That's fine, because the title covers it.
- "Along the floor lay a staff": the floor of the chest, or the floor of the shaft?
- "annal" is explained right away, so it's fine.

**L34.** "Nothing moved in them." This is good. I understood that the flasks were empty, so the oils would have to be made.

**L36.** It's clean. I liked "I put my water bucket down and forgot all about it."

**L40–42, Halyna's lines.** "by which a keeper is set over a great work, to build it and to keep it, until it stands" is clear. Note that this says *keeper*, and later the text says *overseer* (L78, L92) and *Commander* (L96). Are they three names for one job? I assumed so.

**L50–52, the law of the staff.** It's clear, and it set up the ending nicely. I saw the leaning staff coming as a meaning.

**L56.** "the pauses between their lines grew long" is lovely. Two small stops:
- "walls sloped to turn a shot aside": a shot of what? Arrows, or cannon? I.5 later mentions "guns", so I guess cannon. But the era is unclear in I.4.
- "the breach would fall outward": I didn't know why outward is good. It's not needed, but I paused.

"The slates don't say what they fought against" is a good, ominous hook.

**L58.** It's clean.

**L60.** "Stannard is one of the True Men, *the Captain's* own company of twelve": this is the first time the Captain is mentioned, and it's in passing, as someone else's possessive. He's the hero of the tale, and he isn't introduced until L72 ("The Captain, who leads us"). Introduce him first and properly. A smaller point: "a quarryman of this very ridge" made me wonder whether he's a local who joined the march, or a man from the coast.

**L62–64.** It's clean and lovely ("the cedar breathing out of its stones"). "They wouldn't cut down a living tree" is an unexplained custom, but a good one.

**L66.** The gloss on bloom is the best gloss in the file, because it's poetic and short. But how do you get an *oil* from a crust you scrape off? I wondered.

**L68–70.** It's clean. "My fingers were raw from it" set up L90 perfectly.

**L72.**
- The myrrh gloss is fine. The Road gloss lands as "That was our month-long march...". This is the third "That was / That's ..." add-on sentence so far (L13, L66, L72), and it starts to feel like footnotes read aloud.
- "They carried it for the guild's dead": I had to infer that myrrh is for anointing the dead. Most readers will manage.
- "The Captain, who leads us, would be anointed": *why him?* The builders found the rite and the builders made the oils, so why is a soldier made keeper? Who chose him? I.5 makes the soldier-and-mason union land, but at this point in I.4 it reads as unexplained.

**L74.** "a boy of the guild who had walked the Road beside me, knotting a net as he went. He never reached the Keep." It's very good. A net-knotting boy in a builders' guild is a small mystery, and I liked it.

**L76.** It's clean, and very good. The Captain with mortar to his wrists looking at the flask moved me.

**L78.** I read this twice. "On the fourteenth day a thin sun came out, the first since the day of the banner. *That was* the evening we reached the Keep..." Grammatically, "That" grabs "the fourteenth day", so I first read it as *the fourteenth day was the evening we reached the Keep*. It also drops a full backstory into the build-up of the climax. "the overseer must stand" uses *overseer* before L92 explains it.

**L80.** "He didn't like it. He stood there." This is excellent.

**L82.** It's clean. "snow-water running in the old gutters" is lovely.

**L84.** "That winter their hands shook at everything else. On the flasks they didn't shake." This is the best line in I.4 for me.

**L86–88.** It's clean.

**L90.** It's clean, and the raw hands raised and smiling are a real payoff.

**L92.** This is the biggest over-explanation in the file, and it falls at the worst moment. The staff is being held out, and the text stops to quote the slate, then defines *ashlar*, then defines *overseer*. I read it twice for another reason as well. The staff was "borne by every overseer of a great Stonewright work since the founding". But L50 says a staff "never leaves its work", so how did every overseer carry this one? Then "This staff was the first of them all" untangles it: it's one *kind* of staff, and this is the first of the kind. I got there in the end, but it was hard work right where I wanted to feel something. I'd keep "The Captain looked at it for a long while before he put out his hands" and cut most of the rest, or move it.

**L94.** "Their four hands laid it in his two." It's perfect.

**L96.** It's clean. The name "Rivenkeep" appears here for the first time, "as this Keep is called". Who called it that, and what does "riven" mean? See I.5.

**L98.**
- "Their mark isn't mine to draw": that's an intriguing custom. But the book has just drawn {{Aldwena}}'s mark for me (the L26 glyph). Why is one mark shown and the other not? I wondered.
- "the Title, the five things the Captain had written in coal" is clear.

**L100–103, the Title glyph.** See the Romanization section below. The caption's "in the *course-hand*" is unexplained. Is that the same as "the old hand" (L36)? Did the Captain, a soldier, write in the builders' script? L107 says it's his handwriting, so I couldn't tell which language he wrote in.

**L105–109.** It's clean. "Neither of them had tidied a single stroke" is lovely.

**L111.** It's clean. "the unwritten half of his cloak" is a good image.

**L113.** "If you doubt the tale, go and look at the dust on it." This is great. It mirrors the chest's dust.

**L115.** I read it twice.
- Seren goes down "alone", "turn by turn". Halvard needed a rope, with Rhyna paying it out, so how does a twelve-year-old go down alone? The geography again.
- "I lifted the empty flasks": which flasks? The three found flasks were up in the ward, in {{Idrenna}}'s laps. Did she carry them back down? Were they there all along?

**L117–119.** It's clean, and very moving. "the way a child's are, and the way mine still are" is the line that made Seren real to me.

**L123–125, the apprentice's leaf.** It's a strong payoff for "Laid by me". The course image was glossed back at L56, so it works.

**L129.** It's clean. "We forgot it, and found it again at the bottom of a chest" is good.

**L131.** "He stood to tell it, because he wouldn't sit." It reads as circular. Why wouldn't he sit: pride, restlessness, respect? I read it twice and still don't know. "Kael the Counter" isn't explained until I.5, and that's fine.

**L133–137.** It's clean, and a good close.

## I.5 · The Naming of the Rivenmen, paragraph by paragraph

**L141 when-line.** It's clear. Jumping from the first spring to the sixth winter is a good jolt.

**L143 headnote.** It's dense: Eldhythe, harbour-house, True Men, harbour battery, guns, and "Voss who asked", five glosses in five sentences.
- The voice changes from I.4's "Laid by me" to "He grew up...". I worked out it's the scribe speaking, but I paused.
- "commands the harbour battery, a group of our guns": the word "guns" jolts in a world of slates, myrrh and chisels. I also couldn't tell where this battery stands. Everyone is up on the mountain, and the havens are held by the enemy.
- Ending on the gate-stone line again works as a refrain.

**L145.** "I've owed this hearth an answer for a long time." It's a great hook.

**L147–149.** It's clean, and very good. The skipping rope on the quay, then "His name was Jory."

**L151–153.** It's clean.

**L155.** If I've just read I.4, Stannard is introduced all over again, and it's a bit much. The knuckle on the hearthstone is a good callback.

**L157–159.** "It's not the ice I fight for." I half-got it: the stone of his own ridge is his home. It's good that it's left as a hint.

**L161.** It's clean and lovely.

**L163.** This is long, and it's the third time I've been told about the torn cloak (I.4 L78, L98, and here), with the whole Title quoted again. "under the torn cloak. That's our banner." is choppy.
- "The Captain *stood down* among us": "stand down" is a military idiom for being relieved of command. For a beat I thought he'd resigned. Try "stood among us", or "came down and stood among us".
- "For once in his life he wasn't whistling" sets up L179 well.

**L165–171.** It's clean, and strong. "down to the smallest child who carried the guild's water" made me smile, because it's Seren. "our master builders who never take up a weapon" was new to me, and it's important. It also explains, after the fact, why the soldier got the staff in I.4.

**L173.** It's clean, and the best paragraph in the middle of I.5. "the way two courses of stone become one wall once the mortar sets".

**L175.** This reads like a glossary: a claim, then a gloss, then another gloss. "Every haven the grey sails hold is Rivenkeep. The havens are... The grey sails are..." I followed it, but I could feel the explaining.

**L177.** I read it twice.
- "We named ourselves the Rivenmen." Then three sentences later: "We call ourselves just *we*." So which is it? It muddies the tale's own title. I think it means that among ourselves no name is needed, but I had to work for that.
- "Riven" is never glossed. Readers who know it means *torn* will connect it to the torn cloak, and that's a lovely link. Most modern readers won't know the word, so they'll miss the best hidden meaning in the tale.

**L179.** It's clean, and very good. "came back down whistling" landed. "held fast" with the rope image works.

**L181–183.** It's clean.

**L185.**
- "ten great enemy ships came out of the fog": L175 already said the grey sails "came out of the fog" and burned the havens. Are these new ships, or the same? I took them to be new, this winter's.
- "The wall calls them the Mystarchs": "the wall" as shorthand for the men on the wall gave me a small pause. The name is eerie and I liked it being unexplained.
- Ebba arrives here, very late, as "old Ebba of Fenholm, the marsh town". She works anyway, but she's introduced and buried within about fifteen lines.

**L187–193.** "Perhaps." / "I didn't understand it then. I think I do now." I got it once she died. It's good.

**L195–197.** It's clean, and quietly devastating: "nobody built it up again."

**L199.**
- "The Captain tied a strip to a pike above her": I took it as an honour, and that's enough.
- It's still "the Captain" here even though he's Commander. That's consistent with the end of I.4, and it works.

**L201.** It's very moving. "the width of one old voice" does all the work. "It's hers." spells it out, and to me it's the one over-explanation in the closing run. I'd trust the reader here.

**L203.** "and he never heard it asked" puzzled me for a beat, and the next sentence solved it. Fine.

**L205–209.** It's clean, and the emotional peak of the answer for me: "I have no child of my own, and still I said *our*."

**L211.** It's clean. "It's a place to go out from" is good. (The crimson cloak is mentioned for the first time here, which is fine.)

**L213.** I read it twice. "Rivenkeep is home, and on the banner we swore to bring it home". *Bring home home*? And the banner says "In memory of our home", not a promise to bring it home. The oath was sworn at the stone under the pike, not on the banner. The answer the tale has been building since L145 comes out a little foggy. L209's "our" is the sharper answer, and this line wanders away from it.

**L215.** "Marl has the stone she carried out of Fenholm in his pack." *What stone?* This is the first I've heard of it. It's the closing image of the tale, and it was never set up. One earlier touch would make it land hard: Ebba turning a stone in her lap at the fire (L185), or a word at L195.

**L217–223.** It's clean. "The hearth waited a breath for Ebba to begin" is the best last line in the file.

## The romanized Title (L102)

I loved seeing it. It made the world feel real. I tried to line the words up with the English and nearly managed:

| Ul | dumol | ol | varn | ol | theldeth | ol | Mardh | ol | lunnath | ol | sollan |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| in? | memory (of)? | our | home | our | families | our | God | our | freedoms | our | peace |

But I'm guessing:
- Is "Ul dumol" "in memory", or is one of those words "of"? Is "ol" "our", or "of our"?
- Capitalized "Mardh" for God was a nice tell.

A word-by-word gloss under the line would turn a puzzle into a delight. It could be a small interlinear row, or each English word in small type under its native word. The caption's "course-hand" also needs three words of gloss. Otherwise I can't tell whether this is the builders' script or the Captain's own language.

## Tale by tale

**I.4 · The Rite of the Cornerstone. Did I follow it? Yes.** The spine is clear: a rhyme leads to a buried chest, the chest holds a rite, the rite needs three oils, the oils get made, the Captain is anointed, and a custom is found. Where I got lost was the setting and the people, not the plot:
- I couldn't picture the stair, shaft and rope.
- The capstone's size makes no sense.
- The bonded pair's relationship is unclear (siblings or spouses?).
- Seren's place in the household is never explained.

**Did it move me? Yes, quite a lot**, in the small, physical moments:
- the boy knotting a net who never reached the Keep
- the Captain looking at the empty flask and going out again
- hands that shook at everything else except the flasks
- children raising raw hands and smiling
- "Their four hands laid it in his two"
- the dust on the staff
- the apprentice's child-handwriting

The momentum sags twice, both times because of glosses at peak moments: the day-of-the-banner backstory at L78, and the ashlar/overseer lecture at L92. The "That was..." add-on sentences (L13, L66, L72, L78) are the most noticeable explaining habit.

**I.5 · The Naming of the Rivenmen. Did I follow it? Yes, more easily than I.4.** The question-and-answer frame (Jory, "ice and stone", the answer at last) holds it together. I stumbled on "Rivenmen" against "we", on "stood down", on the foggy final answer (L213), and on Ebba's stone appearing from nowhere.

**Did it move me? Yes, more than I.4.**
- Jory not whistling, then whistling, then under his cairn before the question.
- Ebba's "Perhaps."
- The fire nobody built up.
- The gap the width of one old voice.
- The hearth waiting a breath.

All of these landed. The middle (L175–177) is the flattest stretch, because it explains names instead of showing the courtyard.

## Top fixes, ranked by how hard they stopped me

1. **Bonded pair, cradle, husband and wife (L11, L13).** Say how a pair comes to be, so no one reads Halvard and Rhyna as siblings.
2. **Capstone size (L13 against L30 and L111).** "the great stone across its top" can't also be "the stone in my hands".
3. **Ebba's stone (L215).** Set it up earlier, or the last image falls flat.
4. **"We named ourselves the Rivenmen … We call ourselves just *we*" (L177).** Pick one, or bridge them. Also gloss *riven* as *torn*, to link it to the cloak.
5. **L92 lecture mid-rite.** Cut or move the ashlar and overseer glosses, and settle "keeper / overseer / Commander".
6. **L78 "That was the evening…"** The pronoun grabs the wrong day, and the backstory lands in the climax.
7. **The Captain's first appearance (L60).** Introduce him before he becomes a possessive.
8. **The Title gloss (L102).** Give word-by-word English and explain "course-hand".
9. **Geography of the descent (L11, L20, L115).** Spiral stair or rope shaft, and how Seren goes down alone. Also, where are the "empty flasks"?
10. **Smaller points:**
    - "tale-stone" in the L26 caption
    - "Laid by me … who write" (L7)
    - "stood down" (L163)
    - "He stood to tell it, because he wouldn't sit" (L131)
    - "bring it home" on "the banner" (L213)
    - "It's hers." (L201)
    - the "Cornerstone" title against the capstone
