# Whole-book newcomer read: plain_v31_draft.md

I read the whole draft, all 2,956 lines, from start to finish as a modern reader who knows nothing about Rivenkeep. I did not open the Book or any notes. Line numbers below are from `plain_v31_draft.md`.

---

## The short version

- **The book works.** By the end of Book One I cared about Seren, Kael and Ebba. From Book Four on it moved me again and again. Setting each wood tale against our own tale of the same events is the strongest idea in the book. Brenn's confession (IV.3) next to the gift's own memory (IV.4) hit hard.
- **The biggest problem is that it explains the same basics over and over**, mostly in the italic headers. About 4,750 of the 46,765 words (10%) are header. Most of that header text repeats what the note for new readers already told me. Some counts across the book:
  - "laid, like a stone in a wall": **17** times
  - "the husband and wife who lead our builders": **19**
  - "ten cities": **27**
  - "who write(s) this Book": **18**
  - "the Captain's own company of twelve": **10**
  - "great fog / fog bank": **20**
  - "pebble beach": **11**
  - "the old fortress": **11**
  - the torn cloak retold: **about 10**
  - The headers of V.3, V.4, V.6 and VI.2 all open with **the same 104-word paragraph, word for word.**
- **The second problem is invented names that look alike.** There are six "Myst-" words. The wood's words come in near-twins: Naelear, Naelsaen and Naelthar; Ralenthae and Ralensaen; Rhenear and Rhenvael; Aelthae and Ael'thar. They read like misprints of each other until you notice they are built from shared roots. **If the book shows the reader those roots, the confusion becomes one of its pleasures** (section 7). This is also what the user asked for: romanized words shown with the English for each piece.
- **There are a few real contradictions** (section 2). Wick is "the fastest" in one tale and Hale is "the fastest" in two others. The Guest's gift is a "dark stone" in the foreword but "dark, old wood" in II.2. II.2 says the wood people's name "hasn't come to us yet", then prints it in a note box. Voss is a True Man in I.5, more than a thousand lines before the book says why.
- **There is one broken template token.** VI.3 prints `{LAST_THRONE_AT_HAVEN}` raw in the body text (line 2638).
- **The hardest tales to follow** are II.2, VI.2, V.6, IV.2 and the Epilogue's grain box (section 6).

## 3. Repetition

### 3.1 The italic headers (the single biggest fix)

Almost every header re-explains four things: what "laid" means, who holds the stone, who {{Halyna}} are, and who writes the Book. The parentheses that start to appear in II.3 and later, "(We say a tale is laid...)", show the draft already knows this. By III.1 I was skipping headers, and then I missed the useful facts they carried: who told the tale, when, and from what wood.

**Proposed rule.**
- Explain "laid" and "holding the stone" once, in the foreword and the Invocation. I.1's header may repeat it, but no later header should.
- Gloss {{Halyna}}, the True Men, the havens and the grey once per Book, at first use, in the body text, not in the header.
- Keep each header to when it was told, who told it, what wood it came from, and the one human detail that belongs to that tale only. That is about 40–70 words.

**Sample rewrites**

I.3 (line 413, now 102 words):

> *Hale the runner laid this tale. He was seventeen, in his first weeks on the wall. Our runners carry to the Captain whatever they see. Hale told it standing up, as fast as he runs, and I've set it down slower.*

II.2 (line 793, now 212 words). This is the first wood tale, so it may explain the form once, fully:

> *This is the first of the wood's tales in this Book. When {{Halyna}} lay their hands on carved wood from behind the fog, what the grain holds comes into them whole, the way a memory does. They give it to us in broken pieces, one taking up where the other stops. I set the pieces down first, as they came, with a gap where their words run out. Then I tell it whole, in the wood's own voice. No one at our hearth has ever answered the wood.*
>
> *This one came from the oldest thing in our chest, the dark stone you'll hear of in the Book of the Wound. A child had asked whether the people behind the fog had houses. For many nights {{Halyna}} held it, one awake at the hearth and the other asleep, and each time the sleeper woke knowing it too. Those people had put the whole of their home into it, and our language has no word for a home made of air. Their name for themselves hadn't come to us yet, so I've set down only its sign.*

## 7. Romanization: what a newcomer wants

The user asked for each romanized word to show its English. As a newcomer, that is exactly where I got lost, and exactly what would fix it. The book's own glosses already show that the wood's words are built from a few shared roots. I worked these out only by lining the names up afterwards:

| Root | Meaning (from the book's own glosses) | Words built on it |
|---|---|---|
| nael | breath / mist | **Nael**ear "those of the breath", **Nael**saen "mist-hearts", **Nael**thar "blood of the sky" |
| rhen | stone | **Rhen**ear "the stone-deaf" (stone-folk), **Rhen**vael "stone-grain", Ael**rhen** "bond-to-stone" |
| ael | bond / binding | **Ael**rhen, **Ael**varen "Bearer of the Binding", **Ael**'thar (the guest-rite), **Ael**thae (the Joined Tide) |
| thae | tide | **Thae**saen "tide-heart", Ralen**thae** "the tide the roots taught", Ael**thae** |
| saen | heart | Nael**saen**, Thae**saen**, Ralen**saen** "root-heart" |
| ral(en) | root | **Ralen**thae, **Ralen**saen |
| vael | grain | Rhen**vael**, **Vael**ress "the grain that rots" |
| varen | bearer | Ael**varen**, Lea**varen** "bearer of new branches" |
| thar | blood | Nael**thar** "blood of the sky", so Ael'**thar** reads as "bond-blood": the cut-arm rite the Guest began |
| -ear | folk, those of | Nael**ear**, Rhen**ear** |

Once I saw this, the near-twins stopped looking like misprints and started to mean something. *Aelthae* is literally "bond-tide", which is what the wall called the Joined Tide. That gives Seren's "I have written the name, and I won't write what I thought" (V.7:2293) real weight: the enemy named its tide after the bond the warden broke. As written, a newcomer can't reach that.

**Where to show it.** Seren says "I love our language" (line 158), so she is the right person to notice the roots, and V.7 Part I is where she already notices *Ael*. Rewrite V.7:2293:

> The wall calls each year's enemy fleets a Tide, and the carving gave this one its own name: *Aelthae*. I knew both halves of it. *Thae* I'd had from the wood before, in *Ralenthae*, the tide the roots taught. *Ael* I had written once, in *Ael'thar*, the rite the Guest began as he knelt at our outpost's door, in our grandparents' time. *Ael* is a binding. We had called this year's fleets the Joined Tide without knowing that they called themselves the same. I have written the name, and I won't write what I thought.

Then let the word-by-word gloss in each name box show the pieces, for example *Thae·saen*, "tide · heart". By VI.2 the reader can decode the ten Throne names alone, and that hard tale becomes a game the reader plays along with.

Smaller requests:
- **The Title box (lines 170–175).** I guessed from the repetition that *ol* means "our". Confirming it word by word (*Ul* = in memory of? *dumol* = ?) would reward exactly the kind of curiosity the box invites.
- **Ael'thar at IV.4:1474.** Gloss it at first use ("the blood-binding") so that V.7 can pay it off.
- **The Stonwryt vow box (lines 109–111).** It names "the Hal" but shows only English. If the Hal has a romanization, show a word or two here, because this is the first romanization box in the book.

**The Myst- words** are shore-speech, not the wood's language, and six of them share one prefix: Mystaeri (the people), Mystarchs (the Thrones), Mystholders (the pillars), Mystlands, Mystwood and Myststone (first seen in the Epilogue at 2843). Keep **Mystaeri**, which VI.3 and the Last Note need, and perhaps Mystlands. Change Mystarchs to Thrones. Mystholders can just be "the black pillars", which IV.1 calls them anyway. Mystwood can be "the wood out there". Myststone can be "fog-stone", or simply "stone that was once wood".
