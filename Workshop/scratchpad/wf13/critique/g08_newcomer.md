# g08 · Newcomer read: V.2 The Night of the Naming, and V.5 The Tides, as the Wall Counted Them

I read only `plain/g08.md`. The file ends with a WRITER'S NOTES section. I read through it, but this critique judges only the tale text, and I have tried to set aside anything the notes explained. Where I say "I didn't get it", I mean I didn't get it from the story.

Line numbers refer to `plain/g08.md`.

Key: **STOP** means I had to stop or read the passage twice. **WHO/WHY** means I didn't know who someone was or why something mattered. **HINT** means I could tell it was meant to mean something, but not what. **DRAG** means it was over-explained or slow. **GOOD** means it worked.

## V.2 · The Night of the Naming

**L5, the italic line under the title.** Clear overall. *"The wall called that year's enemy fleets the Second Tide"* made me ask briefly whether "the wall" was a person or the soldiers on it. I worked it out, and the phrase comes up so often later that it settles in.

**L7, the headnote.** **GOOD.** The note in brackets about laying a tale works, and because of it *"Hold the stone, Seren."* makes sense. Small **STOP**: I wasn't sure who says *"Hold the stone, Seren."* It reads like the hearth handing her the stone, and I got there, but for a moment it looked like Seren talking to herself.

**L11, "All through that first winter…"** **GOOD**, apart from one thing. *"the Captain's twelve True Men"*: who the Captain is goes unexplained until V.5 (*"the Captain, who leads us"*). I could guess he's the leader. "True Men" reads as an honour guard, which was fine for now.

**L13, "One night late in the Second Tide, Halyna…"**
- **STOP:** I could see the `{{Halyna}}` braces as raw text. I assume they get styled later. *"Halyna are"* with a plural verb made me stop, until the next sentence showed it's one name for two people. You could say outright that it's a name made from both of theirs (*"we call them by one name, Halyna"*). Once I knew, I liked it.
- **STOP, and the biggest snag in this paragraph:** *"the vault where we found the first builders' black chest, with the Rite inside it that made the Captain our Commander."* One clause brings in four new things (the first builders, a black chest, the Rite, the post of Commander), and none of them matters to this tale. It also muddled me: if he was made Commander, why does everyone call him the Captain? I spent the next paragraph wondering whether these were two different men. I'd cut this down to the oldest vault under the hall, or at most *"where the first builders of the Keep left their chest"*, and drop *"Commander"* completely.
- **GOOD:** *"like a field the plough has turned up full of stones."*

**L15, "Fifty-three planks…"**
- **STOP:** *"The rest went into the soldiers' fires."* The rest of what? Straight after *"every one of them was carved"*, I first read it as the rest of the planks, which made no sense. *"The rest of the wreckage"* would fix it.
- **STOP:** *"The wall called the carved planks the tablets. They were only planks."* I couldn't tell who thinks they were "only planks". Is Seren dismissing the soldiers' grand name? Is it how things looked to them then? Is it irony? It reads like a pointed line, but I couldn't tell what it was pointing at.
- **GOOD:** *"It took two runners to a plank, and the salt water ran down their arms."*

**L17, the "knowing" paragraph.** **GOOD.** This is the clearest bit of world-explaining in the file: *"the way you know something you remember, without reading it… and I can't do it."* I never wondered why only Halyna can do it, and I was happy to leave that as a mystery. Small **WHO/WHY**: why does the enemy carve its ships at all? I took that as a hook, not a gap.

**L19, "The planks of the Second Tide…"**
- **STOP, motive:** *"Until that night, nobody had gone down to ask the fifty-three old planks for anything more."* Why that night? I think the logic is: the new planks have two signs and gaps, so maybe the old one-word planks were hiding more as well. But I had to work that out myself, and I'm still not sure I got it right. One line of motive would help, something like *"If the new wood held two words, we wondered what the old wood had kept back."*
- **DRAG, slight:** *"I haven't filled those gaps with guesses."* This sounds like an archivist's footnote, and it slowed me just before the action starts.

**L21, "Summer nights are short…"**
- **STOP, and the second-biggest snag in V.2:** *"A bonded pair, two of our builders joined as one from birth."* L13 told me they are *"the husband and wife who lead our builders."* So are they married, or joined from birth? Is it magic? Were they betrothed in the cradle? Are all builders born in pairs? Joined from birth and husband and wife clash, and I didn't know which to picture. (V.5 later says *"joined as one since the cradle"* and *"a bonded pair dies together"*, so it's clearly a real bond, which makes this first sighting matter.) Something like *"born bonded, and later wed"* would settle it.
- **GOOD:** *"Even that night they weren't far apart: only the length of the vault."*

**L23, "Rhyna picked up the very first plank…"**
- **GOOD:** the raw edges get planted here, and I noticed them.
- **STOP, logic:** L38 says the planks were laid *"in the order the sea gave them up."* L17 says the silent plank came *"a few weeks after the very first."* So it should lie near Rhyna's end, by the stair, not at Halvard's *"far wall."* A careful reader trips on this. Either Halvard walked over to fetch it, or it had been set apart. One phrase would cover it.

**L25–29.** **GOOD.** The still flame and the net simile are lovely, and *"Their two faces changed at the same moment"* lands.

**L31–36, the first order.** **GOOD.** *"it wasn't a word. It was a whole order"* is a strong turn. Halvard saying it word for word from the silent plank chilled me.

**L38, "After that, every plank spoke."**
- **STOP, and the biggest logic gap in V.2:** why did every plank suddenly give a whole order, when for a whole winter each had given only one word? Was it because they held two planks at once, at opposite ends? Did the first plank unlock the others? Do they now have to hold two at a time? I know it was a magical breakthrough, but I don't know what made it happen. Then the net simile (L27) suggests the planks are all connected. If that's the reason, say it once here: *"It was all one net, and once the first knot rose, every knot rose."*

**L41–51, the six carvings with commentary.**
- **GOOD on the whole.** Each order recasts something the wall had misread (*"we'd called them mad"*, *"a coward"*, *"hunger"*, *"the wall cheered"*), and that's where the horror builds. *"Wait in the grey, as a seed waits out the frost, and when the stone is still, come"* is the best of them.
- **STOP:** *"Thin wood falls, and the bearer keeps up."* Keeps up with the fleet? Stays afloat? I read it twice. *"Keeps afloat"* or *"sails on"* would be clearer, unless the wording has to stay word for word.
- **HINT:** *"their sign for stone, which they call the mute thing."* How do we know what they call it? I supposed through a knowing. Why "mute"? Because stone doesn't speak and wood does? I enjoyed the hint without fully getting it, and that's fine.
- **DRAG, mild:** by the fifth and sixth orders the pattern of carving, then gloss, then *"we'd called it…"* starts to feel like a list. It works, but just barely.

**L53, "The soldiers named each order by its first words…"** **DRAG / WHY.** I didn't see why this matters here. It breaks the run from the orders into the Emberhythe plank, which is the emotional peak. Unless the plainer names pay off later, I'd cut it or move it.

**L55, the Emberhythe plank.**
- **STOP, and the biggest context gap in the whole file:** this is the first I hear that the grey ships *"burned our ten cities, one after another"* over twenty months. That's the stakes of the whole war, and it comes two-thirds of the way into the second tale. It also changes how I read L11, *"this hearth laughed at the grey sails."* On a first read the laughter felt light. Knowing they had lost ten cities, it becomes defiance or grief, which is better. Plant *the Fall* in the headnote or in L11 (*"the grey sails, the ships that burned our ten cities"*). Then *"Burn"* hits even harder, because the reader already carries the wound.
- **STOP, grammar:** *"after the Fall. That was the twenty months in which…"* — "That was the twenty months" reads awkwardly. Try *"the Fall, the twenty months in which…"*.
- Several new names arrive at once: Emberhythe, forge-town, warm volcanic shore, the Fall. *"Our forge-town"* is enough here, and *"warm volcanic shore"* is extra.
- **GOOD:** *"He couldn't bear to come away from his city with nothing."*

**L57–61, "It said Burn."** **GOOD.** It's the best moment in V.2. *"Nobody spoke, and for a long while I didn't write"* is exactly right.

**L63–67, "They hadn't picked anything. / They had been sent."** **GOOD**, and chilling. Repeating the wrong-fortress joke just before it makes the turn land. This is also where *"the young wood"* finally clicked for me: the ships *are* the wood, a living enemy. I liked working that out from *root*, *branch*, *bark* and *seed*.

**L69–73, the raw edges meet.** **HINT.** I understood what happened: the first plank and the silent plank are two halves of one split piece. I could feel it mattered. Two ships cut from one log are a pair, like Halyna? So the silent half only speaks alongside its other half? But I don't know what it means, and Seren says she doesn't either. As a mystery that's fine, and *"The broken grain met"* is a great sentence. Be aware that for a newcomer this is a felt hint, not an understood one. I'm curious, not confused.

**L75–77, Seren's closing thought.** **GOOD.** *"The worst of the wood isn't behind us. It's still growing."* It's a fine last beat.

**L79, "I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me."** **STOP, mild.** Seren is the eyewitness and the first teller, so who laid it *for* her? I took it as a ritual formula that every teller says, but it reads oddly coming from the person who saw it. V.5 has the same problem, since Kael is the first teller there too. If later tellers say it, maybe the headnotes should hint that we're hearing it retold.

### V.2 verdict
- **Did I follow it?** Mostly, yes. The shape is clear: old planks, a night's work, a breakthrough, every misread enemy act recast as an order, *Burn*, and *they had been sent*. What I couldn't follow: why the breakthrough happened that night (L38), what a bonded pair is (L21 against L13), and Captain against Commander (L13).
- **Did it move me?** Yes. *"It said Burn"*, *"They had been sent"* and *"It's still growing"* are three real chills. It would hit harder if I knew about the ten burned cities from the start, and if the vault paragraph (L13) weren't cluttered with lore that has no use here.

## V.5 · The Tides, as the Wall Counted Them

**L85, the italic line.** Clear.

**L87, the headnote.**
- **WHO:** *"he asked me to mend all six"*. Who is "me"? I assumed Seren because I'd just read V.2, but nothing here names her. A reader who opens V.5 alone won't know. Write *"asked me, Seren, …"*.
- **STOP:** *"as I'd mended his first tale, and I've mended them less."* What first tale? And does "mended them less" mean she edited these lightly? I read it twice. *"and I've changed these less than that one"* would be plainer.
- **DRAG, on a read straight through:** the note in brackets about laying, *"one of the Captain's twelve True Men"* and *"each year's enemy fleets a Tide"* all repeat V.2. I see why, since each tale stands alone. Each one is short, so it's tolerable. I flag the repeats together below.
- **GOOD:** *"he counts everything twice"*, and it pays off beautifully in Count VI.

**L89, "Give me the stone. No, I'll stand."** **GOOD.** It gives his character in seven words.

### Count I · The Hasty Tide
**L93, "I count. That's what I'm for."** **GOOD.** It's a great voice.

**L95.** **GOOD.** It echoes V.2's *Break the wall* order, and that was a quiet reward for having read V.2.

**L97, root-men.** *"like spilled kindling"* is vivid. Then **DRAG:** *"They're the enemy's warriors, and they look like tangled roots"* tells me plainly what the image had already shown. *"as if a name could make them small"* is **GOOD**.

**L99, "The fleet has spent its fire."** **WHY.** I didn't see why the saying matters. Did they run out of shot? It reads flat next to the lines around it.

**L101, the breach.**

- **STOP:** *"the slates on building for war that the first builders of this Keep left under the hall"* is a clumsy phrase, and I read it twice. It also brings back the first builders and the black chest, which still mean nothing to me. Either trim it, or let the reader take *"the old builders' slates"* on trust.

**L103.** **STOP, mild:** *"God forgive us, we laughed."* It's the only mention of a god in either tale, and it felt borrowed from our world. Maybe that's intended. If not, *"Forgive us"* alone keeps the feeling.

**L105, Jory.** **GOOD.** *"where the ground is too hard for a spade"* is a fine gloss for the cairns because it does more than explain. "Eldhythe" goes unexplained, which is fine for a hometown.

**L107, the cloak.** **STOP:** *"cut a strip… from his half of the cloak"* comes before I know there are halves, and the explanation follows a sentence later. Swap them: tear the cloak first, then cut the strip. Small **WHY**, which grew by Count VI: why do *these* dead get strips and not the rest? By the end I noticed it's one name per Tide, but nothing says so, and *"For each of them"* in Count VI made me ask what sets these six apart.

**L109–111, "It was the first strip. / It wasn't the last."** **GOOD.** It's devastating, and Seren's hand confirms who the mender is.

### Count II · The Second Tide

**L123, crown-hulls and winged seed.** Clear enough. **WHY, mild:** I don't know what the seed *does* when it lands. Does it burn, sprout, stab? Corlen dies of it at L135, so one concrete image would make the threat real.

**L125, the bearer passing on.**

- **DRAG, on a read straight through:** the bearer was already glossed in V.2 L45.

**L127, Corlen.** **GOOD.** *"Wait. The sea hasn't finished."* *"the Twelve"* is clear from *"twelve True Men"*.

**L129–133.** **STOP, spatial:** *"He had eyes for the water, and none for anything behind him. / The seed came over the wall."* Did the seed come from behind him, or over the wall from the sea? I pictured it both ways. If the point is that he watched the bait ship and not the sky, say *"and none for the sky"*. That would also set up the Captain looking *up* at L139.
- **GOOD:** *"His were the quiet ones."*

**L137.** **GOOD.** *"Corlen did what the Captain himself would have done. That's the whole of a True Man. It's also what killed him."* It's the first real definition of a True Man, and it's a good one. I'd have liked it earlier, but it lands well here.

**L139–141, Voss and "Change your shot."** **GOOD.** *"looked up, not out to sea"* is clear storytelling. **HINT, mild:** change it to what? I guessed: aim at the seed or the crown-hulls. It's fine as it is.

### V.5 verdict

## Top fixes, in order of how much they cost me
1. **Count IV's outcome (L175–177).** I lost track of what happened. Add one sentence of result.
2. **Plant the Fall early (V.2 L7 or L11).** The ten burned cities are the stakes of everything, and they arrive at L55.
3. **Why every plank spoke that night (L38).** The breakthrough has no cause I could see.
4. **"Husband and wife" against "joined as one from birth" (L13 against L21).** Say what a bonded pair is in a way that fits both.
5. **The lore clause in the vault (L13).** Cut *the Rite* and *Commander*. Captain and Commander confused me.
6. **Hesk's lamp (L147, L221).** It's set up twice and I never understood it. Give it one line, or drop it from Wick's scene.
7. **Small fixes:** *"he's under them too"* → *"under the cairns"* (L191). Introduce Pellow before *"as Pellow said"* (L185). *"came the way the young wood came"* → *"came looking like"* (L183). *"look before they trust"* (L163). *"anything behind him"* → *"the sky"* (L129). *"The rest"* → *"the rest of the wreckage"* (L15). *"the bearer keeps up"* (L45). ## What worked and should be kept
- The slow discovery that the enemy is living wood, from root, branch, bark, seed, *"the young wood"* and *"still growing"*. I pieced it together myself and it felt earned.
- The pale-sail thread: in Count II a sail goes white like a face, in Count V a pale sail gives away a disguised ship, and in Count VI *"the wood can be afraid."* It's the best-built idea across the two tales.
- *"They had been sent."* / *"The broken grain met."* / *"Hale ran it."* / *"It wasn't the last."* / *"Tonight it can't reach his belt."*
- The glosses that also add mood, like *"where the ground is too hard for a spade"* and *"the way you know something you remember, without reading it."* Those never felt like explaining.
