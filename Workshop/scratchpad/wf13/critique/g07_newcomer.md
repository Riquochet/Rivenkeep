**Reader:** someone who has never heard of Rivenkeep. I read only `plain/g07.md`, lines 1–169 (IV.5, the Book Five heading and Argument, and V.1). The file ends with a WRITER'S NOTES section. No reader would see it, so I didn't judge it, and I set it aside rather than let it explain anything to me. Line numbers (L) refer to the file.

I assume `{{Halyna}}`, `{{Wendhessa}}`, the `<!-- -->` comments and the `::: native` block render as plain names, nothing at all, and a picture. If the braces ever show on the page, they will stop every reader.

## IV.5 · The Burning Boat

**L5, the when-line**
- TWICE: *"in the year of the Second Tide, as the wall named that year's enemy fleets."* I read "the wall named" as a wall naming something before I worked out that "the wall" means the people on it. A suggestion: *"the Second Tide, as we on the wall called that year's enemy fleets."*
- STOP: *"the siege"*. A siege of what? I don't hear of a fortress until "here at the Keep" (L11), and I don't learn what the Keep is until V.1.

**L7, the headnote**
- The bracketed aside on the gate-stone and *laid* is clear, and *"a laid tale stays"* is lovely. But the paragraph runs seven sentences before the tale starts, and the brackets split it in the middle.
- STOP: *"the old gate-stone"*. A gate of what? I took it as a kind of talking-stick and moved on. That's fine.
- STOP: *"our cities on the coast"*. Who is "we"? The paragraph suggests "we" are now somewhere other than the coast. I didn't know the cities were lost until "won back" (L73) and "burnt Eldhythe" (L87).
- WHY DOES THIS MATTER: *"why do we fight for ice and stone?"* is a great hook. But the tale never comes back to it, so I kept waiting for an answer. If it only explains Voss's name, fine, but it sets up an expectation the tale doesn't meet.
- Who speaks the italics? Not Voss. In V.1 I find out it's Seren. Here it's an unnamed "we". This is minor.
- WORKED: *"Everyone on shore laughed at it. The sea didn't."*
- Elspet is named once and never again. It does no harm, but it's one more name to carry.

**L9 ("Give me the stone…")**
- WORKED. *"he had it from the sea"* is a great opening line.

**L11**
- STOP (where things are): *"Down on the shingle, the pebble beach below the wall … I heard one of their ships burn."* So the Keep stands over the sea. But L45 says we marched *"over the mountain passes to this Keep"*, and V.1 calls it *"the old mountain fortress"* on *"our bare mountain ridge"*. I couldn't picture a mountain fortress with ships beaching under it until I decided it must sit on a ridge running down to the sea. One clause the first time the Keep is named would settle it, for example *"here at the Keep, the old fortress where the mountain ridge runs down into the sea"*.
- Minor: *"the first holding of this war, the first time the grey sails, the enemy's fleet, came against our wall here at the Keep and we had to hold it"* stacks two glosses in one sentence. It reads, but it's heavy.
- WORKED: *"It screamed."*, *"Merrick, the eldest of them, turned first."*, *"my friend Jory was whistling."*

**L13**
- WORKED. *"At forty, I understood it."* is a good hook.

**L15**
- STOP: *"You can weigh it."* Does it mean "judge it for yourself", "it's worth something", or a hearth saying I'm meant to know? It reads like a formula I haven't been taught. If it means "judge whether it's true", the tale doesn't quite show that.

**L17**
- STOP (where things are): *"They'd come home with the timber from the edge of the grey, the great fog bank out at sea."* If the grey is a fog bank out at sea, how is there timber at its edge? It only made sense later, at L23 (trees standing at the edge) and L31 (an outpost at the edge of the land, "out toward the grey"). The first gloss of *the grey* needs to allow land and trees inside it, for example *"the great fog that hangs where the land runs out, and over the sea beyond"*.
- WORKED: *"the weather still in their beards"*, *"tracks of birds on wet sand"*, and the ship-masters knocking out their pipes on the lowest stone.

**L19**
- TWICE: in *"she laughed and filled his cup"*, which "she" is it? The grandmother at twelve, or her mother who owned the house? I chose the grandmother.
- BORED (slightly): the gloss tacked onto the dialogue, *"using the old harbour name for whatever lies out there inside the grey."* I'd have taken it from context. It could stand as its own short sentence after the quote.

**L21**
- WORKED. *"She said it about her own brother."* is a real turn.

**L23**
- BORED (mildly): *"back in the days when our cities sent men out to the edge of the grey to cut down the tall black trees that stood there, for their timber."* It's clear but long, and *"for their timber"* repeats L17.
- HINT (I got it and liked it): *"one of the last timber-boats"*. I guessed this is why the felling ended.

**L25**
- WORKED: *"into the wind and against the current, which no boat can do"*, and the gulls screaming at boys who rob their nests.
- TWICE: *"After that came the rain, falling on the heads of two crews."* Are the crews in the water, drowning? Is the rain a mercy or a curse? I worked out that the men are in the sea, but only on a second pass. Keep the line, because it pays off in the rhyme (*"and after it, the rain"*). Just put the men in the water: *"falling on the heads of two crews in the sea."*

**L27**
- WORKED. *"Three men came home."*

**L29**
- WORKED: the sea running out of his coat onto the floor she had swept. The spar gloss is short and fine.
- Small: here only Daveth clings to the spar, but later *"the three from the spar"* and *"one of the three who came home on the spar"* put all three on it. Say so here.

**L31 (the paragraph I stumbled on most in IV.5)**
- TWICE, nearly three times: *"After them, the men of the outpost, our last post at the edge of the land, out toward the grey, started swearing to things over their drinks."* Three appositives come before the verb, and *"After them"* is vague. After the three men? After they came home?
- STOP: *"They swore they'd pushed a boat off burning, with a dead man in it."* It took me a moment to see that this was the same boat that struck Daveth, and that the outpost had set it alight. Then came *"the grey man … the stranger who'd come in that boat"*. I couldn't tell whether "a dead man" and "the grey man" were the same person until L49 (*"an armed stranger had come and been put down"*). Letting it out slowly is good, but here I lost the thread. *"with the stranger dead in it"* would let me follow without giving away L49.
- WORKED: *"three words of our own language"*, which chilled me, and *"no two of them agreed."*

**L33–L37**
- WORKED: counting on the fingers, then the flat hand.
- CONTRADICTION: *"Now, when the ship-masters tell it, they say twenty."* The headnote says *"The old ship-masters would never tell it"*, and L45 has one walking out rather than hear it. If they never tell it, who says twenty? Try *"Now, when the quays tell it…"*.

**L39–L43**
- WORKED: *"filled the three men's cups under the lowest stone"*, *"At night she didn't laugh."*, and *"the harbour drowned him a stone's throw from his mother's harbour-house."* These are the best plain sentences in the tale.

**L45**
- TWICE: *"Each of them set his saying down the way you'd set down a cup."* Does it mean carefully, finally, or so as not to spill? It's a nice image, but I didn't know which way to read it. *"…a full cup"* would point it.
- The gloss for *the Road* is fine.

**L47**
- WORKED. The three sayings hit hard, especially *"The shore will never have to meet it."*

**L49**
- WORKED. This is where it all clicked. *"put down"* is cold, as if he were an animal. *"And who's going to argue with writing?"* is good.

**L51**
- WORKED. *"for the littlest ones Jory turned it slower still"* sets up the ending, and it's perfect.

**L53–L60 (the rhyme)**
- STOP: *"the warden's door"*. Who is the warden? He's never mentioned before. I guessed he was the commander of the outpost. A rhyme can be cryptic, but if L31 mentioned *the warden* once, the rhyme would click instead of puzzling me.
- HINT (half-got, and I liked it): *"A stone for a present"*. Were the three grey stones over the fire the stranger's gifts? I wondered and couldn't tell. If that's intended, it's a lovely half-hint. If not, it's a false trail.
- WORKED: *"a cut for a kiss. / Who ever heard of a greeting like this?"* This is where I understood that the stranger came kneeling, with a gift, and was killed. That is the moral weight of the tale, and it landed.
- Minor: the italic *"Then the children sang, as they still sing it"* switches from Voss's voice to the frame's mid-tale. I noticed the switch, but it didn't trip me.

**L62–L65**
- WORKED: *"in case a ship-master heard"*, and the two lines nobody sang.

**L67–L69**
- WORKED: Merrick coiling the rope *"the way a sailor coils a line he means to keep"*, and the reveal of his father.
- WHO IS THIS: if Merrick is the son of Voss's grandmother's brother, he's Voss's own kin. Voss never says "my kinsman", and the silence made me wonder whether I'd got the family tree wrong.

**L71**
- BORED: *"Merrick, the eldest of the ship-masters"* is the third time I'm told he's the eldest. *"Merrick is sitting by the door tonight. He hasn't gone out."* is enough. I caught the echo of the man who walked out into the rain without any help, and it moved me.

**L73**
- The gloss for *the havens* is fine, but *"won back our ten cities on the coast"* is the first time I learn the cities are lost. That's a big fact to slip into a clause. *"our ten burnt cities"* would carry it.
- WORKED: *"It's only a rhyme."*

**L75**
- BORED, and in the worst place: *"along the wall-walk, the path along the top of the wall."* "Wall-walk" explains itself, and the gloss breaks the most important paragraph in the tale. Cut it.
- WORKED: *"He whistled it slowly, the way he used to turn the rope for the littlest of us."* The mooring-rope pulled tight also works.

**L77–L79**
- WORKED. *"I did not tell him to stop."* is the emotional peak, and the uncontracted "did not" is right.

**L81**
- BORED: *"a fire-ship, one of their ships all ablaze."* "Fire-ship" is plain English, and the gloss slows the climax. Cut it.
- STOP (small): how does a ship burning on the stones below kill a man on the wall-walk above? I read it as the boat "coming for him", as in the rhyme, and I think the vagueness is deliberate. A few readers will want a physical cause.

**L83**
- WORKED. *"The tune stopped."* is perfect.

**L85**
- BORED (mildly): *"the first of the heaps of stones we pile over our dead there"*. Most readers know "cairn", or will get it from the next clause. *"where the ground is too hard for a spade"* earns its place, because it is the "ice and stone" again. Trim it to something like *"His is the first cairn on the east face, where the ground is too hard for a spade."*

**L87**
- WORKED. *"with no roof over them but the rain"* is beautiful, and *"burnt Eldhythe"* lands.

**L89**
- STOP: *"Mystwood, the wood out there in the grey."* I had only just learned *the Mystlands*, and now there's a second Myst- name. Is it the same place? Is the wood inside the lands?
- HINT: why would the *wood* remember, and not the sea, which was the thing that "didn't laugh"? I only understood after V.1 (the wood holds thoughts). That's a nice payoff in hindsight, but read on its own the last line is atmosphere more than meaning.
- WORKED: *"We forget. We've always been good at that."* The hearth's *"We remember"* answers it beautifully.

## Tale by tale

### IV.5 · The Burning Boat
**Did I follow it?** Yes, after one reread.

I followed the main line:
- the grandmother's harbour-house;
- Daveth's two boats;
- the stranger killed and his boat burned;
- the rhyme;
- Jory whistling it;
- Jory dead.

I lost the thread in two places:
- **L31:** the outpost confession. "A dead man", "the grey man" and "that boat" don't connect until L49.
- **The rhyme:** the warden is never introduced.

The layout of the place was fuzzy throughout: a sea beach under a mountain fortress, and timber at the edge of a fog bank at sea. It didn't stop the story.

**Did it move me?** Yes, a lot. The ending works:
- the rope turned slowly for the littlest ones;
- *"I did not tell him to stop"*;
- *"The tune stopped"*;
- Merrick by the door, not gone out.

It's a real ghost story with guilt in it, and the rhyme is the best thing in the group. The only thing that weakened it was the glosses at the climax (wall-walk, fire-ship, cairn), which slowed me just when I wanted to be held.

### BOOK FIVE, the Argument
**Did I follow it?** No.

**Did it move me?** It intrigued me. It's a riddle I couldn't solve on a first read, which is acceptable for an epigraph.

### V.1 · The First Glyph
**Did I follow it?** Yes. The middle took effort.

These parts were clear: the wreck, the marks, the hands on the grain and the first word. The gloss for *knowing* is excellent.

The middle (L107–L121) is where I struggled:
- too many new names, four of them starting with Ha-;
- *"bonded … joined as one from birth"* gives me nothing concrete;
- *"Harl of the burning shore"* is never explained;
- the mute stones might or might not be IV.5's stones;
- the wreck is grey, but the legend is about black wood.

**Did it move me?** Yes, at three points:
- Jory alive and whistling the tune. The irony is earned by IV.5.
- Seren's river and cup.
- The closing exchange: the grey man's three words, and our one.

Those three make the tale. IV.5 feeding into V.1 is the strongest thing this group does.

## Top fixes, ranked

1. **L31:** make the outpost confession connect. Tie *"a dead man"* to *the grey man* (*"with the stranger dead in it"*), and break up the three appositives before the verb.
2. **L107:** give *"bonded pair … joined as one from birth"* one concrete handle, and keep it from reading as a child marriage.
3. **L111–L113:** thin V.1's names.
   - Drop *"Harl has a quick temper"*.
   - Explain or drop *"of the burning shore"*.
   - Think about making Hale just "the runner", to break up the Ha- cluster.
4. **IV.5's climax:** cut the glosses.
   - Cut wall-walk (L75) and fire-ship (L81).
   - Trim the cairn gloss (L85).
   - Drop the third *"eldest of the ship-masters"* (L71).
5. **L37:** fix *"Now, when the ship-masters tell it, they say twenty"*, which contradicts *"would never tell it"*.
6. **Settle the layout once.**
   - Put the Keep on a mountain ridge over the sea the first time it's named (L11).
   - Gloss the grey so that it can have land and trees at its edge (L17).
7. **L31:** mention *the warden* once, so that the rhyme's *"warden's door"* clicks.
8. **L121:** settle three things.
   - Grey planks against black wood.
   - What the stones and the bundle *"might speak to us"* about.
   - Whether the mute stones are kin to the harbour stones.
9. **L103:** shorten the repeated gate-stone aside when the tales are read in order.
10. **L131:** make *"the thought of a forest … the thought of the shore"* concrete.
11. **Smaller points:**
   - *"You can weigh it"* (L15): what does it mean?
   - The two Myst- names (L19, L89).
   - *"He was seventeen"*, past tense (L113).
   - The time-step back to a living Jory (L133): consider one nudge.
