## IV.1 · Of the Felling of the Black Pillars

**When-line (l.9).** *"in the year of the Second Tide, as the wall named that year's enemy fleets."* **STOP**, and I read it twice. "As the wall named" seems to mean "according to how the people on the wall named things", but on a first read it sounds like the wall itself was naming the fleets. I also didn't know what *the siege* was, or whose. I got used to the idea later, but nothing in IV.1 says that "we" are refugees in a mountain fortress under siege. The only clue is *"the one I carried up the mountain"* halfway through. One clause would fix this, for example "the siege of this fortress", or "in the year we called the Second Tide, after that year's enemy fleet".

**Headnote (l.11).**
- *"Laid by me, Seren Two-Inks, who write this Book"*. **STOP** twice over. "Laid" is an odd verb for telling a story; the closing *"I have laid this tale"* explains it, but only at the end. *"who write"* reads like a typo for "who writes", even though it's correct English. Then **WHY**: why "Two-Inks"? It's never explained. That's fine as colour, but I did wonder.
- *"from the Harvest rolls, the old records our builders' guild kept of the Harvest. The Harvest lasted…"*: three uses of "Harvest" in two sentences. **OVER**, and clunky. "Harvest" made me think of farming until the next sentence said "cut down the black trees". That part works.
- *"{{Halyna}}, Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders, read the rolls to me"*. **This was my biggest STOP in IV.1.** It reads as a list of three people: Halyna, Halvard, and Rhyna. Then "the husband and wife" says there are two. It took me a while to work out that "Halyna" is one name for the two of them together. The bonded-pair custom, *"two joined as one"*, isn't explained until IV.3's headnote. Until then, *"{{Halyna}} wouldn't read me"* and *"{{Halyna}} have laid all four of their hands"* look like grammar mistakes. Half a sentence here, something like "Halyna, the name our people give to Halvard and Rhyna together, since…", would save the reader the puzzle.
- *"(At our hearth, whoever tells a tale holds the old gate-stone.)"* **GOOD**. It's short, and it sets up *"Hold the stone, Seren."* "Gate-stone" made me wonder "what gate?", but in a pleasant way.

**l.13** *"Hold the stone, Seren."* **WHO**: who says this? I assume Halyna or the hearth. It works as a ritual line, but I'd have liked a speaker.

**l.15** *"the archive chest, the chest of our guild's old records"*. **OVER**: "archive chest" already says it. *"The cord on the oldest roll … broke in Rhyna's fingers"* is a **GOOD** physical detail.

**l.19–21 (Halyna's quote)** *"We two didn't live through it," / "but our fathers' fathers did."* **STOP**. The quote is split over two lines with no speaker tag, and at first it looked like a formatting error. By IV.3, where *"She carried it every step," / "and after the passes she carried it alone"* comes, I'd worked out that each spouse speaks half. That's a lovely device, but nothing tells the reader so the first time. *"They wrote it all down, and never read what they had written."* is **GOOD**, one of the best lines in the tale.

**l.25** *"In the oldest roll, the grey, the great bank of fog that lies out at sea, is only a darkish haze"* is the second gloss of the grey, and it's **OVER**. *"After that it becomes a question"* was a **STOP**: after what? I think it means "in the later rolls". *"who breathed it?"* is a **HINT** I didn't get, but it intrigued me (someone breathes the fog?). *"When the answer did come, it wasn't written in our letters."* is a **HINT** I half-got. I guessed it meant the carved stones or the enemy, and was happy to wait.

**l.27**
- *"Our ships went farther out than their fathers had ever dared."* The ships' fathers? It should be the sailors'. A small pronoun slip.
- *Mystholders* is **GOOD**: the name and its reason (they hold up the fog like posts holding up a roof) are both clear.
- *"harder than oak and lighter than iron"*: almost any wood is lighter than iron, so the comparison falls flat. Something like "lighter than ash" would do more work.

**l.29** *"For two generations the Trade Council sent out the axes. That was the council of merchants at Eldhythe, our oldest city, who ran the havens' trade."* It's clear, but the gloss stands as its own sentence and stops the momentum. *"Listen to it now, and hold on to it, because it's ours."* is **GOOD**: it tells me this is our guilt.

**l.31 (the cargo list).** **STOP, and overload.**
- There are eight new place names in one paragraph: Tidesmeet, Fenholm, Holtward, Highreach, Sandreach, Rimewatch, Emberhythe and Carnhold. Each gets a gloss, so I could follow each one, but I couldn't hold any of them. The "Black wood for…" chant is meant to build, and the glosses keep breaking its rhythm.
- **Contradiction:** these are supposed to be *"our ten cities along the coast"*, yet the list has a fort in a forest, a tower on crags, galleries in a red canyon and a hall on a frozen lake. Those don't sound coastal. I stopped and wondered whether "havens" meant something other than harbours.
- **Confusion:** *"burned slowly in the forges of Emberhythe"*, then *"Carnhold, the forge-town"*. Which city is the forge-town? Emberhythe is the only name with no gloss, and its forges make it sound like the forge-town too.
- *"the cranes of Highreach, which lifted every stone of that tower up its crags"*: "that tower" made me read twice, because no tower had been mentioned. I take it Highreach is a tower-city.
- I counted nine havens (adding Eldhythe), not ten. A reader who counts will notice.
- **HINT** got: *"Rimewatch that stood out on the ice"*. The "stood" hints it's gone. Nice.
- *"the axes were hammered out, and went back to the edge for more"* is a **GOOD** closing turn.

**l.33–43** **GOOD.** *"And then it went into things that didn't need it at all."*, the dancing-hall floor, the dice-boards, the stained roll, and then *"a merchant of Tidesmeet had one whole pillar cut into a single bed. He slept in it, and he was proud."* This is the best stretch of the tale's first half: the ruin moves from building into vanity. The gloss on harbour-houses, *"the taverns on the quays where the ship-masters drank and did their business"*, is welcome, because I wouldn't have known the word.

**l.45** *"Halvard turned the page, and turned it back."* **GOOD**. One line, and it carries all his reluctance.

**l.47–49** **GOOD.** The regrowth is clear, *"our guild bought the poles"* lands as complicity, and *"They cut it, and it grew back. They cut it, and it grew back. They cut it."* is the strongest moment in IV.1. I felt it in my stomach.

**l.51–55**
- *"so small that twice I had to bend the lamp down to it before she could read it"* is **GOOD**.
- *"All along the edge, the stumps put up nothing."* then *"Not one of them was growing back."* **OVER**: the italic line was perfectly clear, and the restatement drains it. Cut "Not one of them was growing back."
- *"I'd have kept his name in this Book."* **GOOD**, quietly moving.

**l.57** *"I tried that word under my tongue, and it gave way, like old mortar that has crumbled to sand."* **GOOD**, and it sounds like a mason talking. The tiny snag is that "good fortune" is two words, not one. **HINT** got: fewer trees means thinner fog, and something is being harmed.

**l.59** *"We Stonewrights did warn the Council."* **STOP / WHO**. This is the first time "Stonewrights" appears. I guessed it's the builders' guild, but until now it had only been "our builders' guild". It needs one introduction somewhere earlier, even just "our builders' guild, the Stonewrights". *"a page no thumb had ever smudged"* is **GOOD**.

**l.61** The warning is clear and plain. **GOOD.**

**l.63–67** All clear. *"the kind of thing grandmothers say by the fire"*, *"Men drank under them and did their sums"* and *"its own warning with them"* are all **GOOD**. *"And there really were carvings."* reads slightly oddly, since nobody had doubted it, but it's fine.

**l.69** **Big STOP.**
- *"When they touch the carved wreck-wood that the sea brings in from the enemy's ships, what's carved there comes into them whole, the way a memory does."* A supernatural ability appears in one subordinate clause, alongside an enemy I haven't met (only *"enemy fleets"* in the when-line). I reread it three times. Is this a Halyna-only gift, or something all pairs can do? Is it new? Why can they do it? It's such a big idea that either it needs one more beat (even "Since the wood began coming ashore this winter, they have found…") or it should be left out of this tale.
- *"Stone doesn't translate."* is **GOOD** and striking. I took the **HINT**: their writing lives in wood, not stone.
- *"the one I carried up the mountain"* is a **HINT**, paid off in IV.3. **GOOD.**

**l.71**
- *"the outermost post, the guard-post farthest out toward the fog"* is **OVER**: "outermost" and "farthest out" say the same thing.
- *"copied it in between a count of poles and a count of oars, with exactly the same care"* and *"read it out the same way he'd read the oars"* are **GOOD**: the flatness is the horror.
- Framing the letter beforehand as "a report … of a stranger and his boat" helped me. I'd have stumbled without it.

**l.73 (the letter)** *"Unknown intruder. Hostile intent. Neutralized. Vessel burned and released."* **STOP, on tone.** It sounds like a modern police or military report ("Neutralized", "Hostile intent"), unlike anything else in this world. Maybe that's deliberate, cold bureaucratic euphemism. It does chill me, but it also jolted me out of the world for a second. If it's deliberate, keep it. If not, it's the one place the plain words feel twentieth-century.

**l.75–81**
- The blot and *"Rhyna laid her finger on the gap, and took it away."* are **GOOD**.
- *"two timber-boats … lost at the edge"* is a **HINT**: I guessed a reprisal.
- *"I didn't know what I was holding."* is **GOOD**, and IV.3 pays it off hard.

**l.83** **GOOD.** *"It wasn't wisdom that ended the Harvest. … No one decided it."* is bleak and true. *"the year of the burning boat"* is a **HINT** I got, thanks to the letter two paragraphs earlier, and getting it felt good.

**l.85** *"Some of you sitting at this fire tonight were those children."* **GOOD**. The direct address to the hearth made me feel I was sitting there.

**l.87**
- *"The temples, where our guild kept the faith of the whole land, were mended."* **STOP / WHY**. The builders' guild keeps the faith? Are the Stonewrights builders and priests both? It's a big fact about the world, given in passing, and it confused me about who "we" are.
- *"as glad as men mending a sea-wall on a calm day"* made me read twice. I think it means comfortable repairs made with no sense of urgency, and so too late. It's ambiguous.
- *"That is our share of the wrong."* is a little **OVER** after *"turned the key on them"*, but it's acceptable as a confession.

**l.91** *"The first dark sails, the first of the grey ships that burned our havens, were already on the sea."* **STOP, and a weakened ending.** "Dark sails" and "grey ships" are two different colours in one breath. Worse, the biggest event in the book, our cities burned, arrives as a tucked-in gloss. Either let *"The first dark sails were already on the sea."* stand alone, with the burning implied by the when-line and the siege, or give the burning its own sentence.

**l.93–95** The closing ritual, *"We remember"*, is **GOOD**.

### IV.1 verdict
- **Did I follow it?** Mostly, I'd say 85%. The story is clear: our grandparents felled strange trees that held up the fog, ignored their own guild's warning, watched the stumps stop regrowing, locked away stones they couldn't read, filed a man's killing as a cargo note, and then the enemy came out of the thinning fog. I could have retold it.
- **Where I lost it:** the three-people reading of Halyna, the wood-reading power dropped in a clause, Stonewrights and the guild-as-temple, and the eight-city list.
- **Did it move me?** Yes, in stretches. The bed, *"They cut it, and it grew back… They cut it."*, the unnamed clerk, the letter between the poles and the oars, and *"Some of you … were those children"* all hit. The glosses flatten the middle third. It reads like a strong tale with a tour guide walking beside it.

## Patterns across the group

1. **The "X, the Y" appositive is the default way of explaining.**
   - Examples: *the grey, the great fog*; *the archive chest, the chest of…*; *the outermost post, the guard-post farthest out*; *the Trade Council, the merchants who…*; *a spar, a broken length…*; *buttresses, the great props…*; *harbour-houses, the taverns on the quays…*; *the Fall, the twenty months…*; *the Road, the long march…*.
   - Each one is fine on its own, but there are so many that the prose starts to drone. Two fixes would help: vary the method (sometimes let context teach the word, sometimes give a whole sentence), and drop the glosses on things already explained earlier in the same group.
2. **Repeated glosses across the two tales.** The grey is glossed three times; Eldhythe ("our oldest city"), the Trade Council, the black pillars and Halyna twice each. If each tale must stand alone, keep the gloss in the headnote and strip it from the body.
3. **Glosses inside first-person testimony** (Brenn, l.113 and l.115) hurt the most, because they break the voice. Move them into Seren's headnote.
4. **The pair-name custom is explained a tale too late.** *"Our builders work in bonded pairs, two joined as one"* should appear, or be echoed, at IV.1's first *{{Halyna}}*.
5. **The guild has three names** (builders' guild, Stonewrights, masons) and it also *"kept the faith of the whole land"*. Introduce "Stonewrights" once, and say plainly if they're priests as well as builders.
6. **Over-restatement after a strong line**, in three places: *"Not one of them was growing back."*, *"He meant: leave nothing…"*, and the double blot explanation. The book's own lines are already plain. Trust them.
7. **What works without help:** the physical gestures, the refrains (*"They cut it…"*, *"We obeyed"*), the letter repeated word for word, the oath and its betrayal, and every ending line. A newcomer needs no gloss for any of these.

## Top fixes, in order of impact

1. IV.1 l.11: make it clear that Halyna is the pair's shared name, not a third person, and borrow the "bonded pairs" line from IV.3.
2. IV.3 l.113 and l.115: take the glosses out of Brenn's mouth.
3. IV.1 l.69: either give the wood-reading gift one more beat, or cut it from this tale.
4. IV.1 l.31: resolve the forge-town clash (Emberhythe or Carnhold), and either soften "along the coast" for a list that includes a forest, crags, a canyon and a frozen lake, or note that some havens lie inland.
5. IV.1 l.91: let *"The first dark sails were already on the sea."* end the tale without the appositive, or give the burning its own sentence. Also fix dark sails versus grey ships.
6. Cut the three over-restatements (l.55, l.163, and the first half of l.197).
7. Both when-lines: rephrase *"as the wall named that year's enemy fleets"*. Separately, check whether *"Tide of Remembering"* is meant to puzzle.
8. IV.3 l.211: say which stone.
