# g02 — a newcomer's read (I.2 The Cry of the Bonded, I.3 The Captain's Long Breath)

**How I read it.** I read only `plain/g02.md`, as someone who had never heard of Rivenkeep. The file ends with a `## WRITER'S NOTES` section. That isn't story text, so I left it out of my judgement and stopped reading at that heading. I've assumed `{{Halyna}}` and the like render as plain names (*Halyna*) and that the `<!-- HALYNA -->` comments don't show. If a reader ever sees the braces, that's a separate problem.

**Tags.** **[WORD]** a word, name, title or custom I didn't understand · **[WHO/WHY]** "who is this?" or "why does this matter?" · **[HINT]** a hint I didn't get, or one left hanging · **[REREAD]** I had to read the sentence twice · **[OVER]** over-explained, and my attention dipped · **[LANDED]** it worked (added so you know what to protect).

Line numbers refer to `g02.md`.

## I.2 · The Cry of the Bonded

**L3 (when-line).** Clear. *"the old fortress high on the mountain ridge"* gives me a picture right away. A small snag: it says the tale was told *"a few nights after the enemy's first attack"*, but that attack never comes into the tale. I accepted it as framing.

**L5 (headnote).**
- **[REREAD]** *"This tale is my own, Seren's, who writes this Book"*. The *"my own, Seren's, who"* run is awkward, and I parsed it twice. *"This is my own tale. I am Seren, who writes this Book"* would read at once.
- **[LANDED]** *"We say a tale is 'laid,' like a stone in a wall, because a laid tale stays."* It's lovely and perfectly clear.
- **[WORD]** *"the old gate-stone by the fire"*. I could only half picture it: a stone from a gate, brought indoors? It's enough to go on.
- *"one of the guild's children"*. Which guild? I find out at L19, which is fine.

**L7.**
- **[WHO/WHY] [REREAD], the biggest stumble in the file.** *"Halyna, Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders"* reads as a list of **three** people. Then *"husband and wife"* tells me there are two. I stopped and counted, and only worked out by elimination that *Halyna* is a joined name (Hal- + -yna) for the couple. Once I saw it, I liked it. But nothing tells me that's what's going on, and the same pattern comes back with *Idrenna* (L63) and *Orvenna, Ormund and Penna* (L73). One clause the first time would fix all three, something like *"Halyna, which is what we call Halvard and Rhyna together"*.
- *"took my two hands and laid them on the stone"* links back to the gate-stone. Good.

**L11–13.** **[LANDED]** *"Hold the stone." / "Hold it, and it will hold you."* I sensed each line came from a different speaker, which fits the pair. It's warm.

**L17.** **[LANDED]** *"So I'm holding it."* This is a lovely, simple bridge into the story.

**L19.**
- **[REREAD]** *"We call it that because of what happened that evening"*. Call **what** that? The nearest noun is *"the Keep's old courtyard"*, so I first took it to mean the courtyard is named for that evening. On a second read it means *"the day of the banner"*. Put the explanation right after *"the day of the banner"*, or say *"We call it the day of the banner because..."*.
- **[WHO/WHY]** *"The Captain"*. Who is he, and why are soldiers with a builders' guild? I'm not told until L33. The word "captain" carries me, but L33's line (*"only the captain of one small company of soldiers"*) would help much more here, at his first appearance.
- **[HINT]** *"wrote on one half in coal"*. Wrote **what**? I wanted to know at once, and the tale never tells me (see L41).
- Small picture clash: they sit *"in the grass"*, but then *"the last of the daylight lay on the snow"*. Is it grass, snow, or patches of both? A word like *"the snow-patched grass"* would settle it.
- **[LANDED]** *"Up on the wall the soldiers were cheering the banner. Down in the grass, we said nothing."* It's a strong contrast and tells me the guild is torn.

**L21–23.**
- **[LANDED]** The boy who *"knotted a net as he went"*, and then *"I came into that courtyard alone. He never reached the Keep."* This hit hard. I didn't need his name.
- **[HINT]**, mostly got. Why would a builders' child be knotting a net? I wondered whether he came from a fishing family. I'm curious, not lost.

**L25.**
- **[WORD]** *"two builders joined as one from birth"*. **How** are they joined? Are they twins? Betrothed in the cradle? Joined by some kind of magic? Halvard and Rhyna are *"husband and wife"* and *"one pair since the cradle"*, and Enno and Della are a boy and a girl, so I guessed something like betrothal from birth. Later, *"Aetherbonded"* (L65) hints at something supernatural. I was never sure what the bond actually is. Some mystery is fine, but at the moment I'm unsure rather than intrigued.
- **[HINT], landed.** *"Of all the children's pairs, Enno and Della were the only one that came up the mountain whole"*, set beside the boy. I worked out on my own that Seren and the boy were a pair. That's exactly the right amount of telling.
- **[OVER], slightly.** *"with both of them still living"*. *"whole"* together with the boy two lines earlier already does the job. It's minor, and you could keep it for very young readers.

**L27.**
- Clear. *"ten havens die in twenty months"* is strong, and the havens and the grey sails are glossed cleanly.
- *"Mortar and chisels were the only weapons we had"* comes right after *"We never lifted a hand to fight"*. That gave me a half-second "wait, weapons?" before I read it as a metaphor. It's fine.
- I don't know who the enemy is, and I didn't mind. It feels deliberate.

**L29.** **[LANDED]** *"sat on us like a wet cloak"* and *"whose blood? Whose hands?"* This is the real question of the tale, and it's clearly put.

**L31.**
- **[LANDED]** *"spoke to no one, because they didn't need to."*
- **[REREAD]** *"Her family had laid that very gate... and his family had cut its stone"*. Coming straight after *Halyna*, which sounds like a woman's name, I first took *"her"* to mean Halyna. It means Rhyna. That's another reason to explain the pair-name at L7.
- **[WHO/WHY]** Their ancestors built **this** Keep? So the guild has come back to a fortress its own people built? That's a big, interesting fact that slips by in a subordinate clause. I wanted one more beat on it.
- **[OVER]** *"They were husband and wife"* repeats what L7 already said.

**L33.**
- *"He leads us all now, but that evening he was only the captain of one small company of soldiers."* It's useful, but it comes late (see L19).
- **[HINT]** *"I had watched his cloak go up, and I didn't know yet what it meant for us."* What **did** it mean? This is the second time the banner is held up as important and left unexplained.
- **[WHO/WHY]** *"It's the only speech of his that anyone at this hearth can repeat word for word"*. Does he hardly ever speak? Or was he unforgettable just this once? I liked the mystery.
- **[LANDED]** The black fingers and *"half of a cloak hung on his back"*.

**L35–37.** **[LANDED]** The question is sharp. *"I remember the wind in the grass"* is a great beat of silence.

**L39–43.**
- **[WHO/WHY]** *"They looked up at the torn cloak on the wall and bowed to it"*. Why bow to a torn cloak? I'm sure it matters (something written on it, or a sign they recognise?), but I don't know what was written, so the bow is a gesture I can't read. **I'd want even a hint of what the coal said, or why a builder would bow to it.**
- *"Their chisels went up together, as one."* followed by *"The last of the light caught on their two chisels."* Two sentences in a row about the chisels. The second is the better image. The first almost uses it up in advance.

**L45–51.** **[LANDED]** The cry. *"The mortar cries out from the ground"* is strange and good. I took it the way you'd take "blood crying out from the ground". *"Rise! Rise! Rise!"* works.

**L55–57.**
- **[WHO/WHY]**, then got it. *"Some people tell it as if she called and he answered, with a silence in between."* My first thought was "why does this matter?" Then *"There was no silence."* answered it: they are one. **[LANDED]**
- *"Hers came first for no reason except that a cry has to begin somewhere."* It's nice.

**L59.**
- **[LANDED]** The arch simile is clear even if, like me, you don't know how arches are built.
- **[WHO/WHY]** *"the column, the long line of our people who had walked up the mountain together"*. If the guild is in the courtyard, who is in the column? Other refugees, families who aren't builders? *"our people"* sounds like the guild again, and that confused me. Say who they are, for example *"the townsfolk who had walked up the mountain behind us"*, or whatever is true.

**L61.** **[LANDED]** *"Old stone doesn't shake. I felt it shake under my feet."* This is the best line in the first half.

**L63.**
- **[LANDED]** *"the great fireplace you're sitting at now"*. Being spoken to directly pulled me into the room.
- *"Idrenna, the oldest pair in our guild"*. By now I've guessed the pair-name pattern, so it's fine. Note that I never learn the two names behind it, unlike Halyna and Orvenna. I didn't mind.
- **[LANDED]** *"the Captain didn't hurry them"*. Later I noticed it chimes with I.3 (*"the Captain who won't hurry"*). Nice.

**L65.**
- Clear: the Stonewrights won't carry weapons, but they will build under fire, and *"a breach, a hole broken in the wall"*.
- **[REREAD], the second-worst stumble.** *"in the guild's own words: with the speed and precision that only Aetherbonded pairs could achieve"*. The tone suddenly changes. A twelve-year-old at a fire is suddenly quoting something that sounds like a rulebook or a product description (*"speed and precision... could achieve"*). It isn't a full sentence, and I couldn't tell whether the *"terms"* were written down. If this wording has to stay, it needs a frame like *"as the terms were later written down"* so I know why it sounds different.
- **[WORD]** *"Aetherbonded is the guild's old word for its bonded pairs, who work as one."* "Aether" makes me think of magic or the heavens. Is the bond holy? Magical? The gloss tells me what the word **covers**, but not what it **means**. I'm left wondering, which may be what you want. Just be aware it raises a question it doesn't answer.
- **[REREAD]** *"gave the Captain the whole of it in two halves, one of them saying each"*. The last clause is clumsy. *"each of them saying one half"* is easier.

**L67–71.** **[LANDED]** *"Not the sword, but the stone." / "Not the killing, but the keeping."* These are memorable, and they answer L29's question.

**L73–77. This is where my attention dipped.**
- **[OVER]** We go straight from the high point (the cry, the terms) to a rules briefing: three named parts of the day, each defined. The quarryman's seam simile is nice. But the laying, the hauling and the holding read like the rules of a system, not like a memory.
- **[WORD]** *"when the guns are moved while they're cold"*. Moved where, and why? I guessed "cold" means not recently fired, but I didn't know why guns need moving at all.
- **[REREAD]** *"Every gun you haul is a row of stone we don't lay"*. Why does hauling a gun cost a row of stone? Same workers? Same hours? It feels like a trade-off that matters, but I can't see how it works. Half a clause would do it (*"because it's our hands that haul them"*, or whatever is true).
- **[WHO/WHY]** *"Before that fire burned down, Orvenna..."* comes straight after a long Idrenna passage. That's a lot of elders in a row.

**L79–84.** **[LANDED]** The chant is clear and has a good rhythm. *"and the wall will hold"* is a satisfying close.

**L86.** **[LANDED]** *"look again. You'll see two."* It's simple, and it made the pairs real to me.

**L88.**
- *"We set every stone across the joint between the two below it, so that no crack runs straight through."* It's a little technical and slightly **[OVER]**. Then I realised it might be about the pairs too (one stone holding two together). If that's meant, it's quietly good. If not, it's a builder's aside I could do without.
- **[LANDED]** *"It's a song for two voices: one singer takes each line up to the comma, and the other sings the rest."* This setup is exactly what I needed for L97. Don't cut it.

**L92–95.** The song is clear. The commas make the *"up to the comma"* rule easy to follow.

**L97.** **[LANDED], the emotional peak of the file.** *"I sang up to the comma, and then, with no one beside me, I sang the rest."* I felt this. As a newcomer, I needed *"with no one beside me"* to be sure of the meaning. It's the one place where spelling it out helps.

**L99–101.** **[LANDED]** Enno and Della asleep holding hands, *"the way they'd come up the mountain"*, while Seren sleeps *"by the water buckets"*. It's quiet and devastating. *"By morning the mortar had set."* is a perfect last line.

**L103.** **[REREAD]** *"I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me."* But L5 says this is Seren's **own** tale, the first she ever laid. Nobody laid it **for** her, because she lived it. I stopped. I guessed it's a fixed formula every teller says, but nothing tells me so. Hale says it in I.3 about his own experience too. One frame line the first time (*"as every teller ends"*) would stop the stumble.

**L105.** **[LANDED]** *"We remember."* It's a good communal close.

### I.2 verdict

- **Did I follow it?** Yes, about 85%. The arc is clear: a broken guild, a question from a stranger, two elders answer, everyone rises, terms are made, the first stones are laid, and a child sings both halves alone. The parts I lost were the pair-names (L7), the meaning of the banner and the bow (L19, L33, L41), and the nature of the bond (L25, L65).
- **Did it move me?** Yes. The boy who *"never reached the Keep"*, Enno and Della being the only pair to arrive whole, and then *"with no one beside me, I sang the rest"* make one quiet thread that goes right through the public story and pays off. *"Old stone doesn't shake"* and *"There was no silence"* gave me chills.
- **Where it sagged:** L65–77. The *"speed and precision"* quote and the three-parts-of-the-day briefing cool the tale between its two best moments. The rest stays with me as a memory. That stretch reads like rules.

## I.3 · The Captain's Long Breath

**L109 (when-line).** Clear.

**L111 (headnote).**
- **[OVER], when read straight after I.2.** *"Whoever holds the old gate-stone by the fire is the one who tells, and we say a tale is 'laid,' like a stone in a wall, because a laid tale stays."* This repeats I.2's headnote almost word for word. If each tale has to stand alone, fine. Read in sequence, I skimmed it.
- **[REREAD]** *"carry what's seen along the wall to the Captain"*. Is it what's seen along the wall, or carried along the wall? It's minor either way.
- **[LANDED]** *"Hale told his standing up, as fast as he runs. I, Seren, who write this Book, have set it down slower."* It's charming and gives Hale a voice before he speaks.

**L113.** **[LANDED]** *"Give me the stone, then. I'm not a storyteller. I run."* This is great voice, and I liked Hale at once.

**L115.**
- **[OVER], slightly.** *"every one of our ten cities on the coast"* glosses *haven* again. Read in sequence, it's redundant.
- **[REREAD]** There are now two stones: the tellers' gate-stone (L113) and the *"runners' stone"*. I had to check which one *"Give me the stone"* meant. It's fine once sorted.

**L117.** **[LANDED]** *"What I saw, I carry. I carry nothing else."* It's a strong oath, and it pays off at L183. Well set up.

**L119.** **[WHO/WHY]** *"Wick swore it last. He's the youngest of us and the fastest..."* Wick gets a whole little portrait and then never comes back. I expected him to matter, perhaps to run the message instead of Hale. Reading this tale alone, it's a loaded gun that never goes off. If he pays off in a later tale, okay, but here he's a distraction.

**L121.**
- **[REREAD]** *"Kael's guns, which are still all the guns we have"*. Does that mean Kael commands every gun, or that we have very few guns? I read it twice and still wasn't sure.
- **[WORD]** *"Kael the Counter"*. Why "the Counter"? It half pays off at L161 (*"Kael was counting under his breath"*), but I never learn what he counts.
- *"the True Men, the Captain's own company of twelve"*. It's clear. "True" makes me curious, in a good way.

**L123.** **[REREAD], an important one.** *"Nothing can change his mind but his own voice at my side, lad, and a man can only stand in one place."* "Change his mind" normally means "persuade him". Here it seems to mean "change the orders he left me". I had to work out the meaning: Kael's orders are fixed until the Captain comes in person, and the Captain is at the far end. That's the **engine of the whole tale**, so it has to be crystal clear. Something like *"Nothing can change those orders except the Captain himself, standing at my side..."* would do it.

**L125–129.**
- The three calls make the rule clearer (*"when the Captain is by"*). Good.
- **[WORD]** *"Change your shot for the sea, the shore, the sky."* The sky? Why shoot at the sky? Are there flying enemies, birds, signals? It made me look up from the page. If it's a setup for later, okay. If not, it's a puzzle with nothing behind it.
- *"watch new water"* is clear enough.

**L131–135.**
- **[OVER]** *"whistled a tune that the old ship-masters hate. It's the tune of a harbour rhyme from long ago, and the men who captained our boats can't bear to hear it."* "Hate" and "can't bear to hear it" say the same thing twice.
- **[HINT], not got.** **Why** do they hate it? Is it bad luck? A curse? A memory of something? *"Merrick didn't laugh."* is a great ominous beat, and I expected the rhyme to call up the late hull. The tale never connects them. I can't tell whether the rhyme **caused**, **foretold** or **has nothing to do with** what happens. As it stands, it feels like a thread from another story.
- **[WHO/WHY]** Jory and Merrick: two more names, and neither comes back.

**L137.**
- **[REREAD]** *"the sails of my first holding"* uses "holding" before it's defined in the next sentence.
- **[OVER], a pacing problem.** The ships have just burst out of the fog *"faster than I could count them"*, and then we stop for a three-sentence lesson on the laying, the hauling and the holding (which I.2 already gave). The action stalls right as it starts.
- **[LANDED]** But the last three sentences are good and earn their place: *"In the laying the hours go slowly... In the holding, an hour is gone in a heartbeat."* They set up the time-stretch. Keep these and cut the definitions before them.
- **[WORD]** *"In the hauling they go slower still."* Why slower than laying stone? I was curious, but it's a small thing.

**L139.**
- **[WORD]** *"the other ships were spent"*. Spent means sunk? Out of shot? Retreated? It's unclear.
- **[OVER], and a pacing problem.** *"one of the spyglasses Pellow of the True Men ground from the glass of Glasspire, his city."* At the tensest moment, I'm given a new person (Pellow), a new city (Glasspire) and how the spyglass was made. I lost the hull for a moment. Just *"through a far-glass, a spyglass"* would be enough here.
- **[HINT]** *"where no ship had ever hung back before"*. Ever? This is the first attack on this wall, so I guessed it means the twenty months of the havens. It needs a moment's thought. More important: **why is a ship that hangs back dangerous?** I understood it was unusual, but not what the threat was.

**L141–145.** **[LANDED]** *"Hull hangs back."* / *"I ran it."* It's clipped and urgent.

**L147.** **[LANDED]** The switch to present tense (*"I run. Under the smoke, head down..."*) is exciting.

**L149–151.**
- **[LANDED]** *"Everything on that wall was moving except him."* This is the image of the tale.
- The tense flips back to past (*"was moving"*, *"didn't turn"*) right after *"The Captain is standing still."* I noticed the wobble. Maybe that's deliberate, memory slipping between now and then. It's fine.

**L153–159.** **[LANDED], the best writing in I.3.** The stone-cart on the long hill, counting the spokes, the ripples on the sail counted like tree rings, *"The gulls hung in the air. The smoke stood still."*, and *"I had time to be afraid. Then I had time to be ashamed of it. Then I had time to stop being afraid."* I was right there.
- **[REREAD]**, small. *"Each one, I think, was a minute."* Each ripple was a minute? It's a lovely idea, but I paused over the logic. Fine as a felt thing.

**L161–169. This is the climax, and its logic escaped me.**
- **[WHO/WHY]** The Captain says *"Hold."* and lets the hull come *"closer and closer, until I could see the wet on its timbers"*, then *"Loose."*, and it breaks *"in the one stretch of that water where it couldn't hurt anyone"*. **Why would breaking it close be safer than breaking it far out?** And who could it have hurt? The masons in the breach below? My instinct says "sink it far away", so letting it come close looks like a risk, not wisdom. I understand Hale doesn't know what the Captain thought (L171, which is great). But I need **one physical clue** to feel the choice was brilliant rather than lucky. For example, the masons were working in the water-gap the hull would have drifted into, or the hull carried fire, or there was a reef or a still pool. Right now I feel awe, but I'm only taking the Captain's genius on trust.
- **[HINT]** *"Kael was counting under his breath."* Counting what: seconds, distance, the Captain's steps? It feels meaningful, and I couldn't decode it.
- **[LANDED]** *"Below us the masons kept on laying stone, two by two, and never looked up."* It's a beautiful callback to I.2's pairs.
- **[LANDED]** *"The hour let us go, slowly, like a held breath going out."* This explains the title. Lovely.

**L171.** **[LANDED]** *"I don't know what the Captain thought. I know what he did."* It's the perfect runner's line, and it ties back to the oath.

**L173–177.**
- **[OVER]** The Halyna gloss is repeated word for word (*"Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders"*). In this tale alone it also brings back the three-names stumble.
- **[WHO/WHY]** Why take a carved plank to the **builders'** leaders and not to the Captain? It's minor. I guessed builders read marks on stone.
- **[LANDED]** *"Marks on the wood."* / *"I ran it."* It's a nice echo of the hull scene.

**L179.** **[REREAD] [HINT], a real misfire.** *"It was the first carved plank the sea ever brought us, and what came of it is Seren's tale."* "Seren's tale" in this file means I.2, which I just read, and I.2 has **no plank**. I went back to check whether I'd missed something. If this points to another tale Seren tells later, it has to say so (*"another tale, one Seren tells"*, or *"but that's a tale for another night"*). The hook itself (carved marks, from the enemy?) is intriguing, and I wanted more.

**L181.**
- **[WHO/WHY]** *"Hesk's lamp on the north wall. Hesk is one of the True Men, and the lamp was his mother's."* It's nice texture, and *"the lamp was his mother's"* hints at a story. But it's yet another new name in the closing stretch. By now I'm holding Hale, Wick, Kael, Jory, Merrick, Pellow, Hesk and the Captain, plus Halyna/Halvard/Rhyna and Seren. That's about thirteen names in about 1,200 words, and five of them do nothing in **this** story (Wick, Jory, Merrick, Pellow, Hesk).
- **[LANDED]** *"By its light I knew the north wall was awake."*

**L183.**
- **[WORD] [WHO/WHY], a big one.** *"as if it were a gift of the Rite, the old builders' ceremony that made the Captain our Commander."* A **ceremony** made him Commander? When? I.2 told me how he came to lead (the cry and the terms), and no ceremony was mentioned. *"a gift of the Rite"* suggests magic powers. That's a huge new idea dropped into the second-to-last paragraph. Also, is he the Captain or the Commander? Everyone calls him the Captain. As a newcomer, this is the one place I felt I'd skipped a chapter.
- **[WHO/WHY]** *"Voss, a soldier from Eldhythe, will tell you it's older"*. Yet another new name, for a passing remark.

**L185.** **[LANDED]** *"It's not the world that slows, lad. It's the Captain who won't hurry. The moment he chose was the moment that came."* This is a great close and settles the "magic or not" question in a grounded way. The last sentence needed a second read, but it rewards it. It also echoes I.2's *"the Captain didn't hurry them"*.

**L187.** Same snag as I.2: *"the way it was laid for me"*, said by the person it happened to.

### I.3 verdict

- **Did I follow it?** I followed the shape, at about 70%. A runner carries a warning, the Captain walks over unhurried, time seems to stretch, he holds fire, then fires, and the ship breaks harmlessly. I did **not** follow *why* the moment mattered: what the late hull threatened, and why waiting made it safe. The tale also hands me several threads that go nowhere here: the rhyme and Merrick, Wick, *"the sky"*, the plank pointing to *"Seren's tale"*, and the Rite.
- **Did it move me?** It thrilled me more than it moved me. Hale's voice (*"I'm not a storyteller. I run."*) and the slow-time passage are vivid, and Kael's last line is a satisfying turn. But because the climax's logic is hidden, my feeling at the end was "wow, the Captain is calm" rather than "wow, the Captain was right". The emotion depends on the reader feeling the danger, and I never quite felt it.
- **Where it sagged:** the definitions at L137 and the Pellow/Glasspire aside at L139, which both stall the action exactly when the ships arrive.

## Patterns across both tales

1. **Pair-names read as three people.** *"Halyna, Halvard and Rhyna"*, and the same with Orvenna. Explain the custom once, at L7, in a single clause, and every later use becomes a pleasure, not a puzzle.
2. **The closing formula contradicts the tale.** *"the way it was laid for me"* is said by both tellers about things they lived through. Frame it once as a ritual line.
3. **Repeated glosses.** If these are read in sequence, the second tale re-explains *laid*, the gate-stone, the havens, Halyna and the three parts of the day almost word for word. Pick one rule: either each tale stands alone (and then accept the repetition), or the later tale trusts the earlier one. Right now I.3's repetition is what I skimmed.
4. **Explanations at moments of action.** I.2 L73–77 and I.3 L137–139 both stop for definitions or backstory just as the energy rises. The emotional moments are told sparingly and land. The rules are told fully and drag.
5. **Hints with no payoff in the file.** The banner's words and the bow, the rhyme, Wick, *"the sky"*, the Rite, and *"Seren's tale"* for the plank. Two or three open threads make a world feel deep. Here, at least six make it feel as if I'm missing pages.
6. **Things that felt over-explained** (I noticed less than I expected, which is good): *"with both of them still living"*, *"husband and wife"* twice, *"hate"* / *"can't bear to hear it"*, and the course-laying aside at L88 (unless it's a metaphor).
7. **Reading level is right.** The sentences are short and the words are plain. Apart from the stumbles above, I never felt talked down to. The modern-reader goal is met.

## Top fixes, in order of how much they'd help a newcomer

1. **I.3 climax:** give one physical clue for why breaking the late hull close in was safe (L161–169).
2. **I.2 L7:** one clause explaining that *Halyna* is the couple's joined name.
3. **I.3 L179:** point the plank to *another* tale, not just *"Seren's tale"*.
4. **I.3 L183:** either explain the Rite in a sentence or drop it from this tale. It's the only spot where I felt I'd skipped a chapter.
5. **I.3 L123:** make *"Nothing can change his mind"* plain, because it's the engine of the tale.
6. **I.2 L65:** frame or soften the *"speed and precision... could achieve"* quote. It breaks the voice.
7. **I.2 L19/L41:** give a hint of what the banner said, or why the elders bow to it.
8. **I.3 L131–135:** give the rhyme even a half-line of reason for the ship-masters' hatred, or link it to the late hull.
9. **I.3 L137–139:** move or cut the three-parts definition and the Pellow/Glasspire aside so the ships keep coming.
10. **Closing formula:** frame *"laid for me"* once.
