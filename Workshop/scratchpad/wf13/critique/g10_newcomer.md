# Newcomer read: g10 (VI.3 · The Knowing of the Green Wood)

I read this as someone who has never heard of Rivenkeep. I judged only the tale itself (lines 1 to 149). The WRITER'S NOTES at the bottom are not for readers, so I set them aside and did not use them to fix anything I had misread. I was reading the raw file, so I also flag the markup that shows up as text (`{{Halyna}}`, `{LAST_THRONE_AT_HAVEN}`, the `:::` block). If those get rendered in the real build, ignore the markup itself but keep the problems the content causes.

Line numbers refer to g10.md.

## Paragraph by paragraph

**L5, the when-line.** *"Told in the sixth autumn, the year we took back our ten cities, on the night the boat went out."*
- This works well as a hook. "The night the boat went out" made me want to read on.
- "Sixth autumn": sixth since what? I can guess that something big (a war, an exile) began six years ago, and later "six winters" backs that up. This is a small stop, and an acceptable one.

**L7, the headnote.**
- *"I, Seren, who write this Book, held the lamp, and I laid this tale."* I read this twice. Which lamp is meant, the one tonight at the fire or the one in the story? Only later did I realise the lamp belongs to the events of the story. In the headnote it reads as though it is happening tonight.
- *"(We say a tale is laid, like a stone in a wall, because a laid tale stays.)"* This is charming and clear. It is the right amount of explanation.
- *"the old gate-stone by our fire"*: I understood it well enough. It is a stone from an old gate, kept by the fire, and whoever holds it tells the tale. That's fine.
- **The biggest stumble in the whole tale:** *"Tonight Halyna, Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders, stood at the stone with me."* I read "Halyna, Halvard and Rhyna" as a list of **three people**. Then "the husband and wife" covers only two of them, so I wondered who Halyna was. Was Halyna their child? Then line 15 says *"Only Halyna can know carved wood. When they touch it…"*, so I took Halyna to be one person called "they". It wasn't until **line 29** (*"because they're a bonded pair, joined as one since the cradle"*) that I worked out that Halyna is the joint name of Halvard plus Rhyna. That is about 400 words of being wrong about who the main characters are. Please say it in the headnote, for example: "Halyna, which is what we call Halvard and Rhyna together, the husband and wife who…", or "Halyna, the bonded pair Halvard and Rhyna, …". Line 29 can then just remind the reader.
- *"each with a hand beside mine, and we held it like that a long time before any of us spoke"* is lovely. I could picture it.

**L11.** *"Hold the stone, Seren."*
- Who says this? It stands alone with no speaker. I guessed it is a ritual phrase the hearth says to the teller. That's fine once I'd guessed, but the first time it read like a stray line of dialogue. Some small signal would fix it, such as italics, or "Someone said, as someone always does:".

**L13, the Thrones.**
- *"a hill of timber taller than the harbour-houses on our quays"* is a great image to open on.
- Then the exposition arrives all at once: *"The Thrones were ten great ships that came out of the grey, the fog bank at sea, at the end of last winter, and settled in our ten fallen cities, one in each. The people of the wood behind the fog had grown them from the oldest of the black pillars, the great trees at the fog's edge that our grandparents' axemen cut for timber."* That's two sentences with about six new ideas: Thrones, the grey, fallen cities, people of the wood, grown ships, black pillars, grandparents' axemen. I had to slow down and take notes in my head.
  - *"settled in our ten fallen cities"*: did ships come up onto land? "Fallen" to whom, and when? The order of events took me two reads to work out. The cities fell (when?), the ships came last winter, and we took the cities back this year.
  - *"the people of the wood behind the fog"*: nothing tells me these are **the enemy** until "taken our cities back from them" two sentences later. Saying "the enemy" plainly once would help.
  - "Grown" ships are a wonderful idea, but they get lost in the pile.
- *"It was {LAST_THRONE_AT_HAVEN}."* In the raw file this is a placeholder, and I don't know what it was. If it becomes a city name, I'd want a word saying it is one of our ten cities.
- *"even dead it frightened them"*: good. This is the first hint that the ships were somehow alive, and it gave me a chill.

**L15, knowing.**
- *"A heart is the oldest wood at a ship's core, where its orders are carved."* This is clear and intriguing. Someone carves orders into ships.
- *"what its grain holds comes into them whole, like a memory. We call that a knowing."* Clear. I liked it.
- *"All nine hearts had been dead wood."* So what? Were they hoping for something else? It only matters once the green sliver turns up. It's fine as a set-up, but on first read it felt flat.

**L17.** *"the Captain, who leads us … with his hood up."* Good. The hood made me curious in the right way.

**L19–25.** These are excellent: the dripping water, the grey heart, *"a sliver no longer than a hand. / It was green."*, and *"because I didn't trust the light."* I had no trouble here, and this is where the tale caught me.

**L27.** *"we'd searched the wrecks for orders, and the ships' hearts for names, and the fog for sails."* It has a nice rhythm. "Names": whose names? It's a small question, and I let it go. *"Not one of us had ever looked for anything alive"* is a strong line.

**L29, the bonded-pair gloss.**
- *"Since the first winter Halyna had needed only one hand on any wood, because they're a bonded pair, joined as one since the cradle, and what one of them knows, the other knows."* I had to read this twice. It's a long sentence of explanation that lands right at the tensest moment of the scene, just before they touch the sliver. It finally answers who Halyna is, but it does so too late and in the wrong place. If Halyna is explained in the headnote, this could shrink to "Halyna had always needed only one hand. Tonight they each put out a hand."

**L31–33.** The hiss of the drop in the flame, and *"Their two faces changed together."* Excellent.

**L35.** *"It wasn't an order, and it wasn't a wound."*
- **A wound?** Nothing set this up. Were the other knowings wounds? Do the hearts hold injuries? I didn't get the reference. If the nine dead hearts gave "orders and wounds", say so at L15.
- *"as if someone had put the word into her mouth and gone away"* is beautiful.

**L39.** *"Halvard said she was right. But the green wood hadn't given the word alone, and it didn't stop where we stop. At our hearth everyone answers a tale with two words, We remember, and stops there. The green wood went on."*
- I read this twice. "It didn't stop where we stop" comes before I know what "we stop" means, and the explanation only follows a sentence later. Put the custom first and then the turn, for example: "At our hearth everyone answers a tale with two words, *We remember*, and stops there. The green wood didn't stop." That reads in one pass.
- "Halvard said she was right" is slightly odd. Right about what? That the word was "home"?

**L42.** *"We remember our home too."*
- It moved me, but the "too" puzzled me slightly. Too, as in "like you do"? Has the wood heard our tales? I took it to mean the wood answers in our own words, as if to say "you remember yours, we remember ours." That reading works, and it's the emotional heart of the tale. A newcomer gets there, just a beat late.

**L44.** *"Rhyna wept, and Halvard didn't, because she was weeping for them both."* This is lovely, and the bonded-pair idea pays off here.

**L46, the name.**
- *"folded inside the answer the way a seed is folded inside a fruit"*: good.
- *"Naelear, those of the breath. The breath is their word for the grey fog, the breath of their sleeping trees."* This is clear, and the fog as the trees' breath is a strong image.
- On the language: the gloss "those of the breath" was enough for me to follow, but it made me curious which part of *Naelear* means "breath". I'd enjoy a small word-by-word key, something like *nae* (breath) + *lear* (those of). The same goes for *Mystaeri* "from the mist", where I wondered which part is "from". It's not needed for understanding, but it's the sort of detail that makes a made-up language feel real.
- *"I said the name over three times before I wrote it, to hear whether it would hold, and it held."* This is good. It ties back to the stone and wall idea.
- *"Mystaeri … is only the shore's word."* Clear. I understood that we've been calling them by an outsider's name.

**L50.** *"Since the iron came, as the wood calls our axes, the pillars hadn't put up a single leaf."*
- I read it twice. The inserted "as the wood calls our axes" breaks the sentence. Try "Since the iron came (that is the wood's word for our axes)…" or put it in its own sentence.
- *"They had been waiting for their sky to come back, the grey breath they used to hold up between their crowns."* This is a beautiful idea. But how does Seren know it? Presumably from the knowing, but the text doesn't say so. It reads as Seren suddenly knowing the trees' minds. Something like "The knowing held this too:" would fix it.
- "The wood" now means three things: the material (carved wood), the forest, and the people. *"as the wood calls our axes"* means the people. Usually I could tell which, but this is one place where I hesitated.

**L52.** *"So the green sliver was the first growth they'd put up since then. It was seed."*
- I got there on a second read. The ship was grown from a pillar, so a green shoot inside the ship means the pillars are alive again. The text doesn't spell out that step, and "It was seed" felt like a leap, since a sliver of wood isn't a seed. If "seed" means "hope, a future", it lands. If it literally means "the trees can grow again from this", one more clause would help.

**L54, the fisher-girl.**
- *"A fisher-girl of the east keep"*: there's **the Keep** ("our old fortress on the ridge", same sentence) and now **an east keep**. Are there two keeps? Is it a part of the Keep? This confused me.
- *"Since Hesk died this year, she has kept a lamp burning on our north wall, where Hesk lit his mother's lamp every dusk of the war."* **Who is Hesk?** He's never introduced, and his mother's lamp is a whole untold story. I could feel it meant something to the people at the hearth, but to me it was a door that opened onto a room I couldn't see into. It also crowds the girl's introduction, and she matters a lot later on. Either give Hesk one clause ("Hesk, a boy who kept watch on our wall") or cut this down to "she keeps the lamp on our north wall."
- The tense also shifts to "she has kept", which was a small bump.

**L56–62.** *"Home." / "And we two have to find it a boat."* The split speech ("one beginning and the other finishing") is a lovely touch, and the line is moving.

**L66.** *"The sea was always something we built walls against, never a road."* This is great, and the Stonewrights gloss is short and clear.

**L68, Tarnard and Tarnel.**
- *"the boatwrights of our cities had carried their adzes up the mountain, just as the masons carried their chisels"*: up the mountain when? It is explained at the end of the paragraph ("our long march over the mountains"), but I hit the question first.
- **Tarnard / Tarnel**: the names are so alike that I had to check which was the father and which the son.
- *"Tarnel was my cradle-mate, the boy I should have been sealed to as a bonded pair, and he died of the cold beside me on our long march…"* This hit hard, but it's packed into one subordinate clause in a paragraph about boat-building. This is the narrator's own grief, the boy she was meant to be bonded to, and it rushes past. It earns its place later (the knot), but it deserves its own short sentence or two. "Sealed to" was new to me, but I understood it.

**L70, Harl.**
- *"Harl of the burning shore"*: I don't know what the burning shore is. I took it as a place where something terrible happened to him. That's an acceptable mystery.
- The True Men gloss is fine.
- *"Burn it, Harl had said of every plank in the war. This one he set down at Tarnard's feet, and he said nothing."* This was **one of the best moments**. It moved me with no explanation needed.

**L72–74.** *"Wood that knew the sea knows the way home."* and *"now they would speak for us."* Both are good.

**L76, the stem.**
- *"the two planks I had once laid side by side, raw edge to raw edge"*: when, and why? This plainly points back to an earlier tale. Since this is late in the book that's fair, but read on its own I felt shut out.
- *"the plank that said shore, the first word we ever knew from the wood"* is clear and meaningful. *"the plank that was silent for so long"*: did it speak eventually? What did it say? It's a hint I didn't get.
- **{{Orvenna}}, Ormund and Penna**: this is the same three-name list problem as Halyna. By now I'd worked out the pattern, so it was only a speed bump this time. It would trip a reader who missed the Halyna explanation, though.
- *"locked them together with keys, the way masons lock stone"*: I took "keys" to mean wooden pegs or wedges. That's clear enough.
- *"Penna held the last key, and Ormund struck it, once."* / *"The broken grain met, and held."* Both are good.

**L80, the Stonwryt.**
- **Stonwryt vs Stonewrights**: I honestly thought it was a typo at first. Two in-world terms differ by one letter, one meaning the guild and the other the mark, and that made me stop and read the sentence again. If the spelling is deliberate, make the difference obvious in the text, for example "which the guild, in the old spelling, calls the Stonwryt."
- *"only the pair's own hands may cut it, always in stone. No mark of ours had ever gone into wood before."* This is clear, and it gives the moment real weight.
- *"the lamp burned down a finger's width"* is a nice way to show time passing.

**L84–86, the `:::` caption.** *"where the two halves of the first heart are locked together: the mark Halvard once drew in the ash"*
- **"The first heart"**: the prose never told me the two stem planks were halves of a ship's heart, let alone the *first* one. I had just read that they were a plank that said "shore" and a plank that was silent. Now they're "the first heart". This contradicts my picture or adds to it, and I couldn't tell which.
- *"the mark Halvard once drew in the ash"* is a new backstory hint with no context. If the caption shows up next to the prose, it raises questions the prose doesn't answer.

**L82, L88.** *"The wood took it."* / *"Rhyna laid her palms on the cut, and Halvard laid his hands over hers."* Both are beautiful.

**L90–92, the knot.**
- *"the one he taught me on the Road, a knot for every mile"*: "the Road" with a capital R is the long march, I assume. "A knot for every mile" confused me for a second. Is that the knot's name, or did he tie one each mile? The next sentence ("He knotted a net with it as he walked") clears it up.
- *"A net is only knots holding hands."* **This was the emotional high point for me.** The father saying his dead son's saying over the knot the son's would-be partner tied gave me a lump in my throat.

**L94.** Clean and fine.

**L96.**
- *"the whole hearth came down, everyone who gathers each night at this fire for the tales"*: this gloss comes late, because "our hearth" was already used at L39. It's harmless, but it would sit better at the first use.
- *"The old leaned on the young."* Nice.
- *"a Stonwryt coin, part gold and part black stone, struck under a master pair's mark: trust, sealed in stone. It was our dearest gift."* I follow it. "Trust, sealed in stone" is a little abstract, but it's fine.

**L100–102.** *"Let them go with it," / "because we two have cut our last mark."*
- This is ominous. Are they dying, giving up, or retiring? I didn't get it, but it's a **good** mystery. It made me worried for them, which I take to be the intent.
- In the raw file the first line ends with a comma and no speaker tag, so it looked like a broken quotation until I remembered the split-speech pattern.

**L106.**
- *"The soldiers laid down their pikes."* Good.
- *"Kael the Counter, who won't even sit down to tell a tale"*: who is Kael, and what does he count? The joke half lands. I understand he's someone stubborn, so his kneeling means something, but I don't know him well enough to feel it.
- *"Last in the line, with his hood up, the Captain knelt."* Strong.

**L108.** *"He's sitting there now, at the end of the bench. The wet of the shingle still shows dark on his knees."* **This is wonderful.** The jump back to the present at the fire raised the hair on my arms.

**L110, the Guest.**
- *"the Guest, a stranger from behind the fog, knelt like this at a warden's door … set down his best gift. The warden kicked it into the dust, and the Guest was killed on that step."*
- **This is what made the story make sense to me.** We insulted and killed their envoy, so they came for us. Now we kneel with a gift. It reframes everything. It's placed well too, at the moment of kneeling.
- *"Now we were the ones kneeling, with our gift in the bow."* This is a touch on the nose, since I had already made the link, but I wouldn't fight to cut it.
- "Killed on that step": by whom? The passive voice made me wonder. That's fine if it's meant to stay vague.

**L116–118.** *"This is as far as one people can go alone: the gift, and the kneeling." / "The rest needs the other."* Clear, wise and moving.

**L122.** Harl walking the bow into the tide, and the girl up to her waist. Clear and strong. I remembered her from L54, so the callback worked.

**L124.** *"the boat turned. It went into the wind and against the current, into the grey."* This gave me chills. I connected it to "Wood that knew the sea knows the way home" without needing to be told. **This is a hint the text trusts me with, and I got it.**

**L126, Merrick.**
- *"His father was one of three men who came home on a spar, long ago, when a burning grey boat struck two timber-boats at the fog's edge."* This is a whole new backstory at the climax. Grey boat: was it theirs? Did it attack us, or was it an accident? Was it on fire, or did it set our boats on fire? I couldn't work out whose fault it was, and that muddies the clean reversal from the Guest scene just above.
- *"For six winters the ship-masters had turned their faces away from the sea whenever a ship burned."* So ships burned during the war. I understand that.
- *"They did not look away."* This is a good payoff, but the payoff works with **only** the second sentence. The Merrick's-father sentence is where my attention dipped. It's not that I was bored, more that I was overloaded right when I wanted to watch the boat.

**L130.** *"I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me."*
- It reads like the ritual closing line, but Seren *was there*, so who laid it "for her"? This puzzled me. Maybe it means that this is how it happened to her, or that the ritual words always go this way. If it's a set formula, I accept it. It just snagged.

**L132–134, the silence.**
- *"And no one answered."* and then *"In six winters no one had ever said We remember back to the wood."* I read this twice. Two reasons are given for the silence. One is that nobody had ever answered the wood. The other is that they were waiting for Ebba, who used to start the answer. Both are good reasons, but together they slightly blur each other. I also wasn't sure whether "back to the wood" meant to the green sliver, to the wood's people, or to any tale that comes from wood.
- *"the width of one old voice"* is beautiful.
- *"Ebba of Fenholm lies under the east cairns now, the piled-stone graves on the Keep's east face."* Ebba is new to me, but this sentence tells me everything I need. The gloss is short and good.

**L136–140.** *"It was the girl who had gone first into the sea. Old Ebba was her great-grandmother."* / *"We remember."* **This is the second emotional high point.** The child steps into her great-grandmother's place and answers the enemy. It's very moving.

**L142.** *"Then, for the first time, the hearth sang in the wood's way."*
- **"In the wood's way"**: what is the wood's way of singing? I didn't get it. Is it the long run-on third line? Is it singing without stopping, which echoes "it didn't stop where we stop"? If that's the link, it's clever, but I only half-caught it. One clause would let a newcomer in, e.g. "in the wood's way, not stopping at the end."

**L145–149, The Two Homes.**
- It's clear and lovely. *"Stone keeps the promise, and the grain keeps the truth."* and the last line are very good.
- *"We forgot it, and found it, and held it with blood"* points to history I don't know. That's fine in a song.
- The long third line reads breathlessly, which seems deliberate and works if "the wood's way" is meant to point to it.

## Things that stopped me, ranked

1. **Halyna read as a third person** (L7) until L29. This is the worst problem, because it confuses who the protagonists are for the first third of the tale. The same pattern repeats with Orvenna (L76).
2. **"It was {LAST_THRONE_AT_HAVEN}."** (L13) In the raw file this sentence says nothing.
3. **The L13 exposition pile**: six new ideas in two sentences, and the enemy is never plainly called the enemy.
4. **Hesk and "the east keep"** (L54): an unknown person and a second keep, at the moment the key girl is introduced.
5. **"the first heart" / "the mark Halvard once drew in the ash"** in the caption (L85), which don't match anything in the prose.
6. **"it wasn't a wound"** (L35), which nothing sets up.
7. **Stonwryt vs Stonewrights** (L66, L80), which look like a typo.
8. **Merrick's father** (L126), with an unclear "grey boat" whose blame I couldn't place, right at the climax.
9. **"in the wood's way"** (L142), which I didn't understand.
10. Sentences I read twice: L7 "held the lamp", L29 the one-hand sentence, L39 "didn't stop where we stop", L50 "as the wood calls our axes", L130 "laid for me", L134 the double reason for the silence.
11. Minor "who is this?" moments: Kael the Counter, Harl "of the burning shore", the plank "silent for so long", Tarnard/Tarnel being look-alike names.

## Places I felt over-explained

There are only a few, and none were boring:
- L96, "everyone who gathers each night at this fire for the tales", comes after "hearth" was already used without a gloss.
- L110, "Now we were the ones kneeling, with our gift in the bow", spells out a link I'd already made.
- L29: the bonded-pair explanation is needed, but it sits in the wrong place and slows the touch.

Otherwise the short "X, the Y" glosses (the shingle, the ward, the Keep, the Stonewrights, the east cairns) were quick and helpful. I'd keep that style.

## Hints I didn't get (and whether that's fine)

- **Fine as mystery** (I wanted to know more, in a good way): *"we two have cut our last mark"*, the Captain's hood, Harl's burning shore, where the boat went.
- **Not fine** (I just felt lost): "a wound", "the first heart", the plank "silent for so long", Hesk's mother's lamp, "the wood's way", the grey boat in Merrick's story.

## The tale as a whole

**Did I follow it?** Yes, mostly, by the end. In the last enemy ship's dead heart, the bonded pair finds a living green shoot. It gives them the word "home", the line "we remember our home too", and the enemy's true name. The people see that the enemy's trees have been dying since our axes came. They build a boat from the enemy's own planks and fit a stem whose two halves are joined and marked. They put the shoot, their most precious coin and the pair's chisels in the bow. Then they kneel, reversing the old moment when we kicked away a stranger's gift, and send the boat into the fog, where it sails itself against the wind. At the fire nobody can answer until a child does, and then they all sing a song for both peoples' homes.

The first third was harder than it needed to be: the three-name confusion, the dense Thrones paragraph and the placeholder. The middle and end were clear.

**Did it move me?** Yes, genuinely, at several points:
- Harl, who said "burn it" of every plank, setting one down in silence (L70).
- Tarnard saying his dead son's line over the knot (L92). This was the strongest moment for me.
- The Captain's knees still wet at the fire (L108).
- The Guest kneeling then and us kneeling now (L110). This made the whole war make sense.
- The boat turning into the wind (L124).
- Ebba's great-granddaughter answering when no one else could (L136–138).

The emotional core, two peoples who both lost their home and an enemy who answers in our own words, reached me as an outsider, which I think is the point. What held it back were the asides that open doors into rooms I can't see (Hesk, Merrick's father, the plank silent so long, the first heart). Each one costs a little attention right when the tale wants all of it.

## If I could ask for only five changes

1. Explain Halyna as a pair **in the headnote** (and Orvenna as a pair when it first appears), then shorten L29.
2. Split the L13 exposition, call the wood's people "the enemy" once in plain words, and make sure the Throne's city name renders.
3. Cut or shrink Hesk (L54) and Merrick's father (L126). The girl and "They did not look away" work without them.
4. Make the caption's "first heart" match the prose, or say in the prose that the two stem planks were the two halves of a heart.
5. Give the made-up names a tiny word-by-word key (*Naelear*, *Mystaeri*). It isn't needed to follow the tale, but I was curious, and it would make the language feel real.
