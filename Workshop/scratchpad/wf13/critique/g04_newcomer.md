# g04 · Newcomer read (Book Two heading, II.1, II.3)

**How I read it.** I read only `plain/g04.md`, as someone who has never heard of Rivenkeep. I assumed that `{{Name}}` shows up as the plain name, that the `<!-- -->` comments are invisible, and that the `::: native ... :::` block shows a picture of the mark with its caption. The file ends with WRITER'S NOTES, which are for editors. I marked only what the tale text itself did to me. The notes are not reader text, and they reveal things, so they must not ship with the tale.

Line numbers are lines in `plain/g04.md`.

---

## The ten things that stopped me most (worst first)

1. **"{{Halyna}}, Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders"** (lines 11 and 77). I read this as a list of **three people**, and then "husband and wife" told me there were two. I read it three times. I only worked out that Hal-yna is Hal(vard) + (Rh)yna at line 89, when Enno + Della became {{Enrella}}. Line 101 finally says it outright. A comma list cannot hold a pair-name. Something like *Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders (as one pair they have one name, Halyna)* would work, or *Halyna, which is the one name of Halvard and Rhyna, ...*.
2. **Stonewright / Stonwryt** (line 29). The guild and the mark are spelled almost the same, so on first read I took "Stonwryt" for a typo. Nothing on the page tells me the spelling difference is deliberate. One clause would do it, for example *Stonwryt, spelled the old way*, or anything else that shows it isn't a mistake.
3. **"under this stone, the one in my hands. It's that same capstone."** (line 27). There are two problems here.
   - *Scale.* Two lines earlier the capstone was "the great stone across its top" of a gate. Then Seren has it "in my hands". I pictured a girl holding up a gate beam. Line 67 has it right: "Tonight it's **under** my two hands." Use *under* at line 27 too, and maybe put the stone in its niche by the hearth right there.
   - *Spoiler.* Being told straight out that it's the same capstone took the air out of the closing "This is that stone." By the end I already knew, so it only confirmed what I'd been told. "That mark and that vow are under this stone" on its own already made me suspect it, and the suspicion is better.
4. **"long before those two lay as babies in one cradle"** (line 31). I took Halvard and Rhyna for twins or siblings. Then "husband and wife" turned that strange and a little uncomfortable, all the way until II.3 explained cradle-pairs. II.1 needs half a clause here, something like *the cradle the seers gave them*, or a hint that this is a custom and not kinship.
5. **Where is the Keep?** II.1 says "this mountain ridge", "the mountain road", and II.3 has "the mountain lakes" and "our long march over the mountains". Then in II.3 "the enemy's grey ships attack and the wall has to be held" (line 115), and the sea washes wrecks "in under our wall" (line 131). I couldn't picture a mountain fortress that ships attack. One line in II.1 would fix it, for example that the ridge stands over the sea, or the cliff drops into a bay.
6. **"She can't know the wood."** (line 131). This comes out of nowhere. *What* wood? The explanation (the enemy's carved wrecks, the "knowing") arrives after it, in the middle of a paragraph that's really about Seren. The knowing is the strangest and most interesting idea in the whole group, and it gets the least room. It's also unclear what is carved: words? pictures? Put the explanation first, then "She can't."
7. **The seers "read what was written there before we were born"** (line 95). Written *where*? On the baby's skin? In the cradle? In the air? Line 101 then speaks of "the mark the seers read at the cradle", so I started to think babies are born with a visible mark. I couldn't tell whether that was meant.
8. **"People say eight days is the longest anyone has ever lived on alone. Hours, say the Stonewrights"** (line 125). Which is it? I had to work out that this means *people say eight days, but our records say only hours*. Write it that way: *People say eight days. Our count says hours.*
9. **"Sometimes one baby of a cradle-pair died before the sealing ... the other baby lived"** (line 127), and then Seren's case: she was about twelve when Tarnel died on the Road. *Baby* made me think this only happens in infancy, so for a moment Seren's story didn't seem to fit the rule. Use *child*.
10. **Explanations stacked mid-sentence.** On first read I lost the verb in "the founders laid the Keep's slates, the writings they had cut, and its first staff, the rod carried by the overseer of a great work, in the black chest" (line 33). The same thing happened with "At dusk on the day of the banner, the evening we reached the Keep and our Captain tore his cloak in two to make our banner, Halvard and Rhyna came in" (line 49). The explanations are useful, but they sit between the subject and the rest of the sentence. Split them off into a sentence of their own.

## Paragraph by paragraph

### BOOK TWO heading (lines 3–5)
- *"Who we were, and who they were."* I'm fine with "we". "They" could be the founders, the enemy, or someone I haven't met. As a teaser it works, and I didn't need it answered here.

### II.1 · Of the Keep That Was Forgotten

**Line 9 (when-line).** "Told in the first summer of **the siege**." What siege? Who is besieging us? Nothing in II.1 says. (II.3's "the holding" and the grey ships let me guess, too late.) Half a clause would do: *the siege, when the grey ships came against our wall*.

**Line 11 (Seren's headnote).**
- "Laid by me, Seren **Two-Inks**, who **write** this Book." "Who write" reads like a typo to a modern ear. Use *I, Seren Two-Inks, write this Book* or *who writes*.
- "Two-Inks": why? I didn't get it. By the end of II.3 I suspected it ties to her having only one name (two inks standing in for two names? her own words and other people's?). I like the mystery, but I couldn't solve it from these tales.
- The parenthesis "(Here, whoever holds the stone tells the tale, and we say a tale is 'laid,' ...)" is helpful. But "the stone" appears here for the first time, and I don't know *which* stone yet. "Here" = where? The parenthesis also sounds like a footnote, not Seren talking.
- Halyna as three names: see No. 1 above.
- Why can't Seren read the founders' writing herself? II.3 answers this nicely ("the old hand" is taught to pairs). That's a real payoff, and it worked on me.

**Line 13.** "Hold the stone, Seren." **Who is speaking?** It's a nice ritual feel, but nothing tells me whose voice it is: the hearth, Halyna, or Seren talking to herself.

**Line 15.** "It tells how the founders kept it, and how we didn't." Good line. "We" is slightly slippery, though: our people over the centuries, not the refugees who just arrived. I got there, but only after a moment.

**Line 17.**
- "Its first page is a slate". The first page of *the history* is a slate? So is this Book partly made of stone slates, or is she copying one? I read it twice.
- "{{Aldwena}} were a bonded pair, two builders **joined as one from birth**." Joined how? Physically? It briefly made me think of conjoined twins. II.3 makes clear it's a God-made bond, but II.1 leaves "joined from birth" literal.
- "letters ... no bigger than grains of barley". Lovely.

**Lines 19–25 (the founders' page).** This is the strongest writing in II.1. It's clear, and it sounds old without being hard. One small hitch: "left the stone below it for his sons". I only understood it meant *blank space for his sons' names* at line 31. "It stands." at the end is great.

**Line 27.**
- In my hands / same capstone: see No. 3.
- "I can't turn it over, because it takes two." Heavy, or forbidden? I couldn't tell, and here I think the doubt is good.
- "slipped my hand underneath it and traced their mark with one finger". This moved me.

**Line 29.**
- Stonewright/Stonwryt: see No. 2.
- The coins: "No gold can buy one. Still, the master pairs ... stamped their marks on coins". I had to think about why "Still". (The point is that a mark can't be bought, but it can make money trusted.) It's an interesting idea, but it's a side road in the middle of a paragraph that's really about Seren having no mark. **Why does it matter here?** I didn't know, and it slowed me down.
- "I have no mark. ... I'm not one of a pair. I'll never cut one." This hit hard, and it made me want to know why. Good hook.

**Line 31.**
- "below the last name the stone is smooth". A hint I *felt* but didn't get. Did the line end? Is Halvard the last? Is there no son to come? I liked not being told.
- "Rhyna **of the Inner Gate** comes from {{Aldwena}}'s." Nice. The byname ties to the gate Aldwena laid. I caught that one.
- Babies in one cradle: see No. 4.

**Line 33.**
- "Then the people went down to the warm harbours". **Why** did they leave? Nothing says. I'm fine with that as a mystery, but it goes by very fast.
- The stacked sentence and the staff explanation: see No. 10. The explanation "the rod carried by the overseer of a great work" reads like a dictionary entry, and I wasn't told why the staff matters.
- "the black chest". "The" acts as if I already know it.
- "Eldhythe, which people call the Eldest. But it's only the eldest of the havens. The Keep is older." Good. I followed it, and the last line is earned.

**Lines 35–37.** "the wind blew in under a gate that nobody guarded any more" and "It had only been left behind." These are lovely. Nothing stopped me.

**Line 39.** "*point* its mortar ... I love that word." Seren's voice comes through here, and I liked her. "After the rain came the frost, and after the frost, the ice." Good.

**Lines 41–43.** "No one heard it fall." Strong. Nothing stopped me.

**Line 45.** Clear and strong. The Fall is explained well: grey ships, fog, twenty months, ten cities. I wanted to know who the grey ships are, in a good way.

**Line 47.** "Forgetting had done to it what no enemy ever could." Slightly on the nose after lines 37 and 45 already made the point, but it lands.

**Line 49.**
- The banner explanation sits mid-sentence: see No. 10.
- "our Captain". Who? Never named, and that's fine. Why tear his cloak? I liked the image.
- "I was twelve, and I didn't know their names." Moving. It also quietly set up II.3, which I only saw afterwards.

**Line 51.**
- "swept the snow off it". Snow, in a tale told in summer, about an arrival I'd assumed was recent. Fine once I understood they arrived in winter, but I wasn't told the season of arrival until "the night of the pass, the coldest night" in II.3.
- "the bloom had grown, the pale crust ... and it was as pale as frost on a gatepost". The explanation interrupts the image, and "pale" comes twice in one sentence.

**Line 53.** "the way the fishermen of **Tidesmeet** turn over a boat they gave up for lost". Unknown place, but the simile works without it. In II.3 I learned Tarnel was a boatwright's son from Tidesmeet, and the image got heavier. Very good.

**Line 55.** "It was their own." Perfect. I got a small chill.

**Line 57.** Over-explains. "Rhyna's own forebears ... Halvard's own forefathers ... his fathers had left". That's "own" three times, and line 31 already told me all of it. The two gestures (her fingers on the mark, his thumb in the chisel-bite) say it better than the explanations. Trust them.

**Line 59.**
- "Out in the ward, the courtyard inside the walls". The explanation is fine.
- "Their feet made no sound on the snow." Beautiful.

**Line 61.** "The stone had kept its vow. We were the ones who didn't keep ours." The emotional center of the tale. It landed.

**Line 63.**
- "Halvard and Rhyna's **cry**". What cry? Grief, joy, a call to work? I couldn't hear it.
- "sitting in the **grass** with their chisels in their laps". Grass, two lines after snow in the gateway. The picture clashed.
- *Why* were they sitting idle? I guessed despair, and I think that's right, but it's a hint I had to reach for.

**Line 65.**
- "the ceremony that made the Captain our Commander". What's the difference between Captain and Commander, and why does it matter? It didn't stop me for long.
- **Why a *new* capstone** if "The stone was whole"? I guessed: out of respect they kept the old one. Half a clause would settle it.
- "The True Men, the Captain's own company of twelve". Fine.
- "set it in the niche in the wall beside the hearth". This is when I finally understood what "holding the stone" means physically. I wanted that picture back at line 11 or 27.

**Line 67.** "the shame of their children, who forgot it. Tonight it's under my two hands." Strong close, though No. 3 took some of it away.

**Lines 69–71.** The closing words and "We remember." are clear and work as a ritual.

### II.3 · Of the Bonded and the Theoliths

**Line 75.** "on the night of a sealing, our guild's wedding of a bonded pair". A clear explanation that I needed.

**Line 77.**
- "{{Idrenna}}, the oldest pair in our guild". Because of II.1, I now understand that one name can be two people. Good.
- Halyna as three names again: see No. 1.
- "because children like to know". Charming.

**Lines 81–85.** "We hold the stone together," / "the way we hold everything." These are two quoted lines with no speaker, right after Seren says she'll record whose words are whose. Did he say the first and she the second? Did they say both together? I wasn't sure. I suspect it's meant as one sentence split between them, and if so, show it: *he began*, *she finished*.

**Line 89.**
- "where the fire can see your faces". Lovely.
- "kneel down as two and stand up as one. Now they are {{Enrella}}". **This is where the pair-names finally clicked for me** (Enno + Della). It's a good moment, but it should not be the first time I understand Halyna.

**Line 91.**
- "with four hands and no excuses". Funny, and I liked them at once.
- The joke about cutting the mark under the first stone made sense, but the custom is explained inside the same sentence as the joke, so "The whole hearth laughed" got a nod from me, not a laugh.
- "a house with **one door**". Does one door mean something (one door for one pair)? A hint I may have missed.
- "asked when I would be sealed. I had no answer, and I went on writing." Quietly devastating. It set up the end well.

**Line 93.**
- "Back when our people were **still** called Shorelanders". Then what are they called now? The "still" raises a question the tale never answers.
- "not every Shorelander was a Stonewright, but every Stonewright was a Shorelander". I had to read it twice, and I wasn't sure why it matters right here.
- "a jailer's house in **Carnhold**". Why a jailer? I took it as *even the lowest home*, but I wasn't sure jailers were lowly. Is this someone I'll meet later? A hint I didn't get.
- "because the guild looked after the faith of our land as carefully as it looked after its walls". This was a surprise: builders who also keep the faith? It's an interesting idea, but it arrives as a reason (*because*) for something it doesn't obviously explain. I read it twice. I also briefly worried that families *give up* their babies, until line 95 showed the children stay at home.

**Line 95.**
- "God made each pair one before either of them drew breath". Clear.
- "read what was written there". Written where? See No. 7.

- "at his mother's house and at mine by turns ... never spent a night under different roofs". Lovely, specific, believable.

**Line 97.**
- "Theoliths: the old master pair who raise a young pair and teach them". Clear. But the title promises "the Theoliths", and they get one sentence plus a nod. As a reader I expected more of them. (The nod payoff is great, though.)
- "keep the guild's **count of things** in the old hand". Count of things = records? An odd phrase. It comes back later as "we're the ones who keep the count", so I suppose it's a term. It wasn't quite clear.
- "the Covenant of Completion, and nothing more, and we'll tell you nothing more either". A tease, and it worked: I want to know.

**Line 101.**

- "We call that mark the Stonwryt." Repeated from II.1. That's fine, since the tales may be read apart.

Read right after II.1, this is the third time I've been told what the Rite and the capstone are. I skimmed it. (If tales are read separately, keep it but cut it down.) **Lines 107–109 (the picture of the mark).** The caption says "the ash of **the Long Hearth**". This is the first time the Long Hearth is named. I assumed it's the hall's hearth, which is fine.

**Line 111.** "No child moved. In the fire a coal settled." Perfect.

## Tale by tale

**II.1 · Of the Keep That Was Forgotten.**
*Did I follow it?* Yes. The shape is clear: founders build and vow, people leave, the Keep is forgotten and decays, the cities fall, the refugees arrive, the stone is turned over, the vow is found whole, and the stone now sits by the hearth. I lost the thread for a moment four times: the three-names Halyna, the Stonwryt spelling, the babies in one cradle, and the "great stone" held "in my hands".
*Did it move me?* Yes, in the second half. The turning of the stone ("It was their own"), "Their feet made no sound on the snow", and "The stone had kept its vow. We were the ones who didn't keep ours." all landed. The first half is slower because explanations pile up mid-sentence, and the early "It's that same capstone" takes away the surprise of the ending.

**II.3 · Of the Bonded and the Theoliths.**
*Did I follow it?* Yes, and more easily than II.1. Two voices speaking in turn, with the children as an audience, is a natural way to teach lore. The pair-name finally clicked here. Stumbles: "read what was written there", eight days vs. hours, "baby" for a twelve-year-old, the mountain-vs-sea geography, and "She can't know the wood".
*Did it move me?* Very much. Enno and Della's "four hands and no excuses", the boy running out of fingers, "a single name is a mourning", Tarnel under the cloak, Halvard saying her pen hasn't stopped when it had, and Idrenna finally nodding. It's the best piece in the group. It also turned II.1's "I have no mark" into something I felt after the fact.

**Overall.** The two tales work as a pair: II.1 plants Seren's loneliness and II.3 explains it. The single fix that would help a newcomer most is to introduce Halyna as *one name for two people* the first time it appears (line 11). Next would be to make the Keep's place on the sea clear, and to hold back "It's that same capstone".
