# g05 · Newcomer read (Book Three heading, III.1 Of Their Raising, III.2 Of Their Fall)

I read this as a modern reader who has never heard of Rivenkeep. I judged only the story text, from the BOOK THREE heading down to the last "We remember." I did not use the WRITER'S NOTES at the bottom of the file for anything. Line numbers refer to `plain/g05.md`.

Legend: **STOP** means I didn't understand it or had to go back. **TWICE** means I had to read the sentence twice. **HINT?** means I could tell something was being set up but couldn't tell what. **WHO/WHY?** means a name or detail turned up without enough reason to care. **BORED** means it was explained more than I needed. **LANDED** means it worked on me (listed so it doesn't get cut).

---

## BOOK THREE heading and Argument (lines 3–5)

- *"Ten havens, the cities of our coast, and ten men to tell them: what we had, and how we walked away from it."* Clear, and a good hook. **LANDED:** "how we walked away from it" made me ask why they walked and didn't fight. The book answers that much later with the Stonewrights, which is a good long payoff.

## III.1 · Of Their Raising

**When-line (line 9).** Clear. "this old mountain fortress, the Keep, against the enemy's fleets" did plant a question I carried through both tales, though. **STOP:** how do *fleets* besiege a *mountain* fortress? See line 47 below, where this gets worse.

**Headnote (line 11).** This is the hardest paragraph in the file, and it comes before the story starts. Before anyone has said anything human, I've been handed: the True Men, the Captain, Kael the Counter, the old gate-stone, "this hearth", the torn-cloak banner, Halyna, the chest, and Seren. I nearly bounced off it.
- **STOP:** `{{Halyna}}`. The braces show up literally on the page and look like broken template code. Even without them, "Halyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders" gives one name to two people. I stopped and wondered whether it was a typo, a family name, or a title. If it's a pair-name, I need half a clause saying so (for example "Halyna, as we call the husband and wife…").
- **WHO/WHY?:** "Kael the Counter". Counter of what? Nothing here says, and it's never explained in III.1. (It pays off beautifully at the end of III.2, so I don't want it explained. But the tag sits there unused for a whole tale.)
- **WHO/WHY?:** "the old gate-stone". The gate of what? Why does the teller have to hold it? I could live without knowing, but it's one more unexplained object in a paragraph that's already full of them.
- **TWICE:** *"Its own man stood to answer, as the True Men had answered on our first evening here, when the Captain raised his torn cloak over the wall as our banner."* That's three time-layers in one sentence: tonight, the first evening, and the banner moment.
- **HINT?:** "the chest I carried up the mountain". Why did Seren carry a chest? It felt like a planted mystery. That's fine, but I noticed it.
- Small stumble: "the chest **I** carried" comes one sentence *before* "I, Seren, who write this Book". For a moment I didn't know who "I" was.
- **LANDED:** *"(We say a tale is laid, like a stone in a wall, because a laid tale stays.)"* Nice, and it fits a builders' people.
- Small logic snag: "The True Men … laid this tale, each man for his own haven", but there are twelve of them and ten havens. The tale itself sorts this out at the end (Stannard, Kael), so it's minor.

**Line 13.** *"Give me the stone. No, I'll stand. When the roll is called, men stand."* Good voice. Nothing marks the speaker, but I worked out it was Kael within a line, since the italic narrator just said he held the stone.

**Line 15.** **LANDED:** *"Not one hearth on it is sending up smoke."* This was the first moment I felt anything, and it's strong.

**Line 17.** **BORED:** "the havens, our ten cities on that coast". This is at least the fourth time I've been told that haven means a city on the coast (Argument, when-line, headnote, here). By now I have it.

**Line 19.** Clear. **LANDED:** "when his city comes home" is a lovely phrase. The gloss right after it ("when we've taken it back") is fine once, but it does flatten the phrase a little. Small question: Kael now has two titles, the Counter and the oath-keeper. I assumed they were different jobs.

**Line 21 (Seren reads the rolls).**
- **LANDED:** "they paved roads across the mountains like veins" and *"The clerks' handwriting is small and patient, as if they hated to waste a page."*
- **STOP (mild):** "They were afraid of the open water", and yet they founded ten *harbour* towns? I wanted half a sentence on that tension, or else to be told it's the point.
- **BORED / voice break:** *"A course is one row of stones in a wall. A staff is the polished rod carried by whoever oversees a great work of the guild."* This reads like a glossary dropped into the scene. The information is useful, but it would land better worked into Seren's own sentence (for example "…the first course, the bottom row of stones, was laid…").
- **HINT?** (a good one): *"Ten havens, and ten staffs."* I could tell the staffs would matter and I kept it in mind. It pays off in III.2.

**Eldhythe (lines 23–27).**
- Clear. "harbour-houses" is glossed well.
- **TWICE:** *"as he does on the wall at every holding, the hours when the enemy attacks and the wall must be held."* The appositive is awkward. "at every holding" followed by "the hours when…" made me parse it twice.
- **STOP (a jolt, maybe intended):** "his gunners". This is the first time guns exist, in a world of stonewrights, tapers and cranes. It's fine, but it surprised me, and it slips in as an aside. It might deserve its own beat, or an earlier mention ("the guns on our wall").
- **LANDED:** *"Wait. The sea hasn't finished."* I didn't know exactly what it meant, and I liked that. The young men laughing in a windless hall is a good human touch.

**Tidesmeet (lines 29–35).** Clear. **HINT?** (good): Tamm's three stones by his blanket every night. I guessed they were from the bridges. **LANDED:** *"One span is just a plank. Three make a city."*

**Fenholm (lines 37–41).** One of the best. **LANDED:** "A fen-man who can't laugh at that had better go and live on a hill", and above all *"That's her there by the fire, nodding."* Learning that the grandmother is alive and sitting in the room was a real delight. "I'm to lay her stone" is a bit stiff, but okay.

**Holtward (lines 43–49).**
- **STOP:** the heading says "the keep under the canopy". With the Keep being the mountain fortress, a lowercase "keep" made me wonder for a second whether Holtward *was* the Keep. A different word (hold, town, walls) would avoid the clash.
- **TWICE:** *"lay so deep among the trees … that a traveller could walk right underneath it and never see a wall."* "Among the trees" puts it on the ground. "Walk right underneath it" puts it up in the branches. Which is it? I couldn't picture the town.
- **STOP (big, see the when-line):** *"planks from the enemy's wrecked ships, which the sea washes in on the beach under our wall. Wood is scarce up here on the ridge."* So the Keep has a beach under its wall? I had pictured a mountain fortress you reach after a month in the passes (III.2 confirms that later), with the old coast visible far off "all the way out to the haze." Now the sea is right below the wall. I couldn't build a map. One clause early on, saying where the Keep sits in relation to the sea and to the old coast, would fix this for the whole book.
- **LANDED:** Aske touching every plank. *"These were trees once."* This is lovely and needs no help.

**Carnhold (lines 51–53).** **LANDED:** "the way a mother listens to her child's cough", *"A gun that rings true shoots true"*, and the anvil oath. This is the best oath in the tale.

**Emberhythe (lines 55–57).**
- **LANDED:** "our children ran barefoot in the snow."
- **BORED / deflating:** *"And I'll burn the grey, the great fog bank at sea that the enemy's ships come out of, and the sea too, if it would catch fire."* This is Harl's angry oath, and the gloss interrupts it right in the middle. It also tucks a *big* fact about the world (the enemy comes out of a fog bank) into an appositive inside someone's oath. I'd rather learn it plainly somewhere else, so this line can stay hot: "I'll burn the grey, and the sea too, if it would catch fire."
- Small point: the heading calls Emberhythe "the forge on the burning shore", but Carnhold was just the forge town. Two "forges" in a row blurred them a little for me.

**Sandreach (lines 59–61).** Clear and good. **LANDED:** "We called it a haven all the same, because it sheltered us," and sleeping with "the whole mountain for my roof."

**Rimewatch (lines 63–65).**
- **TWICE:** *"all winter long its great hall stood out on the ice."* Did they build the hall on the ice each winter, or did it stand on posts in the lake so the ice froze around it? I couldn't picture it. Five words would do it.
- **LANDED:** the taper going window to window, and *"One of them is burning on our north wall tonight."* That's a good plant.

**Highreach (lines 67–69).** **LANDED:** "the stones hung on the wind the way hawks hang over a field." This is the weakest oath of the ten, though. "Whistle for the wind" doesn't give me a thing he'll do the way Garvel's anvil or Hesk's lamp does.

**Glasspire (lines 71–75).** **LANDED:** "caught the sun like a blade of ice", and "threw the sunlight a day's sail out to sea, into the grey" (they shone a light *into* the fog, which is haunting). **HINT?** (good): "What I carried of it", so the glass broke. **LANDED:** Pellow holding the glass up to his supper, and *"Look close."*

**Line 77.** **STOP:** *"Ten cities, ten harbours."* Ulden *just* told me Sandreach had no harbour. Is Kael being careless, being proud, or is it a mistake? I couldn't tell, so it read as a mistake. Also, "ten peoples who called themselves one nation" is the first mention of a nation, and it has no name. I wanted the name.

**Line 79.** **LANDED:** Stannard knocking on the hearthstone. **WHO/WHY?** (small): "Stannard of the ridge … the ridge … was never a haven." So people lived on this mountain before the column came? Was the Keep empty? This made me curious more than lost.

**Lines 81–83.** **LANDED, strongly:** *"I have no haven. / The Captain found me on a road."* This is the emotional peak of III.1, and it worked on me completely. (Question I carried forward: is "a road" *the* Road from III.2? I couldn't tell, and I liked wondering.)

**Line 85.** **STOP:** *"I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me."* This is supposed to be the *first* telling. Each man spoke for himself tonight, and Seren read out what "no one alive remembered." So who laid it *for* Kael? I took it as a fixed closing formula in the end, but on first meeting it sounded false. If it's a ritual line, one word in the headnote would let me hear it that way.

**Line 87.** **LANDED:** "We remember." It's a good ritual close.

## III.2 · Of Their Fall

**When-line (line 91).**
- **TWICE:** *"in the year of the Second Tide, as the wall named that year's enemy fleets."* I first read "the wall" as the stone wall naming something. "As the men on the wall named…" or "as the wall-crews called…" would fix it. I also wondered what the *First* Tide was.
- The rest is clear: twenty months, and the Road.

**Headnote (line 93).**
- **BORED:** this repeats III.1's frame almost word for word: True Men, "company of twelve", the *same* laid parenthesis, the gate-stone, "I, Seren, who write this Book." If I'm reading the book in order, this is the dullest paragraph in the file. I understand why it's there for someone who opens III.2 first, but I'd cut at least the laid parenthesis the second time.
- **LANDED:** *"The guns were still cooling from the day's fighting, and the mortar of the day's building hadn't set."* This is the best sentence in either headnote. It's specific and it's *now*.
- **WHO/WHY?** (small): "spoke for the guild". Which guild? I knew from III.1 (the Stonewrights), but III.2 doesn't say it until line 171.

**Line 95.** Good echo of III.1's opening. **LANDED:** "This isn't a tale to sit down for."

**Line 97.** **TWICE:** *"don't look away, because we didn't look away in the telling."* "Didn't … in the telling" mixes the tenses. Do they mean when it happened, or now as they tell it?

**Line 99.**
- "This was the last spring of peace" starts abruptly ("This" what?). "It was" reads more easily.
- **BORED:** the banner gets glossed a third time ("the day of the banner, when we reached this Keep and the Captain raised his torn cloak over the wall").
- **LANDED:** swallows in the gate-arches, "walls were built for feast days and for weather", and gates open at night. This is excellent and does a lot with very little.

**Line 101.**
- The "grey" gloss appears again (second time), but I'll allow it.
- **TWICE:** *"No messenger came with words that anyone could understand."* Did a messenger come and speak gibberish, or did no messenger come at all? It reads both ways. If it means none came, "No messenger came. No words came." is plainer.
- **LANDED, strongly:** *"We looked for a captain on those ships, and a crew, even one man at one rope. No one stood on the decks. I swear to that."* This is chilling.

**Line 103.** **LANDED:** *"A man has to call his death by some name."* "Mystaeri, after the mist" is clear. Pronunciation (mis-TAIR-ee?) is a small question, not a stop.

**Line 105.** **HINT?:** "Those sails were simpler than the ones that come against us now." Simpler in what way? I wanted to know, and I think that's intended. **LANDED:** "none had any help to send."

**Line 107.** The reminder of Corlen's saying is useful for a cold reader. I'd read it a few minutes earlier, so it was slightly redundant, but it sets up line 115 perfectly, so keep it.

**Eldhythe (lines 109–117).**
- **TWICE (mild):** *"the wind laid the smoke across the city, so the city never saw its harbour go"*. The harbour burned from dawn to dusk and the city didn't see it? I think the smoke hid it. I got there, slowly.
- **LANDED, strongly:** *"On its last evening the tide came in over the ashes, slowly, the way it always came, and the sea hadn't finished."* His comfort line turns into a threat. This is superb.
- **LANDED:** "Then he waited, the way he waits at his guns, and the hall waited with him."
- **HINT?** (got it, and it hurt): *"It was the last time he held it."* I understood that Corlen dies before the next telling. It's a small sentence that does a lot. Don't explain it.

**Tidesmeet (lines 119–121).**
- **LANDED:** "The upper one went, and then the lower. The middle one stood longest. We waited for it, and it went."
- **BORED (mild):** *"Those are the three I set out by my blanket every night."* I had already made that click myself from III.1 the moment he said he took a stone from each, and having it spelled out took the click away from me. Consider ending at "took a stone from each of them."
- Small logic snag: the spans "go down into the water", so how does he take a stone from *each* that night? From the rubble on the banks? I let it go.

**Fenholm (lines 123–125).** **LANDED:** "as if the fen … had only been waiting", and "That night nobody laughed." "It was the stone from the top course of our house" repeats III.1 a bit, but it's short. Ebba refusing to hand over the stone until the town was gone is great.

**Holtward (lines 127–129).** **LANDED:** *"Its bark was as warm as a living hand."* This is short and perfect.

**Carnhold (lines 131–135).** **LANDED, strongly:** *"Then it did not ring."* Giving it its own line is right.

**Emberhythe (lines 137–139).**
- Clear. **LANDED:** sleeping warm on the bare ground. I didn't need "because the ground there was always warm", since III.1 already told me, but it's harmless.
- **HINT? (didn't get it):** *"one of our masons carried a charred plank out in his pack. I told him to burn it."* Why? Was it Harl's temper, grief, or a link to the wreck-wood they burn on the wall now? I couldn't tell whether I was *supposed* to understand. If it's meant as a mystery, fine, but right now it reads like a dropped thread.
- Timeline wobble: refugees from five fallen havens gather at Emberhythe and winter there. I followed it, but I had to stop and count which havens had fallen by then.

**Sandreach (lines 141–143).**
- **LANDED:** hearing the gallery above go "before any runner came down to tell us", and *"Every night since, I've slept on the open ground, with my ear to it."*
- **STOP (a productive one):** *"they took it from us one gallery at a time."* Who are "they"? Line 101 told me no one stands on the decks. A canyon city with no harbour, taken room by room, by ships with no crews? That made me deeply curious about the enemy. If it's meant to raise that question, it works. If not, it's a contradiction.

**Rimewatch (lines 145–157).** This is the best passage in the file, and the one that moved me most.
- **LANDED:** "The fire went from window to window, the way my mother's taper used to go at dusk." *"It burned the way it had always shone."*
- **LANDED:** "I went into the smoke, to her window, and took down her lamp. It was lit." (Where was his mother? The silence about her is devastating. Keep it silent.)
- **LANDED:** the ice "spoke under me, cracking far off and then near", cupping the flame, and "In front of me there was no light but the one in my hand." / "The ice held."
- **LANDED:** *"Someone has to keep a light."*
- Tiny stumble: "the night-watch steer by it". Watchmen on a wall don't *steer*. I can see it echoes the fishers, and maybe that's enough.

**Highreach (lines 159–161).** **LANDED:** *"there was nothing to do with that day but leave."* That's quietly brutal. "Behind us the cranes stood burning on the crags."

**Glasspire (lines 163–169).**
- **LANDED:** "the glass coming down around me in pieces, and the snow coming down with it," and *"I looked close. I saw nothing."* Turning "Look close" around like this gave me chills.
- **HINT?** (good): the shadow "bigger than any ship", and "The dark hole beneath it is still open." I wanted more, which is good.
- **BORED / deflating:** *"Then Pellow, who holds his far-glass, his spyglass, up to everything, sat down with it in his lap, and didn't hold it up."* This is a silent, heartbreaking beat, and "his spyglass" interrupts it. I know what a far-glass is from III.1 (and could guess anyway). Cut "his spyglass" here.

**The Stonewrights walk out (lines 171–177).**
- **LANDED:** cleaning the trowels "as they did after every day's work" and wrapping them "for the road".
- **LANDED (morally):** *"The Stonewrights did not fight. They simply walked, and I was among them."* This finally answers the Argument's "how we walked away from it." I couldn't tell whether Seren is ashamed or proud, and I think that uncertainty is the point. It stuck with me.
- **TWICE:** the bank-the-fire simile, *"It was the way a woman banks the fire on her last night in her mother's house, heaping ash over the coals to keep them till morning, the same as every night."* On first read I thought: why keep coals till morning if she's leaving? On the second read I got it. It's the habit of care, done at an ending. It's worth the second read, but the sentence is long. Maybe split it: "…on her last night in her mother's house. She heaps ash over the coals to keep them till morning, the same as every night."
- **LANDED:** *"A staff is cut for its work, and never leaves its work."* and *"There were ten staffs, and not one came up the mountain."* The "ten staffs" plant from III.1 pays off. **WHY?** (a good one): I sensed it matters for the Keep (does the Keep have no staff, so its work isn't a true work?), but I couldn't say how. That's a fine thing to carry out of a tale.

**The Road (lines 179–191).**
- Clear.
- **LANDED, strongly:** *"Some of the old died with them. They had given the children their cloaks."* This is the hardest line in the book so far.
- **LANDED:** "We got up out of it, as many of us as could get up, and walked."
- **LANDED, strongly:** *"At the foot of the passes I counted us, and I counted us again at the top. I won't tell you the difference."* "Kael the Counter" from III.1 finally clicked here. It's a great payoff, and it's why I wouldn't explain "the Counter" earlier.
- Line 189: "the way it was laid for me" still puzzles me (see III.1, line 85).
- **WHO/WHY?:** the Captain is absent from the Road. I've now heard about him in both tales (the torn cloak, finding Kael on a road) and know nothing else about him. That's fine for these two tales, but by the end of III.2 I wanted *one* concrete detail about him.

## Tale by tale: did I follow it, and did it move me?

**BOOK THREE heading and Argument.** Followed it, yes. Moved, a little. It's a good promise, and "walked away" is the hook.

**III.1 · Of Their Raising.** *Followed it?* Yes, once I got past the headnote. The roll-call structure (Kael calls the city, a bold heading, then "X of the Y" and the oath) is very easy to track, and naming each man at the start of his part solved the "who's speaking" problem neatly. What actually confused me:
1. The headnote's pile of unexplained proper nouns, and the literal `{{Halyna}}`.
2. Where the Keep is: a mountain fortress a month up the passes, besieged by *fleets*, with a *beach* under its wall.
3. Small contradictions: "ten harbours" after Sandreach has none, a lowercase "keep" for Holtward, Holtward above or among the trees, the Rimewatch hall on the ice.
4. "the way it was laid for me" at a first telling.

*Moved?* Yes, steadily if not overwhelmingly. Ten portraits in a row could have felt like a catalogue, but almost every one has a living object or gesture (Ebba nodding, Aske's hand on the planks, Garvel's anvil, Pellow's glass at supper). Kael's "I have no haven. / The Captain found me on a road." is a real ending. Highreach is the flattest portrait. The glosses jutting into oaths (Harl's fog bank) and the course/staff definitions are where it felt most like a textbook.

**III.2 · Of Their Fall.** *Followed it?* Yes, completely. It's the clearer of the two tales. The order (Eldhythe to Glasspire), the column gathering, the guild walking out and the Road all read cleanly. The only real stops were "as the wall named", "words that anyone could understand", the banking-fire simile, and "who took Sandreach if the ships had no crews?" (which I think is a good question to leave me with).

*Moved?* Strongly. This is the best thing in the file. Its power comes from paying off III.1 detail by detail: the sea hadn't finished, the leaves on the wall-walk, the ear to the floor, the taper, the anvil going quiet, "Look close", the ten staffs, Kael the Counter. Rimewatch and the Road made me stop reading for a moment. One caution: much of that power depends on having read III.1 first. The headnote and reminders keep a cold reader *oriented*, but they can't give a cold reader the echoes. That suggests the reminders could be lighter for anyone reading in order, especially the repeated headnote and "Those are the three I set out by my blanket."

## The handful of fixes I'd make first

1. **`{{Halyna}}`** (line 11): it shows up as literal braces, and one name for "the husband and wife" reads as an error. Render it, and say in half a clause that it's the name the two go by.
2. **Where the Keep sits** (lines 9, 15, 47): add one early clause that reconciles "mountain fortress, a month up the passes", "the enemy's fleets", "the beach under our wall" and "you can still see our coast … out to the haze." This is the biggest picture problem for an outsider.
3. **Pull glosses out of emotional lines:** Harl's fog-bank appositive (line 57), "his spyglass" in Pellow's silent beat (line 169), and the course/staff dictionary sentences (line 21). Give "the grey = the fog the enemy comes from" its own plain sentence somewhere neutral.
4. **Trim the repeats for in-order readers:** the haven gloss (four or more times in III.1), the banner gloss (three times), and III.2's headnote echoing III.1's (cut the laid parenthesis the second time). Also cut "Those are the three I set out by my blanket every night."
5. **Untangle five sentences:** "at every holding, the hours when…" (line 27), "as the wall named that year's enemy fleets" (line 91), "we didn't look away in the telling" (line 97), "No messenger came with words that anyone could understand" (line 101), and the banking-fire simile (line 175, which could be split).
6. **Decide on or fix the small contradictions:** "ten harbours" after Sandreach (line 77), Holtward's lowercase "keep" and "underneath it" (lines 43–45), the Rimewatch hall "out on the ice" (line 65), and "the way it was laid for me" at what reads as a first telling (lines 85, 189).
7. **Leave these alone:** "Kael the Counter" unexplained until the Road, "It was the last time he held it," Rimewatch's silence about the mother, the Glasspire shadow, "simpler than the ones that come against us now," and "Then it did not ring." These are the moments that worked best because nothing explained them.
