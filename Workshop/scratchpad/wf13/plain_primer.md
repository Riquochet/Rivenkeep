## 0 · THE BRIEF, ON ONE PAGE

**Jack, 2026-10-03, verbatim:** *"The old book is good, but the plain text is too hard to read. The whole paragraph should be rewritten so that a modern read can understand. There might be some embellishment because the The Book, the reader knows the Lore, but the Plain Text, They would not."*

**What went wrong with the old Plain.** It used short, common words, but it was written for someone who already knew the world. It said *the Aetherbond*, *the Covenant of Completion*, *a knowing takes two*, *the holding*, *the stair that goes no more*, and left the Book's hints as hints. A newcomer could read every word and still not know what was happening.

**The reader of the new Plain.** A modern person who has never heard of Rivenkeep, perhaps twelve years old, perhaps sixty, reading for the story. That reader should understand each paragraph the first time through, and want to read the next one.

**The job, in eight lines.**
1. Rewrite each paragraph whole, in natural modern English. Use normal word order and ordinary sentence lengths, use contractions where a speaker would, and use no old words. You may merge or split paragraphs.
2. Explain the world as the tale meets it. The first time a tale needs a name, place, custom, object, title or past event, say what it is in a clause or one sentence, woven into the telling. Use the plain English word first, and give the lore name once beside it. Then the story moves on. Never lecture.
3. Every tale stands on its own. A short reminder of who someone is is always welcome, even if an earlier tale already said it.
4. Make the Book's meaning plain. When the Book says *It was the same child*, say which child and why it matters.
5. Explain only what the hearth knows on the night the tale is laid (§2), and never what the Book keeps dark (§4).
6. Keep every beat, fact, name, number and teller, along with the teller's warmth and the build of the story. Keep the fixed lines and the markup exactly (§1.7, §1.8).
7. Open each tale with a plain *when* line in italics in place of the Book's dateline, then give the headnote in plain words (§1.5, §7).
8. Use as many words as clarity needs, usually 110 to 150 per cent of the Book's words for the tale.

**How to use this primer.** Find your tale's step on the hearth's clock (§2). Check every term you mean to explain against the lexicon (§3): its plain sentence, and the step from which the hearth knows it. Check the list of mysteries (§4) and your tale's watch-points (§7). Use §5 for one-line reminders of who's who. Copy the pattern of the two models (§8).

**The test.** Read your leaf as the twelve-year-old who has never heard of any of this. Is any sentence a wall? Read it again as the sixty-year-old. Is it still a story that moves, in this teller's voice? Then read it as Jack, who knows the Book by heart. Is anything lost, added, or given away too soon?

## 1 · THE CRAFT

### 1.1 Explaining as you go

- **One clause or one sentence, then the story.** *They looked to the Stonewrights, the guild of master builders who had raised every wall on our coast, and the Stonewrights were sitting in the grass.* The gloss rides inside the sentence the story needed anyway.
- **Plain word first, lore name once.** Write *our teachers, the old master pair the guild calls Theoliths*, and after that, *Theoliths* or *our teachers*. Don't use the lore name alone at first meeting, and don't explain it twice in one tale.
- **Through the teller's mouth when he'd say it.** Kael would say *I come from no haven, none of our cities, because the Captain found me on a road.* He'd never say *A haven is a city of the Shorelands.*
- **In the when-line or the headnote when the teller wouldn't.** Seren's headnote can carry the frame: who is speaking, what the stone at the hearth is, why the tale was told that night. Those are her words, so she may explain.
- **Explain again in each tale.** A reader may open any tale first, in the game or on the page. The tenth tale explains the Stonewrights in half a clause, just as the first does.
- **Don't explain what explains itself.** If the next sentence makes it clear, let it. *Then the holding began* needs a gloss. *Then the shouting started* doesn't.
- **No parenthetical lectures, no footnotes, no "Picture a…".** A short aside in parentheses is fine once in a headnote. In the story, use a comma or a dash.

### 1.2 What embellishment is allowed

Allowed: the context the Book's reader already carries. That means who someone is, why a thing matters, what a custom means, where a place is, what a hint points to (when the hearth knows it), and how two people are related. Example (from IV.5 on): *Merrick, the eldest of the old ship-masters, whose father had been one of the three men who came home on the spar.*

Not allowed:
- New events, new pictures, new feelings, new speeches, or any reason the Book doesn't give. If the Book doesn't say why, you don't either.
- New lore. Never invent a custom, a meaning or a history to explain a deed. An invented reason makes a deed weaker. (Jack, of the burning: *"make it less impactful, not better."*)
- Explaining a thing before the tale where the hearth learns it (§2).
- Explaining anything on the list of mysteries (§4).

### 1.3 Implications: make plain, or keep dark?

| Kind | What to do | Example |
|---|---|---|
| A hint inside the tale whose meaning the hearth knows that night | **Make it plain** | II.3: *She woke beside him* becomes *In the morning she woke beside him. The cold had taken him in the night.* · I.2: *I sang to the comma, and then I sang the rest* becomes *I sang up to the comma, and then, with no one beside me, I sang the rest.* |
| A later tale paying off an earlier one | **The later tale may remind the reader in a clause** | the Epilogue: *It was the same child: the fisher-girl who had first answered the green wood, and who keeps the north lamp.* · IV.4 may recall that the warden carried the half-made cut as a scar for the rest of his life. |
| An earlier tale planting something a later tale will pay off | **Keep the plant a plant.** Don't point forward. | III.2: *It was the last time he held it* stays ominous. Corlen's death is told in V.5's second count, so don't tell it here. · The foreword's teller *who held up a wet finger to every wind* stays nameless. |
| A puzzle lock between two tales that the Book leaves for the reader to see | **Leave it for the reader.** Make each half clear on its own, but don't join them. | V.3's hull fires at an empty gun-place, and in V.5's third count Wick dies at the gun-place that was moved. Never connect the two. |
| Something the Book keeps dark on purpose | **Keep it dark** (§4) | the warden's name; why the boat turned; who the Last Carver was |

A rule of thumb: make a sentence plain enough that the reader knows *what happened*. Don't tell the reader *what it means* when the Book has chosen to let them find that out.

### 1.4 Voice and readability

- **Natural modern English.** Normal word order. Contractions where a speaker would use them. Sentences of ordinary length that flow, joined with *and*, *but*, *so*, *because* and *when*. Mix short and long, aiming at a mean of about 12 to 16 words. A Flesch-Kincaid grade of about 3 to 6 is right (the models score 3.0 and 4.8), but the formula can't see lore. The old Plain scored grade 3 and still lost every newcomer, so the real test is the one in §0.
- **No old words.** Never use *ere, naught, aught, wist, ye, thee, thou, thy, -eth, -est, spake, bade, sware, bare (bore), nigh, whither, thither, whoso, lest, forgat, yet* (in the sense *still*), *wherefore*, or *mine* before a vowel. Never use the Book's inversions (*Up went their chisels.*). Avoid semicolons. The one exception is the guild's oldest saying, which is fixed.
- **Keep each teller's warmth.** Kael counts, swears to things and won't sit. Hale speaks in a rush and *runs it*. Voss circles back to his question, says *You can weigh it*, and owns his guilt. Brenn confesses in short, flat sentences. Seren is exact, loves her words and notices small things. {{Halyna}} speak in halves, and one finishes what the other starts. The wood says *we*, slowly, on a large scale, in present-feeling memory. The Book's best short pictures stay: the ice going out on a river, a mother's hand on a feverish child, a fledgling that could sleep in a hand. Keep what they mean, and put them in plain words.
- **Turn the Book's turns of phrase into plain meaning.** *He died, as a man setteth down a load* becomes *He died like a man finally putting down a heavy load.* *Their two chisels took the last of the light* becomes *The last of the daylight caught on their two chisels.*

### 1.5 The when-line and the headnote

Every tale opens with two italic lines: the when-line, then the headnote.

- **The when-line replaces the Book's dateline.** The Book's datelines date the *telling*: the night the tale was laid, or the season Halyna came to know a wood leaf. They don't date the events. So the when-line says when the tale was told, and adds a second short sentence when the events lie elsewhere in time.
  - **Correction to the brief's example.** *"This happened in the first winter of the siege, the night after the Rite of the Cornerstone"* is wrong for I.1. Kael *told* the tale that night. The cloak was torn two weeks earlier, at dusk on the day we reached the Keep. Model: *Told in the first winter of the siege, on the night after the Rite of the Cornerstone, the ceremony that made the Captain our Commander. It tells of the evening we first came to the Keep, two weeks before.*
  - Count the siege the way the Book does, in winters and seasons: *the first winter of the siege*, *the second spring of the siege*. A siege year begins in winter, because we reached the Keep in deep winter. The first time a tale names a Tide, say what a Tide is: *in the year of the Second Tide, as the wall named that year's enemy fleets*.
  - For wood leaves, use *known*, not *told*: *{{Halyna}} came to know this from the wood in the fourth spring of the siege.*
  - §7 gives a when-line for every leaf. Use it, or keep to its facts.
- **The headnote** is Seren's italic note, made plain. It may carry the frame the teller can't: who the teller is, what was happening that night, what the stone at the hearth is. Keep it short, about 30 to 70 words.

### 1.6 Wood leaves

Every wood leaf keeps its two parts. First comes the READING: {{Halyna}}'s broken pieces, as they spoke them. Then comes Seren's telling, in the wood's own *we*.

- **Say plainly, once in each wood leaf, what a wood leaf is.** The headnote is the place for it. Model: *This is one of the wood's tales: a memory held in the grain of the enemy's wood, which {{Halyna}} came to know by touching it. They don't read the wood. When they lay their hands on it, its memory comes to them whole, like something they remember, and they tell it in pieces, one taking up where the other runs out of words.*
- **Use "know", not "read".** The Book is strict about this: *The wood is not read but known.* Rolls, slates, letters, the Title and the Stone are *read*. The wood is *known*, and what it gives is *a knowing*. (The brief's *read by touch* means this. Write *know* or *come to know*.)
- **The reading stays broken.** Make each fragment plain (*Leaves? No. Words, like leaves.*), but don't join them into sentences. The dashes, the gaps `[ ]` and every chip (`[⟦…⟧]`) stay where they are.
- **The story is the wood's *we*.** Gloss its words in the wood's own way, by saying what the thing is: *the iron, your axes*. *The breath, the grey that you call fog* is allowed from IV.2 on, and *the breath of our sleeping trees* from II.2 on (§2). *The shore-men, your people.* Don't make the wood speak like a Shoreland teller.
- **The fixed wood words** are *This is held in the grain.* and, at the close, *and the grain still holds it*, then *\*And no one answered.\**. The hearth doesn't answer the wood until VI.3, so never put a *We remember* after a wood leaf before that tale.

### 1.9 The rules of the world

- **The singular *they* is never used for one person.** Use *he* or *she*, or recast the sentence. *They* is right for a pair-name, because a pair-name is two people.
- **There is no custom of burning, anywhere.** The warden burned the Guest's boat *to hide what had been done*. That was a weak man's cruelty, not a custom. In V.1 the soldiers burn wreck-wood *for firewood, because wood was scarce on the ridge*. Harl's *Burn it* is his temper. Never write a saying, rite or habit of burning, and never give the coast any lore about what the grey sends.
- **Faith is plain and never mocked.** Write *God*, *pray*, *prayer for the dead*. Avoid Christian words and symbols: no *Amen*, *Lord*, *church*, *priest*, *saint*, *blessing*, *sin* or *heaven*. A cross-shape (the Bar Before in the Knowings) is a drawn shape and never a holy sign.
- **The Captain is never named.** His thoughts are never told: *I don't know what the Captain thought. I know what he did.* He keeps his hood up, and he puts it back only twice, after IV.3 and at the end of the Epilogue.
- **No one sails in the grey fleet.** The ships are the living wood, and the wood is their crew. The hearth learns this in stages (§2).
- **The warden's name is always ▒▒▒▒.**

### 1.10 Traps

1. **The when-line dates the telling, not the events** (§1.5).
2. **There are two chests.** *The black chest* is the founders' chest of black stone at the bottom of the shaft. *The archive chest* is the guild's records chest that Seren carried up from Eldhythe. They are never the same chest.
3. **There are two kinds of warden and two twelves.** The *wardens of the old quarry* cut their names over the western stair, and Halvard is the last of their line. *The warden* of the outpost is ▒▒▒▒. The Captain's *Twelve* True Men keep their oaths. The outpost's *twelve* men obeyed. Never join them in the prose, because the Book lets the echo stand unsaid.
4. **There are two girls.** The small girl who falls asleep against Della's knee in II.3 is not the fisher-girl of the east keep.
5. **"The Kneeling Guest" is not a Book name.** Write *the Guest*. In Brenn's mouth he is *the stranger*. In V.1 he is *the grey man of the old tale*. His own name, *Aelrhen*, comes in IV.4.
6. **"Rivenmen" doesn't exist before the Naming** (day 21). I.1 is told on night 15, so Kael says *the Shorelands*.
7. **Hearth order is not Book order.** I.5 is told at the very end of the war but sits fifth in the Book. II.2 is told in the fifth winter but sits in Book Two. The foreword is written in the third summer, yet every player reads it first. In these three, explain only what the tale itself needs, and never borrow a later tale's big reveal (§2).
8. **Don't count *the Fall* as the siege.** The Fall is the twenty months in which the ten havens burned, before the Keep. The siege is the six winters at the Keep.
9. **"The holding"** is the fighting part of the siege day (§3). Gloss it every time it first appears in a tale.
10. **Don't let the wood know what the hearth knows,** or the hearth know what only the wood knows. The council never learns why the host drew back. Nobody at the hearth knows what the Stone's grain says.
11. **No forward glances.** Don't write *little did we know*, *as we'd later learn* or *that was the last time* unless the Book says it.

## 2 · THE HEARTH'S CLOCK: WHAT IS KNOWN WHEN

**The rule.** A tale may explain what the hearth knows on the night it is laid, and nothing that a later night teaches. "Later" means later on this clock, the order the tales were actually laid. That is also the order the game opens them in (notes §5.3). It is not the order of the Book.

**And the second rule, for the leaves that sit early in the Book but were laid late** (the foreword, the Invocation, I.5 and II.2): explain only what the tale itself needs to be followed, in the smallest true words. Never spend another tale's big reveal there, even if the hearth already knew it. Example: II.2 may say *the years of the iron, when your people's axes came to our wood*, because the tale needs that. It must not tell the Guest's death, because II.2 doesn't need it and IV.3 is where it's told.

**H0** means common knowledge: what any survivor in the column knew before the Keep. Any tale may explain it.

## 4 · THE DELIBERATE MYSTERIES: NEVER EXPLAIN

*The Book leaves these dark on purpose. The Plain may say plainly what the Book says, which is often a guess or a silence, but it may never add an answer.*

1. **The warden's name.** It is always ▒▒▒▒, in every tale and every mouth. Never give a name, an initial or a nickname, and never hint at one. In the wood's tales he has no name at all: he is *the master of that house*, *the host*.
2. **The kinship of the two tongues, and the parting of the peoples.** Never hint that the long-lived and our people were ever one people, or that their words and ours are related. Never gloss *ael*. Keep the Book's own glosses of whole names (*Aelrhen*, *bond-to-stone*; *the Aelvaren*, *the Bearer of the Binding*), but never take a name apart. Never point out a likeness of sound (*Aelrhen* and *Rhyna*, *Aelvaren* and *Halvard*, *Brenn*, and so on). In V.7 Part I, Seren notices that *Aelthae* begins like *Ael'thar*. She writes *I have written the name, and I will not write what I thought.* Keep that silence exactly. Don't say what she thought, and don't hint at it.
3. **Why the burning boat turned.** Tell it as Brenn saw it (IV.3): *Well out, the boat turned, into the wind and against the current, into the grey, burning.* Tell it as the harbours told it (IV.5), where it's a wonder, *which no boat can do*. Tell it as the wood tells itself (IV.6): grief, the fire in every board, *Then we turned*, and *Even burnt, even dying, the wood knows the way home.* Add no cause: no spirit, no curse, no magic, no dead man steering, no plan.
4. **The Last Carver.** Give only Seren's guesses. *Young by their count, I think, and seasoned by ours.* *I call him the Last Carver, because I think that when he cut this there was no other.* Never tie him to the *one young hand* of V.7 Part III. Never say what became of him, never say why the cutting stops (Seren only wonders whether his hand stopped), and never explain the extra word *the*.
5. **What the Stone's grain says.** The native block shows it to the reader, *Known by no one at this hearth*. No one in the tale understands it. Don't retell it in the prose, and don't let Seren know it.
6. **Against whom the first builders knew war.** *The slates don't say.*
7. **What it means that the two raw-edged planks fit** (V.2). Say they fit the way the two halves of a split log fit. Then keep Seren's silence: *I haven't asked {{Halyna}} what it means, and they haven't told me.*
8. **Why the warden drew back from the blade.** Brenn says *he had never bled in his life*. The wood says *Fear leaves no mark on wood*, and so the council never knew why. Seren says of the long-lived, *They did not know it was ignorance.* Give each of these as its speaker says it, and never state the reason as fact.
9. **The shadow at Glasspire.** Some who fled saw a shadow in the fog, greater than any hull. Pellow, who was there, looked close and saw nothing. The wood holds nothing of that night. It stays unsolved.
10. **The Covenant of Completion, before the Epilogue.** Give the name only. In II.3 the old masters' saying (*God does not take the one without leaving the other a work to do*) is told, but it is not tied to the Covenant. Only Seren ties them, in the Epilogue.
11. **Where the staff went.** *I know the stair that goes no more. I haven't gone down to look.* Make plain that this is Seren's belief: she thinks the Captain laid the staff down in its work, down the old shaft where the first staff lay. Never confirm it.
12. **What we will send back, whether anyone will come, and whether the Book will be cut in granite.** These are open.
13. **Kael's count of the enemy.** *I know their number, and I won't tell it at this hearth.*
14. **What the last two growings hold** (V.6). *What is cut in them, we don't know.*
15. **Whose ink is whose.** In Halyna's readings, never mark which half is Halvard's and which is Rhyna's. II.3's MARKED form is the one exception. Likewise, which stroke of Enno and Della's mark Della cut alone can't be told: *You can't tell.*
16. **The Captain's name and his thoughts.** *I don't know what the Captain thought. I know what he did.*
17. **Whether the hour really stretched** (I.3). Tell what Hale felt, and Kael's correction (§1.7). Don't settle it either way.
18. **Why Rhyna plunged her hands into the rain-butt** (IV.6's headnote). *She wouldn't say why.*
19. **The foreword's dead teller.** *One of its tellers, who held up a wet finger to every wind, already lies under the east cairns.* Never name him there.
20. **The *small fire* over the Long Winter** (VI.2, Eirlenth). *At the last a small fire stood over us, where the hall had been.* Leave it unnamed. A reader may connect it with Hesk's lamp, but the Plain doesn't say so.
21. **The token** `{LAST_THRONE_AT_HAVEN}`. Leave the token as it is, and never name the haven in prose.
22. **Seren's empty page** facing V.4 stays empty.
23. **The cradle-rhyme's last line**, *the first two keep what the first two know*. Explain only that it led Rhyna to the stair.
24. **The Book of Knowings.** Seren *did not know* a single carving. Only the Bonded can know them. Never let her know one.

**The puzzle locks: the reader joins them, not the Plain.** Make each half clear where it stands, and never write the sentence that joins the two halves.
- V.3's hull fires at the empty gun-place, and in V.5 Count III Wick dies at the moved gun.
- The outpost's twelve and the Captain's Twelve.
- The quarry-wardens' names cut *into* the stair, and the outpost warden's name cut *out*.
- The founders' gate turned over at dusk, and the boat that came back and was known by its stem.
- *came no further*: the warden at his door (IV.3), and the ship-masters at the head of the shingle (the Epilogue).
- *Leave nothing to show* (IV.3), and *Wood can hide nothing* (IV.6).
- The laughter. Brenn's comrades laughed at the door (IV.3). The shore laughed at the three from the spar (IV.5). The wall laughed in the first winter (V.5 I), laughed again in the fifth (V.5 V and V.7 II), and *no child laughed* at the Stone (the Epilogue).

- The fisher-girl in VI.1. She is only *a fisher-girl* until VI.3 says whose great-granddaughter she is.

## 6 · THE TIMELINE, IN PLAIN WORDS

*Years are counted back from the day of the banner, the evening we reached the Keep. The numbers are the design notes' estimates, given for your bearings only. The Book itself uses no numbers before the banner, so in the Plain prefer the Book's own phrases: "long ago", "in our grandparents' time", "two generations". "Told in" names the tale where the hearth hears it.*

**Long, long ago: the First Days**
- The first builders find a mountain ridge split from crown to root and build a fortress in the cleft: the Keep. Aldwena lay the capstone of the inner gate and cut their mark and vow beneath it. The quarry-wardens begin cutting their names over the western stair. At the hearth, one of them lays a tale each night. *(II.1, I.4)*
- The people go down to the warm coast to build the havens. Before they go, the founders lay the Keep's slates and its first staff in a black stone chest at the bottom of a shaft. *(II.1, I.4)*

**Some three hundred years before: the Days of the Havens**
- Ten havens rise along the coast, Eldhythe first and Glasspire last, each walled by the Stonewrights, with a staff cut for each one. The Keep is forgotten, its mortar is never renewed, and one winter its capstone falls without anyone hearing. *(III.1, II.1)*

**About 120 to 70 years before: the Harvest**
- The havens' ships find the black pillars at the edge of the fog. For two generations the Trade Council sends out the axes. The stumps put up new shoots, and those are cut for poles, again and again, until the stumps stop growing. The fog thins, and the Council calls it good fortune. *(IV.1, IV.2)*
- Behind the fog, the long-lived carve a plea on standing stones beside the pillars. The axemen carry the stones off with the timber, and they hang in harbour-houses as ornaments. The Stonewrights' warning is read once and shelved. *(IV.1, IV.2)*

**About 75 years before, in our grandparents' time: the summer of the Guest**
- A stranger comes out of the fog in a small grey boat to the outpost at the edge of the land. He kneels and sets down his gift, and the warden kicks it aside. The warden tears himself away from the stranger's blade, the cut goes deep, and he orders the stranger killed. They beat him to death, throw him into his boat, and burn it *to leave nothing to show*. Far out, the burning boat turns into the wind and against the current. *(IV.3; IV.4)*
- That evening the burning boat strikes two timber-boats. Three men come home on a spar. The outpost's letter calls the stranger a hostile intruder, and the shore laughs at the men from the spar. *(IV.5, IV.6)*
- The boat reaches home with the stranger's bones in it. The long-lived's council knows everything that happened except why the host drew back, and sees only an act of war. The young outvote the old, and the first carving of the war is cut: *Grow until the shore is silent.* *(IV.6, V.4)*

**From about 70 years before: the Forgetting**
- The axes stop, from fear of the fog and because cheaper timber is found. No one decides it. The havens forget the stones. *(IV.1)*
- Behind the fog, the first fleet lies ready and waits, while one old elder who pleaded for peace still lives. *(V.4, V.6)*
- About forty years after the killing, old Brenn confesses at Eldhythe. Wendhessa seal his words around the gift and tell no one. *(IV.3)*
- Very late, the havens grow humble again, and people climb the temple steps for counsel. It is too late. *(IV.1)*

**Twenty months before the banner: the Fall**
- The last old elder dies, and the first fleet is let loose. Grey ships with no one aboard burn the ten havens one after another, over twenty months, Eldhythe first and Glasspire last. *(III.2, V.4)*

**The last month: the Road**
- The survivors climb the mountain passes. On the night of the pass many children die of cold, Tarnel and Wendhessa among them. Seren carries the archive chest on alone. *(III.2, II.3, IV.3)*

**Day 0: the day of the banner** (deep winter)
- The column reaches the ruined Keep. Halyna turn the fallen capstone over and find their own forebears' marks. The Captain tears his cloak and writes the Title. Halyna's cry raises the guild. The terms are made. The first course is laid by torchlight. *(I.1, II.1, I.2)*

**The Fourteen Nights, and the first month**
- Night 3: the black chest. Day 14: the Rite, and the Captain becomes Commander. Night 15: the first tale (I.1). Day 21: the Naming, when we become the Rivenmen. About day 24: the first assault, the hour that stretched, and that night the first word known from the wood, *shore*. *(I.4, I.1, I.5, I.3, V.1)*

**The six winters of the siege.** Each winter a new kind of fleet comes, and the wall calls each one a Tide.
1. **The Hasty Tide** (the first year). Ships come alone and straight. Jory dies late in the winter, and the Captain cuts the first strip. Enno and Della are sealed in high summer.
2. **The Second Tide.** Ships come in pairs, and seed falls. Corlen dies late in the winter, and Voss takes his guns. In late summer, on the Night of the Naming, every plank's order is known.
3. **The Tide of Remembering.** The ships look before they trust. Brenn's bundle is opened, the gift is known, and Wick dies.
4. **The Joined Tide.** Three roads, one hour. The council and the growing are known. Enno and Della die in the summer.
5. **The Tide that Learned Deceit.** Decoys and false retreats. Pellow dies in the spring, and Ruan takes his glass.
6. **The Last Tide.** Fleets go home when their bearer and its second fall. At the winter's end the ten Thrones come and settle in the ten havens. Old Ebba dies, and we go down.

**The sixth year: the homecomings**
- One by one the Thrones fall and the havens are ours again. Hesk dies on the ice at Rimewatch, and a fisher-girl lights his lamp. In the tenth Throne's heart a green shoot is found, and it says *Home* (VI.3). The boatwrights build a boat from the carved planks, Halyna cut their mark across its stem, and the whole hearth kneels as it is sent into the grey with the shoot, a coin and Halyna's chisels.

**The seventh winter.** Halyna die in one night. The Captain cuts the last of his half-cloak and goes into the winter in a plain grey cloak. *(the Epilogue)*

**The seventh spring.** The grey boat comes back with no one in it, carrying a stone cut in our letters. The staff is gone from the gatehouse. From then on the inner gate is left open every night. *(the Epilogue)*

**The fourth year after the homecomings.** Seren writes the Epilogue. That winter she reads three words of ours on the lowest grey stone over the hearth: *Stop… Sky… Dying…* *(the Last Note)*

**The deaths, in order.** Before the banner: the Guest; the two crews; the warden, then Brenn; Tarnel and Wendhessa, on the night of the pass. On the wall: Jory (late in the first winter) · Corlen (late in the second winter) · Wick (the third year) · Enno and Della (the fourth summer) · Pellow (the fifth spring) · Old Ebba (the end of the sixth winter) · Hesk (the sixth year, at Rimewatch) · Halyna (the seventh winter).

Every lore word is explained where the tale first needs it, in the teller's own mouth or in Seren's headnote. Every beat, name, number and fixed line of the Book is kept. The markup is exactly the current Plain's. Nothing is explained that the hearth didn't know that night.*

*Their lengths: I.1 runs 1,556 words against the Book's 1,256, or 124 per cent, with a 350-word PROLOGUE EXCERPT. II.3 runs 1,596 words against 1,252, or 127 per cent. The markup and native blocks match `plain_v3.md` exactly (checked with the logic of `wf11/_markup_cmp.py`), and neither leaf has an old word or a semicolon outside the fixed saying.*

*What to notice in II.3.* Every guild word is explained once, in the speaker's mouth, at the moment the children would ask: *the Aetherbond*, *Theoliths*, *the Stonwryt*, *the holding*, *the unbonded*, *a knowing*. ### I.1 · The Torn Cloak

*Told in the first winter of the siege, on the night after the Rite of the Cornerstone, the ceremony that made the Captain our Commander. It tells of the evening we first came to the Keep, two weeks before.*

*Kael the Counter laid this tale on the first night this hearth ever asked for one. Here, whoever holds the old gate-stone by the fire is the one who tells, and we say a tale is "laid," like a stone in a wall, because a laid tale stays. Kael told me not to change a single word of his. I changed a good many, and when I read it back to him, he swore it was word for word what he'd said.*

Give me the stone. No, I'll stand. I've done enough sitting this winter.

I swear by the stone over the western stair, where the wardens of the old quarry cut their names, each son under his father. I'm a counter, not a singer. I count heads, and stones, and days. What I didn't count, I won't tell you.

The column came in under the outer gate at dusk. I heard it before I saw it: a great many feet, and not one voice. I stood at the gate and counted it in, head by head.

Old men came in, and women carrying bundles, and children so tired they were asleep on their feet. Soldiers came in from six broken companies, men who no longer knew who their officers were. Last of all came the Captain, who had guarded the back of the column the whole way. Two thousand and a few hundred, all told.

We'd walked for a month from the ashes of Glasspire, the last of our ten cities on the coast to burn. The old songs told of a castle up here in the mountains, a fortress that would hold when nothing else did, and we'd come to find it. What we found was a heap of stones. The gate hung by one hinge. The grass in the courtyard stood as high as your knee. The great hall the songs sang about had lost its roof, and snow lay on the hearth where you're sitting now.

Some people shook. Some cried. Some fell down and wouldn't get up.

Then the shouting started, and it wasn't one shout. Some shouted that we should go and beg the grey sails for peace. Those were the grey ships that had come out of the fog at sea and burned our cities one after another, and nobody alive had ever understood a single word from them. Some shouted that we should cross the high passes, into the snow beyond them. Some shouted that we should go down into the cellars, bar the doors and wait, as if the sea might forget us.

On the long retreat from our burning cities I'd heard men break. Until that dusk I'd never heard a whole people break. It sounds like the ice on a river breaking up in spring.

The wind came down off the riven ridge, the great split ridge the Keep is built on, and it had the whole of winter in its mouth.

People looked for a general, but the generals were dead. They looked for the lords, but the lords were scattered. They looked to the Stonewrights, the guild of master builders who had raised every wall on our coast, and the Stonewrights were sitting in the grass with their chisels in their laps.

Nobody looked to the Captain. He was only the captain of one small company, and that company was us. The whole long road he'd been waiting for someone whose place it was to lead, and nobody came, because they were all dead. Make no mistake: there was never a greater man, and I don't think his equal ever lived. But he never went looking to lead. I swear to that.

<!-- PROLOGUE EXCERPT BEGIN -->
We True Men, the Captain's own company of twelve, looked to our Captain. And the Captain looked at the wall.

Then he climbed it. Back in the ruined hall he'd picked up a coal from the cold hearth, this same hearth, and none of us knew why. He went up the broken face of the wall alone, using the stones that stuck out of the old mortar. We stood at the bottom and let him go, because he hadn't told us to follow. I've counted those stones since. There are thirty-one, and four of them wobble.

At the top he took off his cloak. It had been crimson once, before the road wore it down to the colour of old brick. In that wind it was all he had to keep him warm.

Nobody in the courtyard said a word. Somewhere among the women a child coughed, and his mother hushed him. After that there was nothing but the wind.

He tore the cloak in two, down the back.

It made a small sound, the sound old cloth makes when it gives. I heard it over the wind.

He spread one half on the stones along the top of the wall, knelt on it with the coal, and wrote. He wrote slowly, the way men write who haven't written much. He tied that half to a pike he'd pulled from the rubble. The other half, with nothing written on it, he put back around his shoulders.

I was the first up the wall after him, so I was the first to read it. His hand hadn't shaken once. I turned to the courtyard and read it out loud. My voice wasn't steady, and I won't pretend it was.

**IN MEMORY OF OUR HOME, OUR FAMILIES, OUR GOD, OUR FREEDOMS, OUR PEACE.**

He wedged the pike into a crack in the wall and gave it to the wind, and the wind took it. That was the last unbroken wall in the Shorelands, our whole country by the sea, and that half of a cloak was the banner flying over it.

Now hear who answered, and in what order, because I counted. A count isn't a count until it has names.

The True Men answered first. The Captain had found us one at a time, in garrisons and on roads, and he bound us to him with nothing but trust. We kept our oaths. We built what we promised. We never fought except in a just cause. That's the whole of a True Man. I tell you so you'll know it isn't much. It isn't little, either.

Each of us goes by the place he came from. Stannard of the ridge. Corlen of the harbour. Tamm of the three spans. Marl the fen-born. Aske of the canopy. Garvel of the smithies. Harl of the burning shore. Ulden of the galleries. Hesk of the mere. Lanner of the crags. Pellow of the spire. And me, Kael the Counter, their oath-keeper, the one who holds the oaths they swear. I come from no haven, none of our ten cities, because the Captain found me on a road.

Twelve. I counted us twice, and it didn't take long.

After us the six companies answered, one troop to each of us True Men, and they trust us the way we trust him. Then the people of the column answered, the ones who had carried their children on their backs through the mountain passes. One voice. Then a dozen. Then hundreds, until I couldn't count them any more.

I stopped counting. It's the only time I ever have.

He never gave an order. He only wrote down what we had left, and it was enough.

That's why the pennant over this Keep is crimson, and over every keep we'll ever raise. It's the colour of that cloak.

Look at the end of the bench, you children at the front. There he sits, with his hood up.

The other half of the cloak is on his back.

He'll tell you it wasn't like that. It was like that.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

### II.3 · Of the Bonded and the Theoliths

*Told at midsummer in the first year of the siege, on the night of a sealing, our guild's wedding of a bonded pair.*

*That morning {{Idrenna}}, the oldest pair in our guild, had sealed Enno and Della under the inner gate. It was the first sealing at the Keep since the first builders left it, long ago, and I held the lamp. That night {{Halyna}}, Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders, told this to the children. They wanted me to write down whose words were whose, because children like to know.*

<!-- HALYNA -->

*They laid their hands on the stone together and began:*

"We hold the stone together,"

"the way we hold everything."

<!-- MARKED -->

**Rhyna.** Come closer, all of you, where the fire can see your faces. The children get the front, and the old can listen too, if they're quiet. This morning you watched Enno and Della kneel down as two and stand up as one. Now they are {{Enrella}}, one name for the two of them.

*A small girl asked them what it felt like. "It is like being one person," said Enno, and Della finished it for him: "with four hands and no excuses." A pair cuts its mark under the last stone of a finished work, but Enno and Della said they'd build a house with one door and cut their mark under its first stone, because they couldn't wait for the last. The whole hearth laughed. Then the girl turned to me and asked when I would be sealed. I had no answer, and I went on writing.*

**Halvard.** Back when our people were still called Shorelanders, not every Shorelander was a Stonewright, but every Stonewright was a Shorelander. The guild calls its children while they're still in the cradle, from any home at all. Once, the records say, it called a child from a jailer's house in Carnhold. No mother or father ever said no, because the guild looked after the faith of our land as carefully as it looked after its walls.

**Rhyna.** We believe God made each pair one before either of them drew breath. So the seers go among the cradles with a lamp and bend down low, and they read what was written there before we were born. They don't choose. They only read. That bond, made by God and read at the cradle, is what we call the Aetherbond. A seer bent over his cradle and mine like that, and from that night on we slept in one cradle, at his mother's house and at mine by turns. In our whole lives we two have never spent a night under different roofs. Remember this, because you'll need it: the bond is made before the cradle, read in the cradle, and made whole only at the sealing.

**Halvard.** Our teachers were {{Idrenna}}, who are sitting by the door tonight. The guild calls such teachers Theoliths: the old master pair who raise a young pair and teach them. They taught us to cut stone, to lay it and to set it. They taught us to pray, and to keep the guild's count of things in the old hand, the writing of the first builders. They told us the name of our vow, the Covenant of Completion, and nothing more, and we'll tell you nothing more either. You'll understand it when it's yours to understand. {{Idrenna}} will nod when we two get this right.

*By the door, {{Idrenna}} sat with their two white heads close together. They didn't nod.*

**Rhyna.** At a sealing, the guild says out loud what the cradle said first. The masters give the two of them one name, made from both their names, the way one gate is made from two posts and the stone laid across them. With the name, they give the mark the seers read at the cradle. That's a name for the mouth, and a mark for the stone. We call that mark the Stonwryt. No hands but the pair's own may cut it, and no other pair will ever break it. That's how we two were sealed and named {{Halyna}}. He is my husband and I am his wife, but we were one pair long before we were either.

*Halvard leaned toward the hearth, and the children leaned with him.*

**Halvard.** The ash is soft tonight. Watch my finger, because I'll only draw this once. This is our mark. On the night of the Rite, when the Captain was made our Commander, we two cut it under the new capstone, the great stone across the top of the inner gate. It lies face down there, where no eye will see it again until the wall falls. Look once.

::: native legend_stonwryt_halyna chip
{{Halyna}}'s Stonwryt, drawn by Halvard with one finger in the ash of the Long Hearth, for the children
:::

*No child moved. In the fire a coal settled.*

**Rhyna.** Now I sweep it away with my hand, and it's gone. Ash is for the wind. A mark is for stone, and stone holds.

**Halvard.** You've seen the two of us on the wall in the holding, the hours when the enemy's grey ships attack and the wall has to be held. We work as one, and never need a word between us. The old masters counted the work of one pair as the work of twelve. I'm Elder Halvard and she's Elderess Rhyna, and neither of us is the elder of the other. There was never a stone she laid second.

*A boy at the front counted to twelve on his fingers, and ran out of fingers.*

**Rhyna.** If I cut my hand in the south tower, he feels it on the north wall, and he can tell you which hand. The old masters had a saying for it. Harm to one is harm to both.

**Halvard.** The Bonded are seldom apart, and never far apart. In all the records of the guild, no bond was ever broken. Separation, God did not permit.

*The fire had burned down a finger's width. Enno and Della sat hand in hand, and the small girl had fallen asleep against Della's knee.*

**Rhyna.** So the Bonded die together, and we two aren't afraid of it. If one of a pair is killed, the other has no wound and no sickness. But the heart breaks, because it knows, and the body goes still, because its work is done. People say eight days is the longest anyone has ever lived on alone. Hours, say the Stonewrights, and we're the ones who keep the count. Many pairs have drawn their last breath at the same moment, a mile apart, and each knew the other was going. And each one went too. When the left hand dies, does not the right one die too?

**Halvard.** One last thing, and then bed. Sometimes one baby of a cradle-pair died before the sealing. Because the bond wasn't whole yet, the other baby lived. We call that child unbonded, and the guild gives the child to a bonded household as its third. The unbonded keep only one name, and among the guild a single name is a mourning, because everyone who hears it knows someone is missing. But the old masters said this: God does not take the one without leaving the other a work to do.

**Rhyna.** Seren, who held the lamp this morning and is writing all this down, is our third. She lay in one cradle with Tarnel, a boatwright's son from Tidesmeet, the river city of three bridges. All along the Road, our long march over the mountains to this Keep, he knotted a net as he walked, so he could fish the mountain lakes when we came to them. He taught her his knot. On the night of the pass, the coldest night of the Road, the cold took many of the children, and some of the old. Tarnel and Seren lay down together under one cloak. In the morning she woke up beside him. The cold had taken him in the night.

**Halvard.** So she lived, and she is one. Tarnel told her once, "A net is only knots holding hands," and this Book is bound with his knot. She can't know the wood. When we two lay our hands on the carved wreck-wood that the sea brings in under our wall, what's carved there comes into us whole, the way you know a thing you remember, without reading it. We call that a knowing, and it takes two to hold one, the way it takes two hands to lift a stone that one hand can't. But she won't die of anyone else's death. She can go alone where we two can't, and carry this Book on after us. Every scribe before her was one of a pair, and when a pair died, their book was laid away in the vault after them, and no one read it again. A book that nobody reads aloud is only ink. Look, her pen hasn't stopped. This Book has to outlive the people who tell it. That's why Seren is the one who writes it.

*My pen had stopped when Rhyna told of the cloak. I dipped it, and wrote on. By the door, {{Idrenna}} nodded at last.*

*Together, {{Halyna}} ended it with the oldest saying of the guild:*

Birth united them; death could not separate them.

We have laid this tale the way it was laid for us.

<!-- HALYNA END -->

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*
