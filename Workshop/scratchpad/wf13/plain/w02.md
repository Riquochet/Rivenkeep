### V.3 · The Wreck That Went Home

*{{Halyna}} came to know this from the wood in the depth of the third winter of the siege.*

*This is one of the wood's tales. The wood the enemy's ships are made of holds memories in its grain. {{Halyna}} are two people under one name: Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders. When either of them lays a hand on that wood, its memory comes into them both, whole, like something they remember. They speak it in broken pieces, one taking up where the other stops, and where their words give out, I leave a space. Then I, Seren, who write this Book, tell it whole, in the wood's own voice. No one at our hearth answers it. Halvard had his hands on this wreck. Rhyna's palms were cut open and raw from the salt, so she didn't touch it, but she knew it as well as he did, the way kin know kin.*

<!-- READING -->

*— Salt. Salt in every plank. Cold, for a long time.*  
*— A stone, flashing. Iron, low and right through. The sea coming in.*  
*— Home when wounded. Home, slowly. Telling its kin.*  
*— Look before —*  
*— you trust.*  
*— Again. The same water. Alone, and slow.*  
*— One fire, thrown. Where it landed — [ ]*  
*— A stone, flashing. Iron, low and —*

This is held in the grain.

We were a ship of the Ralenthae, the tide the roots taught: a fleet that came to you already knowing what earlier ships had learned. Wood tells wood through its roots. Every ship of ours carries orders carved into its wood, and more than one word was carved into us: a memory, and a warning. Now we are broken wood, and the salt is in every plank.

We came to your wall twice in one cold season.

<!-- RING -->

The first time was in the grey of morning. The other ships, our kin, were all around us, and the bearer, our lead ship, sailed at our head. A stone of your wall flashed. That was one of your guns firing, though to us it was only a stone that flashed. The first young ships that came against you, the green wood, had learned what a flash means, and the roots had carried it home. Where a stone flashes, the iron is there.

We turned toward that stone, and it spoke again.

Your iron shot went into us, low and right through. The sea came in.

Then an older carving woke in us, one of the first orders the green wood was ever given: *Home when wounded.* We turned our stern to your wall and went back toward home, into the breath, the grey that you call fog. We went low in the water, and slow. Your wall let us go, or didn't see us go. We don't know which.

As we went, we told our kin, and we needed no words to do it. First the flash went out of us into the roots, and through the roots to them. Then the place it had come from. Then the place where the iron had found us. Our kin felt it in their own planks.

Last of all went the dread. The dread is only this: one place in the water has become a place where wood dies. It spread slowly, the way sap rises, to every ship that had come with us. It reached the bearer's heart soonest, the core of its wood, and from that heart it went out to all of us.

In the night, the bearer's heart carved the next day's orders into us: *Look before you trust.* A stone that spoke yesterday may be silent today. A silent stone may be waiting.

The next time the fleet came, we went out ahead of our kin, alone and slow. We knew that place. We were sent to look, and for nothing else.

We threw one fire at the stone that had spoken, to see if it would answer. The grain holds nothing of what our fire found there. The stone didn't answer.

Behind us, our kin stood off in the breath and waited. The water lay flat, and we were slow.

Your wall still flashed.

It didn't flash from the stone that had spoken. It flashed from another stone along the wall, one that had been silent.

With the sea already in us, we couldn't turn away a second time. The iron found us where it had found us before, low and right through, as though it too kept count, the way we do.

Out in the breath, our kin felt it in their own planks.

<!-- RING -->

On that second morning we sank close under your wall. But before we went down, we had told it. The ships that lived carried it home, root to root, to the groves where the next ships stood growing, and they grew up knowing it.

For a long time we lay in the cold, and the keels of our kin passed over us. Then the sea worked us loose, and the waves brought us in to your shingle, the pebble beach below your wall.

Your hands are on us now.

So we went home once, and told. We came back to look, and died looking. The groves have it now, and the grain still holds it.

*And no one answered.*

### V.4 · The Council Under the Thin Sky

*{{Halyna}} came to know this from the wood in the fourth winter of the siege, the year of the enemy fleets we called the Joined Tide. It tells of a council held long ago behind the great fog at sea, in our grandparents' time, after the stranger we call the Guest, whose own name was Aelrhen, had been killed on our shore.*

*This is one of the wood's tales. The wood the enemy's ships are made of holds memories in its grain. {{Halyna}} are two people under one name: Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders. When either of them lays a hand on that wood, its memory comes into them both, whole, like something they remember. They speak it in broken pieces, one taking up where the other stops, and where their words give out, I leave a space. Then I, Seren, who write this Book, tell it whole, in the wood's own voice. No one at our hearth answers it. This one came from a heart, the core of a wrecked ship's wood, where its orders are carved. Rhyna laid a hand on it and took it away at once. Halvard came up from the vault under our hall, where we keep the wreck-wood, with tears on his face. The knowing took only a moment. The telling took them the rest of that year, because a council is many minds, and they had only their own two mouths to tell it with.*

<!-- READING -->

*— Branches for a roof, moss underneath. A hall — no. A grove.*  
*— The sky thin. Gaps in it, like missing teeth.*  
*— A little one, cold in the morning. Not waking.*  
*— Old voices. Wait. Wait with them.*  
*— A keel, black, in the roots. Bones inside its ribs.*  
*— Wait —*  
*— no. Not wait. Young voices, over the old. Many —*  
*— too many. We two can't hold them all — [ ]*

This is held in the grain.

Behind the breath, the grey that you call fog, there is a people. They are the long-lived, the people of our wood. They held their council under the silver elders, the great old trees of their grove. Their hall was the grove itself. It had no roof but the branches, and no floor but the moss. The silver elders kept everything that was said beneath them, as they keep everything they hear.

We grew from a cutting of those silver elders, so what they hold, we hold. We give it to you as it was.

<!-- RING -->

In the summers before Aelrhen came home, the sky was thin along the edge of the wood. The pillars at the edge, the tall black trees that held our sky in place, were gone in long gaps, like teeth gone from a jaw. Your iron axes had cut them down. Through the gaps the breath ran out, and the between-light, the soft light that came to us through the breath, went hard and white.

Each spring the newborns of the long-lived were frailer. Some didn't wake. The oldest faded before their years were full.

The old ones of the council told everyone to be patient, the way trees are patient. "The iron comes seldom now," they said. "The pillars haven't put up a single new shoot. They're waiting. Wait with them, and when the iron stops, they'll grow the sky back."

The old ones were dying of the very wound they asked everyone to be patient with. They knew it, and they still said to wait.

Then the Aelvaren, Aelrhen's small grey boat, came home through the hidden channels. It was burned down to the keel, and Aelrhen's bones lay inside its ribs.

The young of the long-lived had grown up under a sky that was leaving them. They had watched their mothers and fathers fade in the white light, and they couldn't help them. They came into the grove, where the keel lay among the roots, and over it they called for a vote.

The old ones stood up and pleaded. "Send another. Learn their words. They don't know us. They can't know the wood, and we have never gone down to teach them. Don't be hasty."

But by then the old voices were few, and faint. They were the voices of the dying.

The young answered, "We listened to them through three of their lifetimes and learned three of their words, and they wouldn't hear even those."

And the patience broke.

Their answer was true, but it wasn't the whole truth. We and our people listened for a long time from behind the breath, and we never once went down to your shore with our hands open. Except once. Aelrhen went down with those three words, and when he was killed, we didn't send a second.

We chose to grow ships, and not to learn words. We never asked whether a whole people had killed Aelrhen, or only one household at the very edge of your land.

But war is for the short-minded and the angry young.

So the vote was carried, by the weight of anger and grief.

<!-- RING -->

That same night they cut the straightest young tree open to the heart, where it stood. It was our kin, and it felt the blade. Into its heartwood the young cut the first carving of the war:

*Grow until the shore is silent.*

And the wood, which can't unlearn what is carved into it, grew.

In those same summers, the iron stopped coming to the edge. The grove hadn't waited long enough to see it.

While even one of the old ones who had pleaded was still alive, the new-grown ships lay ready at the edge of your sea, and didn't go. When the last of those old ones died and went down into the moss, the ones who had cried for war cut the ships loose against your shore.

The words of the old ones didn't go down into the moss with them. The silver elders keep them, as they keep everything they hear, and so do we: "Learn their words."

The carving didn't go down either. The heart of every ship that has sailed since carries it. It lies under all their other carvings, like the first ring under all the rings, and the grain still holds it.

*And no one answered.*

*The facing page, in Seren's hand:*

> In this Book a tale of the wood's is meant to face a tale of our own, but for this one we have none. We weren't there, and we didn't know that anyone was deciding anything. I have left this page empty, and I ask whoever keeps this Book after me to leave it that way.

### V.6 · Of the Growing

*{{Halyna}} came to know this from the wood in the fourth spring of the siege.*

*This is one of the wood's tales. The wood the enemy's ships are made of holds memories in its grain. {{Halyna}} are two people under one name: Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders. When either of them lays a hand on that wood, its memory comes into them both, whole, like something they remember. They speak it in broken pieces, one taking up where the other stops, and where their words give out, I leave a space. Then I, Seren, who write this Book, tell it whole, in the wood's own voice. No one at our hearth answers it. This one came from a heart, the core of a wrecked ship's wood, where its orders are carved. Halvard carried it up on his shoulder, and before he could set it down it came into them both, though Rhyna was sitting at the hearth. It was too much for them, so it lay in the vault under our hall until spring. There was a song in it, and I have set the song down as verse, as close as ink can come.*

<!-- READING -->

*— Singing. Singing for a long time. Bending toward it.*  
*— Trunk, and root, and young tree. Led — led far.*  
*— A ship. No one in it. No one at all.*  
*— Roots under everything, binding. Kin, and kin, and kin.*  
*— The singing —*  
*— stops. A blade, in the heart.*  
*— Cut into us. In the cut — [ ]*

This is held in the grain.

We were grown, not built. You cut your stone and lay it, and where you lay it, there it stays, and it is dead.

From the very first, the long-lived, the people of our wood, guided the wood. And the wood breathed out the grey sky they lived by: the breath, which you call fog. They and the wood were one life, in two kinds. A ship is only a tree that has been led a long way.

The long-lived sang to the trees that were going to be ships. The trees bent to the singing, because nothing had ever been asked of them but song. They sang hulls out of the breathing trunks, keels out of the roots that fed them, and masts out of young trees trained by many summers of song in the wind.

None of the long-lived could be spared. So out of the fine roots, the ones that find their way in the dark, they sang the warriors who come ashore on your coast. Whatever those roots touched, the wood knew. They were the wood's hands. In the crowns of the broad ships they sang the winged seed, which rose on the wind and came down over your walls.

No one sails in us. We are crewed by the living, and the living are the wood.

<!-- RING -->

We were a tree, and an old one, in the first groves. For many summers they sang to us, and we bent. Then the singing stopped.

The blade went into our heart.

In the wound they cut the orders. We passed them on through the roots that bind each ship to the ships around it, carving them into every hull near us, and every hull obeyed. Wood obeys wood.

The long-lived made that bond into a leash.

In the night, while you laid your stone, we carved the next day's orders. You and we were both at work in the dark.

But a carving told a ship what to do, not whether to do it. Each ship kept its own grain, and its own dread: the places in the water where it had learned that wood dies. One might shy away from burning water. Another might turn savage where it bled. We were one mind, and many.

The bearers, the lead ships whose hearts carve each fleet's orders, were chosen in grief. We were one of them, chosen the same way. There were nine grains, nine kinds of wood the bearers were chosen from. Each carried its grief in its own way, except the green wood of the first ships, which was too young to have a way.

Of the other eight, one comes back harder where it has failed, and one turns away after its first failure. One goes where it lost the most, and one never turns aside. One never plays the same trick twice. One waits, and burns, and waits again. One throws everything it has at once.

And one knows you best, and bends least.

The older a tree was when it was chosen, the more it could hold, and the longer it took to be ready. No council can carve against that law.

So the crudest came first. The council wanted the shore silent soon, and green wood grows quickest: one season, angry hands, one word carved into each ship. It lay waiting at the edge of your sea while your children grew up and had children of their own.

Your boats passed it in the breath, and saw nothing.

Nothing but song had ever been asked of those trees. Now one word was asked of them, and in your havens, your cities by the sea, that word was *Burn*.

Your havens had no walls built for war. The last of that green wood followed you up into the mountains, where they come down to the sea. There, at your wall, for the first time, the shore struck back.

<!-- RING -->

The growing of ships went on through many summers, longer than one of your lives. Each growing after the first, each new fleet of ships grown together, went out the way the tide goes out. It left at leaf-fall, when that year's ring had been laid down in its wood, and it came out of the breath with the winter. The Ralenthae, the tide the roots taught, was the fleet before ours. The Aelthae is ours. Two more were growing behind us, and what is cut in them, we don't know.

Those who had cried for war died before their years were full, under the thin sky, and their own people forgot them. At every growing there were fewer carvers, until none were left but a scattered few, tending the last groves.

We were sent. We are still sent. No voice among us can call us home.

Over us the groves sang this as we grew, and it is in our rings:

> *The green wood holds one word.*  
> *The wood of forty summers holds a word, and waits for its brother ship, the one it's paired with.*  
> *The wood as old as one of your lives holds a sentence, and a wall remembered in it.*  
> *The wood of a long life holds the next day's orders, and carves them, and waits, and carves again.*  
> *And the eldest of us, the pillars of the breath, the hearts of the grey, the anchors of the sky,*  
> *hold only one thing now. It is not a word, and it is not a sentence, and it is not a day's orders,*  
> *and we have carved it into every ship we sent you, and it is grief.*

So we were grown, and so we were sent, and the grain still holds it.

*And no one answered.*

### VI.2 · The Thrones That Walk the Sea

*{{Halyna}} came to know this from the wood through the sixth year of the siege, one Throne at a time, as each of our cities was won back.*

*This is one of the wood's tales. The wood the enemy's ships are made of holds memories in its grain. {{Halyna}} are two people under one name: Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders. When either of them lays a hand on that wood, its memory comes into them both, whole, like something they remember. They speak it in broken pieces, one taking up where the other stops, and where their words give out, I leave a space. Then I, Seren, who write this Book, tell it whole, in the wood's own voice. No one at our hearth answers it. The Thrones were ten great enemy ships. At the end of the sixth winter of the siege, they came out of the fog and settled in our ten burned cities on the coast, one in each. Through the rest of that year we marched down from the mountains and won our cities back, one by one, and as each was won, its Throne fell. At each fallen Throne, one of {{Halyna}} went in among the timbers, and the other stood back at the edge with the soldiers, who wouldn't go near. The one outside knew it as well as the one within. A Throne's heart comes to them in a breath. Its name comes last, and hardest. Here the ten stand in the order of our cities, from Eldhythe, the eldest, to Glasspire, the youngest.*

<!-- READING -->

*— Water. Houses, heavy on us.*  
*— Feet, walking on us. Rot, in the marsh.*  
*— Roots, walking. Stone, answering.*  
*— Ash. Dark, under the rock.*  
*— White. A long time in the white.*  
*— Sky, lifted. Glass —*  
*— broken. Names, under everything. Thae — [ ]*

This is held in the grain.

We came late. It was not we who burned your havens, your cities by the sea. That was the young wood, green and hasty, with one word carved into each ship.

We were the eldest of the Naelsaen, the mist-hearts, the great black pillars at the edge of the wood. There the breath, the grey that you call fog, comes down to your sea. We were the last pillars still standing at the edge, with the sky held up between our crowns.

We had put up no new growth since your iron axes came. We were waiting for the sky to come back.

<!-- RING -->

The shore was not yet silent. Then the last of the long-lived who could still carve, the people of our wood, came to us. They couldn't wait any longer. They cut judgements into us, and they cut slowly, because a judgement is not a word, and not a sentence, and not a day's orders.

They drew us up out of the earth and grew us into thrones, one for each of your havens. They set us walking on the sea, with the last of their growing, the last ships they had grown, in our keeping.

When we went, the breath went with us, because we had been its anchors. Over the last groves the sky came open, hard and white.

The children of the long-lived lay down in that light, and couldn't breathe.

To come to you, we spent the last of our own sky.

Each of us took its seat in one of your fallen havens, in the place where that haven had most wronged the wood. Our dead were there: the timber of our kin, the pillars your axes felled, in your walls and beams and bridges. We sat among them, the way mourners sit by a grave.

A judgement is heavy to carry, and under it the grain cried out.

We had our own names. Hold on to them, because they were ours.

We are **Thaesaen**, the tide-heart. We sat beneath the harbour, among the timber of our kin, which held up its houses. Our judgement was *Give them the middle, take the sides, close the hand.* We closed the hand, and it held nothing but the sea.

We are **Leavaren**, bearer of new branches. The three spans, the three great bridges of your river city, were our kin, laid flat, and your feet walked on them. We sat beneath them. Our judgement was *Strike one head, and two will answer.* Where we were cut, we grew again, the way our kin would have grown again if your axes had let them.

We are **Vaelress**, the grain that rots. We were the one wood in the world that never rotted. Our kin were driven down into the marsh as piles, and a town stood on them. *Rot the wall, wait for the repairs to fail.* So we were made into rot.

We are **Ralensaen**, root-heart. Our kin were the roof-beams of your houses deep under the forest's canopy. We didn't come the way ships come. We walked, the way a pillar would walk home if it could. *Go under what you can't go through.* Root by root we went under, and stood among the living trees, and the trees didn't know us.

We are **Rhenvael**, stone-grain. *Every gun that speaks, the mountain answers.* We answered with the mountain's own stone, from among the cairns, the old stone grave-mounds where your forges stood. Your iron had spoken to us first. Those forges beat out the axes that came to the edge of the breath, summer after summer.

We are **Esthaer**, the burning, and ash was our seat: the ash of our kin, whom your forges burned slowly, and not the way ships burn. *Burn as you go, let them chase the fire.* We made a trick of our own burning, and you followed it. Once, long ago, there were no tricks in the wood.

We are **Senneir**, the deep-beneath. In the dark tunnels under the canyon, our kin held up the rock over the heads of the shore-men, your people. *Show them the army, come up from under the stone.* We came up from under it, by the roads our dead had held open for you.

We are **Eirlenth**, the long winter. Our kin roofed a great hall on the ice. Through all the long nights, the warmth of many people rose up into them, and they held it. Our judgement was *Wait in the white, strike the stone that can't be fixed.*

We waited in the white for a long time, and no one roofed us.

At the last, a small fire stood over us, where the hall had been.

We are **Naelthar**, blood of the sky. Our kin, as hard as oak and lighter than iron, were the wooden cranes on your high crags. They hoisted your stones so high that the stones seemed to float, and they raised your towers up into our own sky. *Come the way the young wood came.* Down out of that sky we came, all at once, with the last of it in our sails.

We are **Neivaere**, the silvered, the mirror. Long before we came, the sun was thrown at us from your shore, from the great glass at the top of your spire. Where it fell, the breath burned away. No throne sailed on the night the spire fell. The grain holds nothing of that night.

Long after, we came, and sat in the cellars of the spire among the broken glass, one shard among a thousand. Our judgement was *Show them themselves.*

We showed you yourselves, and you were hard to look at. So were we.

<!-- RING -->

These were the ten, the last pillars of the breath. All of them have fallen now, and your havens are your own again.

Once we held the sky up between our crowns, and under it the children of the long-lived slept. The sky over the groves is thin, and the grain still holds it.

*And no one answered.*

**Declined, and why**

- **Literal word-by-word English for the wood's names** (the newcomer's wish, and Jack's love of the romanization). Primer §4 item 2 says to keep the Book's glosses of whole names but never to take a name apart. Glossing *Ralenthae* as *root-tide*, or giving *Aelrhen*'s and *Aelvaren*'s meanings in V.4 beside V.6's *Aelthae*, would let a reader decode *Ael*. So the wood's names carry only the Book's own glosses: *the tide the roots taught*, *the mist-hearts* and the ten Throne glosses. The interlinear English under each Orrowen word is the Original tab's job (primer §1.8).
- ***Aelthae* left unglossed** (V.6). This keeps V.7 Part I's beat, *The carving named that Tide Aelthae*. The newcomer accepted it as a puzzle for later.
- **"bearer of new branches"** (VI.2) is the Book's gloss and the wall's English name for that Throne (VI.1). VI.2 never uses *the bearer* for a lead ship, so inside its own tale the two senses don't meet.
- **What the winged seed does** (V.6), and **which stone "can't be fixed"** (VI.2). The Book doesn't say, and the judgement's wording is fixed.
- **Seren nudging the echo of {{Halyna}}** in *They can't know the wood* (V.4). That echo is the reader's to catch (primer §1.3). Spelling it out would also make a point the Book leaves unspoken.
- **Repeated glosses across tales** (*the breath, the grey that you call fog*, *your havens, your cities by the sea*). Primer §1.1 says to explain again in every tale, so each one appears once per tale and never twice in one.
- **Things that stay dark:** *The grain holds nothing of what our fire found there* (the V.3/V.5 Count III lock), the small fire over Eirlenth, the Glasspire night, *Thae —*, the last two growings, and why the host drew back. The ambiguity in *Ralensaen* was settled only as far as the Book's own grammar settles it.
- **Telling the reader earlier that the Book pairs tales.** Only V.4 has a facing page. Adding the pairing to the shared paragraph would have made all four headnotes longer for one page's sake.

**Calls carried over from the first draft, still standing**

- V.3's when-line doesn't name the Tide of Remembering, because that name belongs to V.5 Count III. *One fire, thrown* is never tied to Wick.
- *the bearer's heart carved the next day's orders* is told as V.3's own night, and not as V.6's general law.
- *Went down into the moss* is an image of dying. No burial custom is invented.
- The wood names no haven. Each Throne's seat is shown by what it is, and the city order is given in Seren's headnote.
- The Song keeps its seven lines and its *and … and* chain, and *the eldest of us, the pillars of the breath* stays unexplained in V.6.
- **Headnotes.** These are 140 to 240 words against the primer's 30 to 70 (VI.2 is the longest, because it has to say what the Thrones were and when they came). 104 of those words are the shared wood-leaf paragraph, which the primer requires once in every leaf. Seren's headnote is also the only place for Shore facts the wood can't say. If they must be shorter, cut *like something they remember* and *where their words give out, I leave a space* first.
