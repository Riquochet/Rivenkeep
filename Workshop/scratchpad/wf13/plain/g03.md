### I.4 · The Rite of the Cornerstone

*Told in the first spring of the siege, our long stand against the enemy's fleets at the Keep, the old fortress on the mountain ridge. It tells of the Fourteen Nights, as we call our first two weeks there, in the deep of that first winter.*

*This tale is my own. I'm Seren Two-Inks, and I write this Book. Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders, go by one name between them, {{Halyna}}, and they took me into their household as its third member. In the Fourteen Nights I was twelve, a child of the Stonewrights, our guild of master builders, and nobody trusted me with anything but water. Whoever holds the old gate-stone by the fire, our tale-stone, is the one who tells.*

Hold the stone, Seren. I tell myself that every time.

A moving light woke me. Rhyna was up, and in the same breath Halvard picked up the lamp. They're a bonded pair, two of our builders joined as one from birth, and they move together like that. I didn't call out. I watched the light go down and away from me, turn by turn, until it was only a spark at the bottom of a well.

That was the third night after the cry. On our first evening at the Keep, the Captain, the soldier who leads us, tore his cloak in two and raised half of it over the gate as our banner. Our whole guild sat silent in the knee-high grass of the old courtyard until {{Halyna}} raised us to our feet with one great cry.

Halvard and Rhyna come of two different families, but they've been a pair ever since they shared one cradle as babies. Over it, Rhyna's mother used to sing an old rhyme handed down in her family. Rhyna is descended from {{Aldwena}}, the bonded pair who laid the capstone of our inner gate, the great stone across the top of the gateway. They laid it long ago, in the First Days, when the first builders raised this Keep. Nobody had ever asked what the rhyme meant.

> *Under the hall and under the floor,*  
> *under the stair that goes no more,*  
> *down where the rope let the first stone go,*  
> *the first two keep what the first two know.*

Now that we were at the Keep, the rhyme told Rhyna where to look. There's an old stair here that winds down into the rock and stopped leading anywhere long ago: the stair that goes no more. Below it, a shaft drops on into the dark. Halvard went down the shaft on a rope. Rhyna paid the rope out to him, and neither of them said a word.

As they told it to me, a chest of black stone stood at the bottom, with dust a finger deep on its lid. Halvard swept the lid clean with his sleeve, and there was a mark cut into it.

It was the mark of {{Aldwena}}.

That was their own mark. The same mark is cut on the underside of the tale-stone, the stone my hands are on now. It's the old capstone {{Aldwena}} laid, and it takes two people to turn it over. We found it lying face down in the gateway when we came.

Inside the chest lay the Rite, a ceremony of the first builders, written on leaves of slate. There were slates about building for battle, too, and the founders' annal, the first builders' record of their years. A staff lay along the bottom of the chest, and three stone flasks stood in a corner. Halvard shook each one by the lamp.

Nothing moved in them.

At the fire, {{Halyna}} read the slates for their meaning, because the two of them know the old hand, the first builders' writing. One of them read a line, then came a breath, then the other read the next. I put my water bucket down and forgot all about it.

On the slates underneath, the pauses between their lines grew long. There were walls sloped so a shot would glance off, and gates set crooked so that a battering ram couldn't strike them square. There were courses, the rows of stone in a wall, laid so that if the wall was ever broken through, the breach would fall outward. The first builders had known war. The slates don't say what they fought against.

The Rite needed three oils, and no one alive had ever made them.

For the cedar oil, Stannard of the ridge led {{Halyna}} through the snow to the stand of cedars where the first staff had been cut. Stannard is one of the True Men, the Captain's own company of twelve, and a quarryman from this very ridge. The three of them wouldn't cut down a living tree, so Stannard went along the fallen trunks, knocking on each one with his knuckle and listening.

"I know which of these will hold."

They boiled the heartwood down for its oil here at this hearth. On a wet night you can still smell the cedar breathing out of its stones.

The second oil was bloom, the masons' name for the pale crust that grows on worked stone when nobody has tended it for generations. They call it bloom because it flowers where no one comes.

So we children came out with old chisels and bent along the forgotten stones, scraping it off, like gleaners picking over a field after the harvest. It was the first work I'd ever been given that wasn't water.

My fingers were raw from it.

The third oil was myrrh, a sweet-smelling gum, and the mountain couldn't give us that. {{Idrenna}}, the oldest bonded pair in our guild, had carried one flask of it the whole length of the Road, our month-long march over the mountains to the Keep. They'd carried it for the guild's dead, but up on the pass the ground was frozen too hard to bury them. When Rhyna warmed the gum into the cedar oil, the whole hall went quiet, the way a street goes quiet when the dead are carried through it. It was the Captain who would be anointed with the myrrh of the Road's dead.

I knew who it had been carried for. One of those dead was a boy of the guild who had walked the Road beside me, knotting a net as he went. He never reached the Keep.

All night {{Idrenna}}'s empty flask stood on the hearthstone, and none of us would touch it. Late that night the Captain came in off the wall with mortar up to his wrists. He looked at the flask, and went out again.

On the fourteenth day a thin sun came out, the first since the evening the banner went up. In the ward, the courtyard inside our walls, lay the new capstone for the inner gate. Stannard had quarried it, and {{Orvenna}}, our wall-masters, had dressed it smooth. Beside it sat {{Idrenna}}, with the flasks in their laps. The Captain stood where the slate said the keeper of the work must stand, with his hand on the stone and his face to the people.

He didn't like it. He stood there.

Above the gate the torn cloak lifted once in the wind, and fell. I could hear snow-water running in the old gutters.

Then {{Idrenna}} stood up, the one leaning on the other, and with their four old hands they tipped each flask over his bowed head. That winter their hands shook at everything else. On the flasks they didn't shake.

One of them named each oil, and the other gave its meaning:

"Cedar, for endurance. Granite-bloom, for strength. Myrrh, for the burden of command."

The oil ran down into his collar, and he didn't wipe it away. We children at the front held up our raw hands to one another and smiled, for the first time since the mountain passes.

Then {{Idrenna}} held out the staff of the guild. The slate names it this way: *a rod of polished wood capped with the finest polished ashlar, borne by every overseer of a great Stonewright work since the founding*. Ashlar is smooth-dressed stone. Every great work has had its own staff ever since, and this one was the first of them all. The Captain looked at it for a long while before he put out his hands.

Their four hands laid it in his two.

With that staff he was named Commander of Rivenkeep, as this Keep is called. Until that day his only rank had been captain of one small company. He stood still in all the noise.

That night I held the lamp while {{Halyna}} cut their mark and their vow into the underside of the new stone. One of them held the chisel, and the other struck. Their mark isn't mine to draw. Beside the vow they cut words that aren't a vow: the Title, the five things the Captain had written in coal on his torn cloak, copied stroke for stroke. He'd written them in the course-hand, the square letters of our own tongue that masons cut in stone.

**IN MEMORY OF OUR HOME, OUR FAMILIES, OUR GOD, OUR FREEDOMS, OUR PEACE.**

It was the Captain's own handwriting, large and slow.

Neither of them had tidied a single stroke.

The True Men raised the stone into the gate with its mark facing down, hidden, just as {{Aldwena}}'s had been hidden under the old one. The Captain leaned the staff in the corner of the gatehouse, because his work was the wall and the war. Then he went back out to the wall, with the unwritten half of his cloak on his back.

No one has moved the staff since. If you doubt the tale, go and look at the dust on it.

When the stone was set and the tools were cleaned, {{Halyna}} sent me down alone with the lamp to put the slates back in the chest. I went down, turn by turn, the way I had watched their light go. I lifted the empty flasks to set them back in their corner.

There was one more leaf of slate.

Its letters were large and careful, the way a child's are, and the way mine still are. I carried it up inside my shirt, cold against my skin. At the fire {{Halyna}} read it to me. It was the leaf of the youngest apprentice of the First Days.

"When the stone is set and the tools are cleaned, we sit at the hearth, and one of us lays a tale."

"We lay it the way a course is laid on a wall, one course each night, so the wall and the telling rise together."

<!-- HALYNA END -->

So the custom of this hearth is older than the havens, our ten cities on the coast. We didn't make it up. We forgot it, and found it again at the bottom of a chest.

The next night Kael the Counter, one of the True Men, laid the first tale. He wouldn't sit to tell it. He'd had enough of sitting that winter, he said. It was about the Captain.

The people called him Commander. In his own heart he was still only the Captain.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

### I.5 · The Naming of the Rivenmen

*Told at the end of the sixth winter of the siege, our long stand against the enemy's fleets at Rivenkeep, the old fortress on its riven ridge, a mountain ridge split in two. It was the night before we marched down from the Keep to take back our cities on the coast.*

*Voss who asked, as we call him, laid this tale. He grew up in Eldhythe, the oldest of our harbour cities, in his grandmother's harbour-house, a tavern on the quay. He's one of the True Men now, the Captain's own company, and commands the harbour battery, a group of guns on our wall. Whoever holds the old gate-stone by the fire is the one who tells, and we say a tale is laid, like a stone in a wall, because a laid tale stays.*

Give me the stone. I've owed this hearth an answer for a long time.

Near the end of the first winter I buried a friend on the Keep's east face. The ground there is too hard for a spade, so we bury our dead under cairns, heaps of piled stones. When we were boys, he and I used to turn the skipping rope together on the quay at Eldhythe while the little ones jumped. I laid the last stone on him myself.

His name was Jory.

That night, here at this fire, I asked a question. I was cold, and I meant it as a hard one.

"Why do we fight for ice and stone?"

Stannard of the ridge is one of the True Men, and a quarryman from this very mountain. He knocked once on the hearthstone with his knuckle, the way he tests any stone before he trusts it, and answered me.

"It's not the ice I fight for."

That was a good answer, but it was his. Mine has taken me the whole war to cut into shape.

A slow answer is like a slow tide, so my grandmother told me. It comes in over the same stones.

Mine began the week after the Rite of the Cornerstone, the ceremony that made the Captain our Commander. That day the whole Keep gathered in the ward, the courtyard inside the walls, under our banner. The banner is half of the Captain's own cloak. On the evening we first reached the Keep he tore his cloak in two, and on one half he wrote in coal: *In memory of our home, our families, our God, our freedoms, our peace.*

I stood in the slush among the soldiers of our companies, with Jory at my shoulder. For once in his life he wasn't whistling. The Captain stood there among us, and the staff of command the Rite had given him stayed in its corner of the gatehouse.

Kael the Counter, the True Man who counts everything, climbed up on the fallen stones by the gate. He had one thing to say, and he said it once.

"The Commander fought."

And the whole guild finished it: all the Stonewrights, our master builders who never take up a weapon. They spoke in one voice, from {{Idrenna}}, the oldest pair among them, down to the smallest child who carried the guild's water:

"The Stonewrights built."

That was all the speech there was, and it was enough. We looked at one another, soldier and mason. We weren't two peoples sharing a ruin. We were one, the way two courses of stone become one wall once the mortar sets.

That day we laid the Keep's name, Rivenkeep, on the whole land. The grey sails, the enemy's fleets that came out of the fog at sea, had burned our havens, our ten cities along the coast. But every haven the grey sails hold is Rivenkeep, and the edge of the sea is the edge of the Keep. That's a vow.

We named ourselves the Rivenmen, after the Keep. Before that day we were Shorelanders, the people of the Shorelands, our long coast where the mountains run down to the sea. We still call our fathers Shorelanders, because that's what they were. But when we speak of ourselves, we just say *we*. It began in that courtyard.

All that day we went up one by one to the stone at the foot of the pike, the long spear the banner flies from. Each of us laid a hand on it and swore, the way men make their ropes fast on the quay at evening. Jory went up just before me, and came back down whistling. By dark the Keep was held fast.

We swore to take back every stone. Not one stone that wasn't ours. But every one that was.

The oldest among us never liked the new name, though they never said so. They still murmur "the Shorelands" into their shawls.

This winter ten great enemy ships came out of the fog and settled down in our havens like herons standing in the shallows. The men on the wall call them the Mystarchs. After they came, I asked old Ebba of Fenholm, the marsh town, why she kept the old name. She didn't look up from the fire. She just held her hands out to it.

"It's a name to come home to, when the last wall stands and the last Mystarch lies broken."

Then she said one word more, to the fire and not to me.

"Perhaps."

I didn't understand it then. I think I do now.

Four nights ago she sat up late on the end of that bench, nearest the coals, with her shawl pulled up to her chin. We went off to our blankets and left her sleeping. In the night the fire burned down, and nobody built it up again.

In the morning she didn't wake.

At dusk we laid her on the east face, beside the soldiers buried there. {{Idrenna}} said the prayer for the dead in two halves, one of them beginning it and the other finishing it. Marl, her grandson, one of the True Men, laid the last stone on her, and over it he said the old name, "the Shorelands." None of us corrected him. The Captain tied a strip to a pike above her. He cut it from the half-cloak he still wears, the half with nothing written on it.

It was Ebba who gave this hearth its answer, the two words we all say when a tale is done. On the very first night a tale was told here, nobody knew what to say. She said *We remember* into the quiet, and the hearth took the words from her. Most nights she began them, a breath before the rest of us. For these four nights, since she died, there's been a gap at the front of our answer, the width of one old voice. None of us has tried to fill it.

Now here's my answer, and you can weigh it. I asked my question for Jory's sake, and he never heard it asked. We'd laid him under his cairn that same day.

On the east wall I once said:

"We do not fight because we are right. We fight because our children are behind these walls."

It's true, but it isn't the whole of it. I have no child of my own, and still I said *our*.

From the east tower you can see the keeps we've raised in this war, small forts standing along the coast like posts along a quay. A crimson pennant flies on every one, the colour of the Captain's cloak. But a keep isn't a place to stay in forever. It's a place to go out from.

We don't fight for ice and stone. Every stone of our land is Rivenkeep, and Rivenkeep is home. Under the banner we swore to bring every stone of it home.

Old Ebba stays behind on the east face. But in his pack Marl carries the stone she brought out of Fenholm, a stone from her own house there.

Tomorrow we go down to the havens.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*The hearth waited a breath for Ebba to begin.*

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*
