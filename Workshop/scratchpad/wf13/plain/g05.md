## BOOK THREE · THE BOOK OF THE HAVENS

*Ten havens, the cities of our coast, and ten men to tell them: what we had, and how we walked away from it.*

### III.1 · Of Their Raising

*Told in the first autumn of the siege, the first year the enemy's fleets came against us here at the Keep, the old fortress high on a mountain ridge above the sea. It tells how our ten cities on the coast, the havens, were built long ago, and what each one was like before it fell.*

*The True Men, the Captain's own company of twelve, laid this tale. Kael the Counter held the old gate-stone, as whoever tells at this hearth must. Each man stood to answer for his own haven, as all twelve once stood under the banner. (We say a tale is laid, like a stone in a wall, because a laid tale stays, and every teller ends with the same words.) I, Seren, who write this Book, read out from the havens' old records what no one alive remembered. {{Halyna}} had found them in the chest I carried up the mountain. That's one name for two people, Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders.*

Give me the stone. No, I'll stand. At a roll-call, men stand.

At first light I went up the east tower. From up there you can still see the coast where our cities stood, stretching away into the haze. Not one hearth on it is sending up smoke.

Listen now to the names of the havens, and hold on to them, because they're ours.

I won't tell another man's city for him. I'll call each haven, and its own man will answer, and swear what he'll do when we win his city back and it comes home. I'm the oath-keeper. The True Men swear their oaths to me, and I hold them.

*First, as he asked me to, I read from the rolls, the old written records of our builders' guild, which tell how each haven was founded. Our forefathers were builders, not sailors, and they were afraid of the open water. They built where the mountains run down to the sea in long slopes, and they paved roads across the mountains like veins. Every haven's roll begins with the day its first row of stones went down and a polished rod was cut for whoever would oversee the work. It's always the same line:* On this day the first course was laid, and a staff was cut for the work. *The clerks' handwriting is small and patient, as if they hated to waste a page. Every wall of all ten havens was the work of the Stonewrights, our guild of master builders. Ten havens, and ten staffs.*

**Eldhythe, the Eldest Haven.**

Corlen of the harbour. For fifty years I watched the evening tide bring the boats home past my quay, slowly, to the warmest harbour-houses on the coast, the inns by the water where the ship-masters sat by the fire. When the first boat of ours comes home, I'll be the one to steer it in.

*Then Corlen, the eldest of the Twelve, sat down. He wet a finger and held it up to feel the wind, the way he does on the wall whenever the enemy's ships attack. There's no wind in this hall. The young men at the back laughed. He told them what he tells the men at his guns when they're too eager to fire: "Wait. The sea hasn't finished."*

**Tidesmeet, where the river meets the tide.**

Tamm of the three spans. Tidesmeet stood on both banks of the river, where it meets the tide coming in from the sea, and three great spans, three stone bridges, held it together across the water.

*Tamm set three stones out by the fire in their order, the way he sets them out by his blanket every night.*

One span is just a plank. Three make a city. I'll set these out again on the bank where the spans stood.

**Fenholm, the town that was always sinking.**

Marl the fen-born. Fenholm stood on wooden posts in a fen, a wide marsh, and it sank a hand's breadth every lifetime. So every generation laid a new course of stone, a new row, on top of the old one. A fen-man who can't laugh at that had better go and live on a hill.

Old Ebba, my grandmother, laid the top course of our house. I've got one of its stones in my pack, and I'd carry her too, if she'd let me. That's her there by the fire, nodding. When Fenholm comes home, she wants me to lay her stone on the old course.

**Holtward, the keep under the canopy.**

Aske of the canopy. Holtward was a fortress in the forest, hidden so deep among the trees, under the roof of their leaves, that a traveller could pass right beneath its walls and never see one. We wouldn't cut a single bough. Every autumn we swept the leaves off the wall-walk, the path along the top of the wall, by hand.

*By the hearth lay the night's firewood: planks from the enemy's wrecked ships, which come out of the grey, the great fog bank at sea. The waves wash the planks in on the pebble beach below our wall, and wood is scarce up here on the ridge, so we burn them. Aske laid his hand on every plank before it went on the fire.*

These were trees once. While I live, no axe will come into Holtward.

**Carnhold, the stronghold of the cairns.**

Garvel of the smithies. We cut Carnhold out of the rock among the cairns of the first quarrymen, the mounds of stones piled over their graves. It was a town of forges, and you could tell which forge was at work by the ring of its hammer. I listen to every gun on this wall the way a mother listens to her child's cough. A gun that rings true shoots true. But at an anvil in Carnhold, I'll make something that isn't a gun, and hear it ring.

**Emberhythe, the forge on the burning shore.**

Harl of the burning shore. Emberhythe was a forge-town too. It stood where a smoking mountain runs down into the sea, and the ground there was so warm that our children ran barefoot in the snow. I'll have my shore back. And I'll burn the grey, and the sea too, if it would catch fire.

**Sandreach, the citadel carved in the canyon wall.**

Ulden of the galleries. Sandreach had no harbour. We called it a haven all the same, because it sheltered us. It was dug, not built: gallery below gallery, long halls cut one under another into the red rock of a canyon wall. Every night until the havens fell, I slept with the whole mountain for my roof and my ear to the floor, and I heard it hold. I'll sleep under that roof again.

**Rimewatch, the watch on the frozen mere.**

Hesk of the mere. Rimewatch stood by a mere, a broad lake that froze hard every winter. Its great hall was built out on the lake itself, so all winter long it stood on the ice, and the town feasted under that roof and was warm. Every dusk a taper, a thin candle, went from window to window lighting the lamps, and the fishers out on the ice steered home by them. My mother kept those lamps. One of them is burning on our north wall tonight. I'll light it on the ice again.

**Highreach, the tower of the floating stones.**

Lanner of the crags. Highreach stood on crags, high cliffs of rock. They were so high that every course of its stone went up by crane, and the stones hung on the wind the way hawks hang over a field. I can smell a wind a day before it comes. A boy from those crags can whistle one up before he can walk. I'll climb the highest crag again, and whistle for the wind.

**And Glasspire, the last and proudest.**

Pellow of the spire. Seven generations built Glasspire, and it caught the sun like a blade of ice. At the very top stood the great glass, which threw the sunlight a day's sail out to sea, into the grey. My father ground it. What I carried of it up the mountain, packed in straw, I've ground into our far-glasses, the spyglasses we keep watch with on this wall. When Glasspire comes home, mine will be the first eye on it.

*Then Pellow, the youngest of the Twelve, held his glass up to the fire, the way he holds it up to everything, his supper included. To the children at the front he said, "Look close."*

Ten cities. Ten harbours, if you count Sandreach the way Ulden does. Ten peoples who called themselves one nation, the Shorelanders.

Twelve of us answered under the banner, on our first evening here, when the Captain tore his cloak and raised half of it over the wall. Ten have sworn tonight. Stannard of the ridge swore nothing. He's a quarryman from this very ridge the Keep stands on, and the ridge was never a haven. He knocked once on the hearthstone with his knuckle. He won't trust a stone until he's knocked on it.

I've called ten havens tonight, and I hold ten oaths. I have no haven.

The Captain found me on a road.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

### III.2 · Of Their Fall

*Told in the second winter of the siege, in the year of the Second Tide, as the men on the wall called the enemy's fleets of our second year. It tells how our ten cities on the coast, the havens, fell in the twenty months before we reached the Keep, this old fortress on the mountain. It also tells of the Road, the long march that brought us up here.*

*The True Men, the Captain's own company of twelve, laid this tale too, each for the fall of his own city. They told it in the order the cities fell, Corlen first for Eldhythe and Pellow last for Glasspire. (We say a tale is laid, like a stone in a wall, because a laid tale stays, and every teller ends with the same words.) It was a night of hard frost. The guns were still cooling from the day's fighting, and the mortar of the day's building hadn't set. Kael the Counter, the True Men's oath-keeper, held the old gate-stone, as whoever tells at this hearth must, and called the men. I, Seren, who write this Book, spoke for our builders' guild.*

Give me the stone. No, I'll stand. This isn't a tale to sit down for.

Last autumn you heard the names of our havens, and you held on to them. Hold on to them now while they fall, and don't look away, because we won't look away while we tell it.

In the last spring of peace, twenty months before we reached this Keep and raised our banner over it, the swallows nested in the arches of the havens' gates. No one in the havens ever broke a nest. The havens had no warships. Their walls were built for feast days and for weather. Their gates were wide, and they stood open at night.

Then, without any warning, the dark sails came out of the grey, the great bank of fog that lies out at sea. No messenger came, or none that anyone could understand. All that came was a knocking out of the fog, like thunder that went on and on. The soldiers on this wall call it the drums. We looked for a captain on those ships, and a crew, even one man at one rope. No one stood on the decks. I swear to that.

So we called it the ghost fleet, and whoever sent it we called the Mystaeri, after the mist. A man has to call his death by some name.

Those sails were simpler than the ones that come against us now. Each one came alone, and one was all it needed. No haven sent to another for help, because none had any help to send.

*Then each man laid his hand on the stone in his turn, Corlen of the harbour first. He's the eldest of the Twelve, and when the men at his guns are too eager to fire, he tells them, "Wait. The sea hasn't finished."*

**Eldhythe.**

On the first morning of that spring, the dark sails came in at the harbour mouth on the rising tide. From dawn to dusk the harbour burned: first the boats at their moorings, then the harbour-houses, the inns on the quay where the ship-masters of the whole coast used to sit by the fire.

*Then he waited, the way he waits at his guns, and the hall waited with him.*

In the evening the wind blew the smoke across the city. The smoke hid the harbour, so the city never saw it go. Eldhythe held out for three months. On its last evening the tide came in over the ashes, slowly, the way it always came, and the sea hadn't finished.

*Corlen took his hand off the stone so that Tamm could lay his there. It was the last time he held it.*

**Tidesmeet.**

It took three days. From the high road we watched its three spans, the great bridges that held the city together across the river, go down into the water, and we heard them go. The upper one went, and then the lower. The middle one stood longest. We waited for it, and it went. That night I climbed down in the dark and took a stone from each of them. I still have all three.

**Fenholm.**

It went under in one night, as if the fen, the marsh it stood in, had only been waiting. "A town is just the newest course on the old," we used to say, and laugh. That night nobody laughed. My grandmother, Old Ebba, carried her course-stone out on her own back. It was the stone from the top course of our house. She wouldn't give it to me until the fen had closed over the last course of the town.

**Holtward.**

It burned among its trees, and the leaves came down burning onto the wall-walk we used to sweep by hand. I laid my hand on the last tree still standing. Its bark was as warm as a living hand.

**Carnhold.**

It burned in its smithies. In the street of the smiths I heard the hammers stop, one forge and then the next. The last anvil rang on for a while, alone.

Then it did not ring.

**Emberhythe.**

Emberhythe held out for five months on its burning shore. By then five havens had fallen, and their survivors had gathered into one long column. The column spent that winter with us, sleeping warm on the bare ground, because the ground there was always warm. When the city fell, one of our masons carried a charred plank out in his pack. I told him to burn it.

**Sandreach.**

Sandreach was dug into a canyon wall in galleries, long halls cut one under another into the rock. It was taken from us one gallery at a time. Each night I lay with my ear to the floor, and I heard the gallery above me go before any runner came down to tell us. At the end we came out in the dark at the foot of the canyon. Every night since, I've slept on the open ground, with my ear to it.

**Rimewatch.**

It was taken when the first ice came. Our great hall stood out on the ice of the mere, our broad lake, and it burned there with its lamps still lit. The fire went from window to window, the way my mother's taper, a thin candle, used to go at dusk, lighting the lamps one by one for the fishers out on the ice. It burned the way it had always shone.

I went into the smoke, to her window, and took down her lamp. It was lit.

Then I turned for the shore. The ice was new, only a night old. All the way across the lake it spoke under me, cracking far off and then near. Once the wind blew the flame down to the wick, and I cupped my hand around it until it stood up again. Behind me the roof fell in. In front of me there was no light but the one in my hand.

The ice held.

I carried the lamp out lit. Every dusk since, I've lit it on the north wall, and the night-watch steer by it, as the fishers did.

Someone has to keep a light.

**Highreach.**

The wind comes before the sails. I smelled the grey on it a whole day ahead of them, and there was nothing to do with that day but leave. We came down the crag-stairs in the dark, the steps cut into the cliffs, with the children on our backs. Behind us the cranes stood burning on the crags.

**Glasspire.**

With the first snows, it went down in one night into its own cellars, the spire and the great glass at its top together. The dark hole beneath it is still open. Some who fled say a shadow stood out in the fog that night, bigger than any ship. I was on the last stair of the spire, with the glass coming down around me in pieces, and the snow coming down with it.

I looked close. I saw nothing.

*Then Pellow, who holds his far-glass up to everything, sat down with it in his lap, and didn't hold it up. He gave his place at the stone to me.*

On the last day of every haven, when there was nothing more a mason's hands could do, the Stonewrights, our guild of builders who never take up a weapon, went out by the landward gate. They carried their chisels in their packs, and nothing else. The masters, the guild's senior builders, went last. They had cleaned their trowels, as they did after every day's work, and wrapped them in sacking for the road.

The Stonewrights did not fight. They simply walked, and I was among them.

They didn't carry the staffs. Every haven had its own staff, the overseer's polished rod, cut for it on the day its first course of stone was laid. The masters leaned each haven's staff against its falling wall. They did it the way a woman banks the fire on her last night in her mother's house. She heaps ash over the coals to keep them till morning, the same as every night. It's the guild's law: A staff is cut for its work, and never leaves its work.

There were ten staffs, and not one came up the mountain.

*Kael laid his hands on the stone again, and told of the Road.*

When Glasspire was gone, the column turned up the mountain. It had gathered haven by haven, as each one fell, until there was no haven left behind it. We were a month in the passes.

On the night of the pass, the coldest night of the Road, many of the children died. Some of the old died with them. They had given the children their cloaks.

In the morning the snow was still falling. We got up out of it, as many of us as could get up, and walked.

At the foot of the passes I counted us, and I counted us again at the top. I won't tell you the difference.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*
