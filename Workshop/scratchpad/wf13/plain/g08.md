### V.2 · The Night of the Naming

*Told late in the second summer of the siege, on the evening after the night it tells of. The soldiers on our wall called that year's enemy fleets the Second Tide.*

*I, Seren, who write this Book, held the lamp that night, and the next evening I laid this tale at the Long Hearth, the great fireplace in the hall of our mountain fortress, the Keep. (We say a tale is laid, like a stone in a wall. Whoever lays one holds the old gate-stone by the fire, and ends with the words every teller ends with.) The planks I tell of are still in the lowest vault, and anyone who wants to can go down and lay a hand on them.*

Hold the stone, Seren.

All through that first winter, this hearth laughed at the grey sails. Those are the enemy's ships, which come out of the great fog bank at sea, the same ships that burned our ten cities on the coast before we climbed up here. Kael the Counter used to say, "They've picked the wrong fortress," and I laughed with the rest. Kael is one of the True Men, the twelve sworn to the Captain, who leads us.

One night late in the Second Tide, {{Halyna}} went down to the oldest room under this hall. That's the one name we give to Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders. The room is the vault where the Rite was found, the first builders' ceremony, written on slate, that set the Captain over the whole Keep. I came down behind them with the ink and the lamps. It's as cold down there in summer as in winter. The planks lay in rows, grey on grey, like a field the plough has turned up full of stones.

Fifty-three planks from the first winter lay there, pieces of the enemy's wrecked ships, and every one of them was carved. The runners, the young men who carry word along our wall, had carried them home from wherever the sea gave them up, two runners to a plank, with the salt water running down their arms. The rest of the wreckage went into the soldiers' fires for firewood, or back out with the tide. The wall had a grand name for the carved ones, the tablets, but they were only planks.

{{Halyna}} are the only ones among us who can know what the enemy carves in its wood. When they lay their hands on a carving, what's cut there comes into them whole, the way you know something you remember, without reading it. We call that a knowing, and I can't do it. Every plank but one had given {{Halyna}} a single word. They knew more than that word, but the rest was a forest's thinking, and they had only our people's words to tell it with. I'd written each word on a page of its own. That one plank had given them nothing, though it was carved as finely as any of the others. The sea brought it in a few weeks after the very first.

The planks of the Second Tide, this year's ships, carried two signs each, cut close together, like names crowded onto the stone over an old doorway. Down on the shingle, the pebble beach under our wall, {{Halyna}} had learned to hold both signs at once. Even so, there were gaps in everything they could tell me, and I haven't filled those gaps with guesses. But until that night, nobody had gone back down to the fifty-three old planks, with their one word each, to ask them for anything more.

Summer nights are short and the rows were long, so they started at the two ends, Rhyna by the stair and Halvard at the far wall. I'd never seen them work apart before. They're a bonded pair, two people joined as one from birth, long before they were husband and wife, and a bonded pair works as one. Even that night they weren't far apart: only the length of the vault.

Rhyna picked up the very first plank of all, the one carved with a wave, which gave us our first word from the wood: *shore*. One long edge of it was split, rough and raw. Halvard had the silent plank with him at the far wall, and he picked it up. One long edge of that one was raw too.

I stood between them with the lamp. No wind reaches that far down, and the flame stood straight up in my hand.

Rhyna lifted her plank, and at the far wall Halvard lifted his. It was like lifting a net: raise the near knots and the far ones rise with them, because it's all one net.

Their two faces changed at the same moment.

Then Rhyna spoke, and it wasn't a word. It was a whole order.

<!-- W -->
*To shore. Go as a root goes to water, and wait for no branch.*

At the far end, with the silent plank in his hands, Halvard said it with her, word for word.

After that, every plank spoke. We'd laid them out in the order the sea gave them up, and that night each one gave up its own order, whole. All night {{Halyna}} worked along the rows toward each other, and I went back and forth between them and wrote. By the middle rows my hand ached all the way to the wrist, and I didn't stop. Here are the others, as I wrote them down:

<!-- W: the italic carvings only -->
*Break the wall. First stone, only stone, so strike until it opens or the wood is ash.* That plank was cut with a closed square, their sign for stone, which they call the mute thing. Ships with that order had struck the first wall they touched until they sank, and we'd called them mad.

*Go until you are hit, and where the bark splits, turn away.* That ship had swerved off when it was hit, and none followed it. We'd called it a coward.

*Thin wood goes before the bearer. Thin wood falls, and the bearer keeps up.* The bearer is the chief ship that a fleet follows. The thin wood, the ships at the head of the line, had been told to die first while the bearer kept coming on behind them, and they died first.

*Strike where it flashed. Last flash, only flash.* The flash was our guns firing. They turned on whichever gun had fired last, and we'd called that hunger.

*Wait in the grey, as a seed waits out the frost, and when the stone is still, come.* Those ships waited out in the fog until our wall went still, and came just when we'd stopped looking.

*Go home when wounded. One branch falls and all turn, each by its own water.* At the first loss, those ships scattered, each its own way across the water, and the wall cheered.

The soldiers gave each order a name from its first words, in plainer speech: *To the shore. Break the wall. Wait in the grey.*

When {{Halyna}} met in the middle, the grey light of morning was coming down the stair. One more plank lay at the end of the last row, by itself, charred black at one end. A mason from Emberhythe, our forge-town, had carried it up the mountain after the Fall, the twenty months in which the grey ships burned our cities one after another. He couldn't bear to come away from his city with nothing, and he didn't know what the plank was.

{{Halyna}} held it last of all, each with one hand on the grain, while the lamps went pale in the morning light.

It said *Burn*.

Nobody spoke, and for a long while I didn't write. Then Halvard laid it back at the end of the last row, by itself.

That was all the young wood of the first winter knew: one word to a ship, and inside that word, one order. Each ship said its word at our wall, and said it again, and kept on saying it until it drowned. All that winter we'd laughed that they had picked the wrong fortress.

They hadn't picked anything.

They had been sent.

At full light I picked up the first plank and the silent one. All night my eyes had kept going back to their raw edges. I laid them side by side, raw edge to raw edge.

The broken grain met.

It fitted the way the two halves of a split log fit together. I haven't asked {{Halyna}} what it means, and they haven't told me.

Then the three of us sat down among the planks. The lamps kept burning, and nobody put them out. At last I said this, and {{Halyna}} let it stand:

"I think those ships were the youngest of the wood, and they were sent first because the young grow fastest. The worst of the wood isn't behind us. It's still growing."

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

### V.5 · The Tides, as the Wall Counted Them

*Told at the end of each year of the siege, one count for each year's fleets, six in all.*

*Kael the Counter laid these. He's one of the True Men, the Captain's own company of twelve, and he counts everything twice. The men on our wall call each year's enemy fleets a Tide, and Kael wouldn't tell of any Tide until it was over. (Here, whoever tells holds the old gate-stone by the fire. We say a tale is laid, like a stone in a wall, and every teller ends with the same words.) In the sixth winter he asked me, Seren, who write this Book, to mend all six, the way I'd mended the first tale he ever laid here. I've changed these less than that one.*

Give me the stone. No, I'll stand.

**I · The Hasty Tide**

I count. That's what I'm for.

The first year's fleets were the Hasty Tide. On the first morning there were eight sails, and not one of them kept in line with another. Each one struck at the first wall it touched and kept striking until it sank.

No one stood on their decks. Off the transports, the ships that carried their fighters, came the root-men, warriors like tangled roots, pouring out like spilled kindling. The wall called them treelings, as if a name could make them small.

By noon their fire had slackened, as if they'd used it up, and we learned to say, "The fleet has spent its fire."

A breach opened on the south face and fell outward, away from the people behind it, just as the slates the Keep's first builders left us in a black chest said it should. Everyone behind that breach lived. I swear to that.

All that winter I said at this fire that the grey sails, the enemy's ships out of the fog, had picked the wrong fortress. Every night it went round the benches with the cup, and every man drank to it. We laughed. God forgive us, we laughed.

Jory of Eldhythe, who used to whistle on the east wall, laughed the loudest. His name is first under the east cairns, the piles of stones over our dead on the Keep's east face, where the ground is too hard for a spade.

A fire-ship took him, an enemy ship that came in burning. On the evening we first came to this Keep, the Captain, who leads us, tore his cloak in two. He wrote on one half, and that half is our banner. The other half he wears. The night Jory died, he cut a strip a hand wide from his half and tied it on a pike over Jory's cairn.

It was the first strip.

***Added in Seren's hand:*** *It wasn't the last.*

**II · The Second Tide**

This is the second count, and a harder one.

The second year's fleets, the Second Tide, came in pairs. The front ship took our shot, and the one behind it came in to land.

Off the north point we hit a ship. A mile to the south, another ship's sail went from grey-green to white, the way a man's face goes white, and it turned.

It hadn't seen. It had felt.

Then came the crown-hulls, broad ships carrying winged seed, and the seed came flying in wherever our guns were quiet.

Every fleet follows one ship, its bearer. When we sank a bearer, another ship took up the standard, the way a soldier picks up a fallen flag, and the whole fleet took on the new bearer's temper. I still tell the young gunners: when you pick which ship to sink, you choose which heart you leave them.

Corlen of the harbour was the eldest of the Twelve. Before every holding, the hours when the grey sails attack and the wall must be held, he wet a finger and held it up to the wind. He'd say, "Wait. The sea hasn't finished."

In one seed-fall, a ship came in slowly on the water his guns covered. On his own judgement he kept his guns quiet, to let it come close. He watched the water, and nothing but the water.

The seed came over the wall where the guns were quiet.

His were the quiet ones.

He died at the parapet, still watching the water, and three of his gun crew died with him. That night the Captain cut the second strip.

Corlen did what the Captain himself would have done. That's the whole of a True Man. It's also what killed him.

Voss, a soldier from Eldhythe, took over Corlen's battery, the guns he'd commanded, and was raised a True Man in his place. At the next holding Voss wet a finger and held it up to the wind, as Corlen used to. The Captain, at his shoulder, looked up at the sky, not out to sea.

"Change your shot."

**III · The Tide of Remembering**

You all knew Wick. He was the youngest of our runners, the young men who carry word along the wall to the Captain. He ran everywhere, even to this hearth, and sat at the front where the children sit, with his knees up under his chin. He could run the whole wall-walk in the time it takes to say the Title twice, the words the Captain wrote on our banner. All he wanted was to run a word to the Captain himself.

One night the Captain had the south gun moved forty yards. In the grey of the morning, Wick stood at the old gun-place, watching the water. It was so early that Hesk's lamp was still burning up on the north wall. Hesk of the mere is a True Man from Rimewatch, the town by the mere, a great lake that freezes in winter. He lights that lamp every dusk for the night watch to steer by.

One ship came, alone and slow, feeling its way in, like a boat sounding the depth. It didn't come for the wall. It came for the place where the gun had been. It fired there, to see whether the gun would answer.

The shot took the parapet at the old gun-place, and Wick fell with it.

I didn't see it. Hale did, Hale the runner, the fastest on the wall. Wick gave him three words.

"They are looking."

His word came to the Captain, as he'd wanted all through the war.

Hale ran it.

That night the Captain cut Wick's strip. So a boy died of a gun that wasn't there.

We'd thought they were like beasts, that only learn from the stick. But they look before they trust, as Wick saw. These were the third year's fleets, the Tide of Remembering. They knew our walls by heart, but they never once knew what our Captain would do. After that we moved our guns by night, and it was a quieter war, and I liked it less.

**IV · The Joined Tide**

The fourth year's fleets were the Joined Tide, and they moved as one. All the night before, the drums went in the grey from ship to ship, that knocking out of the fog like thunder that won't stop. At first light the sails came on three roads at once, three ways in across the water, from the north and the east and the south. Three roads, one hour.

Lanner of the crags, a True Man from our tower town of Highreach, whistled for the wind, the way men from the crags do. The Captain stood at his shoulder, and was still. Lanner stopped whistling, and waited for the Captain's word to fire.

"Loose."

An hour after dawn, Lanner's battery sank their bearer.

The fleet didn't falter. Their bearer's heart, the core of its wood, had already carved the hour for them to turn, and at that hour, with the bearer sunk, they turned in on the east road all the same, into empty water, one sail going pale and then the next, a breath apart. They were keeping an hour set by a mind that was already dead. Only the bearer's own ships, the ones sailing with it, went wild. That day they broke our wall in two places, in the east and in the north.

Before dusk I walked the wall to count our dead. I found Enno of the guild in the east breach, and a mile on, in the north, Della. They were the youngest bonded pair in our builders' guild, two people joined as one since the cradle. A bonded pair dies together, however far apart.

**V · The Tide that Learned Deceit**

This is the fifth count, and I don't like it.

The fifth year's fleets learned to trick us, and we call them the Tide that Learned Deceit. They came loud and alone and straight, the way the young wood, their youngest ships, had come in the first winter, and we laughed the way we'd laughed back then. Every gun on the wall fired, and then the hunters came, ships that went after every gun that had fired. Each day they sent straw down a new road, decoys that weren't really ships at all, and sent their root-men down the road where no gun woke.

But look close, as Pellow of the spire used to say. Straw rode high in the water, left no wreck, and didn't flinch when a shell hit it. A fleet running away in a false rout still kept its spacing. And a ship that looked green, young and raw like the first winter's, was no green ship if its sail went pale.

Some of us didn't look, and they're under the east cairns.

Pellow, the youngest of the Twelve, held his glass up to everything, and you laughed at him, and so did I. He ground our spyglasses out of the glass of his own city, Glasspire.

Pellow looked, and he's under the cairns too.

Looking didn't keep anyone alive. It kept the wall.

**VI · The Last Tide**

This winter, the sixth, brought the Last Tide. Whenever one of its fleets lost its bearer, and then the second ship that took up the standard, it went home. It didn't scatter. It went.

Back in the Joined Tide, {{Halyna}}, Halvard and Rhyna, the pair who alone among us can know the enemy's wood, had told me this, one beginning and the other finishing:

<!-- HALYNA -->

"The fleet can't be called home,"

"because the wood obeys the wood."

<!-- HALYNA END -->

They meant that the ships obey only the orders carved in the wood. I answered that we would send it home, and I didn't know what I meant. It can't be called home. But sink its bearer, and the next one after it, and it can be sent home.

The wood can be afraid.

At the end of this winter, ten great ships came, single ships and not fleets, and they settled down in our havens, the ten cities along the coast that the grey ships burned before we came to the Keep. The wall named them the Mystarchs. The soldiers call them the Thrones. They're sitting there tonight.

For six winters I've counted the grey sails. I know their number, and I won't tell it at this hearth.

But I'll count ours.

A count isn't a count until it has names. Tonight in the ward, the courtyard inside our walls, I called the roll of the True Men: fourteen names, and twelve men to answer. Two men had been raised to fill dead men's places, Voss in Corlen's and Ruan in Pellow's.

When I called Corlen of the harbour, the ward was quiet. I let it stay quiet for the space of a breath, and called the next name. When I called Hesk of the mere, his answer came down from the north wall, where his lamp was lit. When I called Pellow of the spire, the ward was quiet.

Now hear the dead of the wall, and hold on to them, because they're ours. Jory, who whistled. Corlen, who waited for the sea. Wick, who ran everywhere. Enno and Della, {{Enrella}}, one name for the two of them, hand in hand. Pellow, who held his glass up to everything. For each of them the Captain has cut a hand's breadth from his half of the cloak, the way a poor man cuts up the last loaf and keeps the thinnest slice for himself.

At dusk, among the stones, I named the rest of the dead in the east cairns, every name, twice. My voice wasn't steady by the last of them, and I won't pretend it was.

The first winter, his half of the cloak hung down to his knees.

Tonight it can't reach his belt.

There he sits now, at the end of the bench.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

Count V no longer says *first winter* twice. Some of the V.2 commentary after the carvings is tightened. In Count VI, Halyna's gloss is down to one clause. The parenthesis about laying is worded differently in the two tales, so a reader going straight through doesn't meet the same sentence twice.

### Declined, and why
- **Cutting or moving *The soldiers gave each order a name*.** It's a Book beat, and the plain names (*To the shore*, *Wait in the grey*) are the ones the wall uses later. I kept it in place and shortened it.
- **Cutting *I haven't filled those gaps with guesses*.** It's Seren's exactness and a Book beat. I folded it into the sentence before, so it reads less like a footnote.
- ***God forgive us, we laughed*.** This is the Book's line. God is in the Title, and faith is plain in this world (§1.9), so it isn't borrowed from ours. It stays.
- **What the winged seed does.** The Book never says, and the primer forbids inventing it. *The seed came over the wall where the guns were quiet* has to carry it.
- **Why these six get strips.** The lexicon says the Book gives no rule for who gets a strip, so I don't state one. *For each of them* stands.
- ***Hold the stone, Seren.*** The newcomer worked it out, and the full explanation (*I tell myself that every time*) belongs to I.4 by the fixed-echo table. Either reading works here.
- ***The mute thing*.** The newcomer enjoyed it as a hint, so I left it at the Book's level.
- **Repeats between the two tales** (runners, grey sails, True Men, Halyna, laying). Each tale must stand on its own (§1.1, *Explain again in each tale*), so each keeps its own short gloss. The repeats inside V.5 are cut, and the two parentheses are worded differently.
- **The fitted halves and the net.** These stay felt, not explained (§4 item 7). The newcomer called it *curious, not confused*, which is the aim.

### Still kept from the first draft (spoiler judgments)
1. **The growing (V.6, H24).** Nothing in V.5 says the ships are grown from living wood or that a bearer's heart carves each day's orders. Count IV says only what the Book's own line says: *the hour the dead heart had carved*. Count VI's new gloss stays at V.2's level (*orders carved in the wood*). The one new phrase in V.2, *a forest's thinking*, is V.1's own (H3), and it comes before V.2 in Book order too.
2. **Ruan's raising** (V.7 Part II, H28) is named in Count VI to make *fourteen names* plain. That's allowed at H31. If it feels early for a reader going in Book order, cut *and Ruan in Pellow's*.
3. **Enno and Della.** Count IV gives the law of dying together (II.3, H7) and the two breaches, but not how Della died or the mark she finished alone. Those stay in V.7.
4. **Kept dark.** No sender is named behind *They had been sent*. The fitted halves get only the split-log likeness and Seren's silence. Kael's number is kept back, and so is what the Thrones are. Count III never touches V.3's wreck.
5. **Firewood.** *For firewood* comes from V.1. It stops any reading of a custom of burning.
