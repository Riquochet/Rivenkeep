## BOOK FOUR · THE BOOK OF THE WOUND

*The wrong that was done, told from both sides of the grey, the great fog out at sea: from our shore, and from the far side.*

### IV.1 · Of the Felling of the Black Pillars

*Told in the second spring of the siege, our second year of holding the Keep, the old mountain fortress where we took refuge after the enemy's fleets burned our cities. The men on our wall gave each year's fleets a name, and that year's they called the Second Tide. This tale tells of what our fathers' fathers did, long ago, at the edge of the fog.*

*Laid by me, Seren Two-Inks. I write this Book. At our hearth, whoever tells a tale holds the old gate-stone, and I still have to tell myself to hold it. We say a tale is "laid," like a stone in a wall, because a laid tale stays. This one comes from the old rolls of the Stonewrights, our guild of master builders. They're our records of the Harvest, when our cities cut down the black trees at the edge of the fog for timber. Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders, read the rolls to me at night. Our builders come in bonded pairs, two joined as one, and each pair goes by one name, so we call those two {{Halyna}}. I copied after them so fast that my letters leaned like reeds in a wind.*

Hold the stone, Seren.

We had been reading for a year when, on a night of thaw, we came to the bottom of our guild's archive chest. The cord on the oldest roll was rotten, and it broke in Rhyna's fingers. {{Halyna}} wouldn't read me a single line of it until I'd written down what they said. As always, one of them began and the other finished:

"We two didn't live through it,"

"but our fathers' fathers did. They wrote it all down, and never read what they had written."

In the oldest roll, the fog we call the grey is only a dark haze along the rim of the sea. In the later rolls it becomes a question, asked by the night watch over and over and never answered: what was it, and who breathed it? When the answer did come, it wasn't written in our letters.

In time the havens, our ten cities along the coast where the mountains run down to the sea, reached the high summer of their pride. Our sailors took their ships farther out than their fathers had ever dared. At the edge of the grey they found the black pillars: tall black trees standing in ranks, each as straight as a builder's plumb-line. The ship-masters, the havens' captains, called them the Mystholders, because they seemed to hold the fog up the way posts hold up a roof. Their wood was harder than oak and lighter than iron, and it never rotted.

For two generations the merchants of the Trade Council, who ran the havens' trade from Eldhythe, our oldest city, sent out the axes. The rolls keep the count, cargo by cargo. Listen to it now, and hold on to it, because it's ours.

Black wood for bridges, and for buttresses to prop up walls. Black wood for the three spans across the river at Tidesmeet, and for the posts that Fenholm stands on in its marsh. Black wood for the roof-beams of Holtward, the fort in the forest, and for the cranes of Highreach, the tower high on its crags. Black wood for the galleries of Sandreach, dug into a red canyon wall, and for the hall at Rimewatch that stood out on the ice of a frozen lake. Black wood, burned slowly in the forges of Emberhythe. And in the smithies of Carnhold the axes were hammered out, and went back to the edge for more.

And then it went into things that didn't need it at all.

Black wood, for the floor of a lord's dancing-hall in Eldhythe.

Black wood, for the trading tables of the harbour-houses, the taverns on the quays where the ship-masters drank and traded, and for their dice-boards.

*[Here the roll is stained, and three lines are lost.]*

Last of all, a merchant of Tidesmeet had one whole pillar cut into a single bed.

He slept in it, and he was proud.

Halvard turned the page, and turned it back.

Next to that count, the rolls keep a stranger one. Wherever a pillar was felled, its stump sent up new growth, straight and black. The axemen went back to cut that too, because it made good poles, and our guild bought the poles for its scaffolding.

They cut it, and it grew back. They cut it, and it grew back. They cut it.

Rhyna's finger stopped below a count of poles. Some clerk had written one more line there, so small that twice I had to bend the lamp down to it before she could read it.

*All along the edge, the stumps put up nothing.*

Nobody wrote down why, and nobody asked. The roll doesn't say whose hand wrote that line. I'd have kept his name in this Book.

Season by season the grey grew thinner, and the Council called it luck. I tried that word under my tongue, and it gave way, like old mortar that has crumbled to sand.

We Stonewrights did warn the Council. The warning is there in our own hand, on a page no thumb had ever smudged:

*"These trees grow in strange soil. The carvings we find beside them bear a tongue we cannot read. Where wisdom is absent, let restraint be our guide."*

We were telling them to leave alone what they didn't understand. But by then trade sat in the high seat and ruled the havens, and our guild's advice was the kind of thing grandmothers say by the fire. The warning was read aloud once, in the Council hall at Eldhythe. Then it was put on a shelf.

The carvings in that warning were real. At the feet of the pillars the axemen had found stones set upright, cut all over with curling letters as beautiful as frost on a window. Nobody could read them.

Some ended up hanging in the harbour-houses as nothing more than ornaments. Men drank under them and did their sums. The guild locked the rest away in its archives, and its own warning with them.

A few of those stones are lying in the chest by my knee, the one I carried up the mountain. The sea washes carved wood up below our wall, from the enemy's broken ships. Ever since our first month here, {{Halyna}} have found that when they lay their hands on that wood, what's carved in it comes into them whole, the way a memory does. We call that a knowing. They have laid all four of their hands on these stones too, and been given nothing. Stone doesn't translate.

Among the counts for one late summer, Halvard came on a letter to the Council from the outermost post, the guard-post nearest the fog. A clerk had copied it in between a count of poles and a count of oars, with exactly the same care. Halvard read it out the same way he'd read the oars.

*Unknown intruder. Hostile intent. Neutralized. Vessel burned and released.*

At the foot of it, someone had cut a name out of the roll: ▒▒▒▒. Rhyna laid her finger on the blot, and took it away.

On the next page, two timber-boats are crossed off a cargo list, and beside them, in the same hand, are the words *lost at the edge.*

I copied it all down just as the clerk had, in between the poles and the oars.

I didn't know what I was holding.

It wasn't wisdom that ended the Harvest. It was only people changing their minds, the way people do. The nearer pillars were growing scarce, and the hills to the north sent cheaper timber. After the year of the burning boat, no crew would row anywhere within sight of the grey, and the rolls don't say why. So the axes stopped. No one decided it.

Then the havens forgot. The stones went on hanging over the ale and the dice, and when children asked what they said, nobody could tell them. Some of you sitting at this fire tonight were those children.

Late, very late, the wheel of pride came round, and the havens grew humble. We Stonewrights have always kept the faith of our land as well as its walls. Our temples were mended, and people climbed our worn steps again to ask for counsel, as glad and easy as men mending a sea-wall in calm weather. Nobody asked about the stones, and we said nothing. We of the guild were the ones who had turned the key on them. That is our share of the wrong.

But humility came too late.

The first dark sails of the enemy were already on the sea.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

### IV.3 · The Warden's Door

*Read out at the hearth in the third winter of the siege, while the enemy's fleets kept coming against our wall here at the Keep, the old fortress on the mountain. The men on the wall gave each year's fleets a name, and that year's they called the Tide of Remembering. This tale tells of one day at the edge of our land long ago, in our grandparents' time, and of what came after.*

*These are the words of Brenn, who was once the runner at the outpost, the last guard-post of our land, out toward the grey, the great fog bank at sea. In his old age he spoke these words to {{Wendhessa}}, the two archivists of our builders' guild at Eldhythe, our oldest city. They wrote them down and sealed them in a bundle. Our builders work in bonded pairs, two joined as one under one name, and no pair will break another pair's seal. So the bundle lay shut in our archive chest until this winter, when the oldest pair among us gave leave. Then Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders, took up the old gate-stone that every teller at our hearth holds. We call those two {{Halyna}}. They read Brenn's words aloud between them. The account of what came after is mine. I'm Seren, and I write this Book.*

"We two are holding the stone,"

"but these words aren't ours. We'll read them just as they were written."

I was nineteen. There were twelve of us, and the warden over us. We held the last post of the land, out where the rock runs into the sea toward the grey. Old Tobe had fished the edge for thirty years. I was the runner, the one who carried the post's news on foot. I had sworn at fifteen with my hand flat on the runners' stone, the same oath every runner swears: "What I saw, I carry. I carry nothing else."

▒▒▒▒ had black wood set into everything: his doorposts, his table, the frame of his bed. It was the wood the axemen were bringing back from the edge of the grey in those days. He held the post because his father had a seat on the Trade Council at Eldhythe. He was hardly older than me, his runner. They had sent him out to the edge because of his temper, where there were only the twelve of us for it to hurt. He had never known battle. He had never known patience.

One morning at summer's end, a small grey boat came out of the grey. There was no wind, and the boat came anyway.

The stranger beached it, and walked up to the warden's door. ▒▒▒▒ stood inside, and came no further. The stranger was tall and pale, and he wore a cloth like mist that moved even when he stood still. I saw no weapon on him.

First he spoke in his own language, loudly, like a storm-wind in old trees, and we were afraid. We didn't know then that it's simply how they speak. Then he said three words in our own language, carefully, the way a man sets down a thing that might break.

"Stop… Sky… Dying…"

They were the right words, and he meant them truly. I know that now. But words without sentences are only sounds, and sounds were all we heard.

Then he knelt on the doorstep and set a dark stone, about the size of two fists, in front of the warden. He set it down the way you'd set bread in front of a guest. None of us moved. Behind him a gull came down and walked about among the stones of the beach.

▒▒▒▒ kicked it aside.

It went into the dust by the wall. Then his men laughed. I was standing among them, and I can still hear that laughter.

The stranger stood up. He laid his right hand on the back of the warden's neck, gently, the way my mother did when I had a fever. He drew their heads together until their foreheads touched, and he said nothing. ▒▒▒▒ stood there and let him. If it had stopped there, I think all our lives would have gone differently.

Then the stranger took a little blade in his left hand, and touched it to the warden's right arm.

▒▒▒▒ tore himself away. He had never bled in his life. As he tore loose, the cut that should have been shallow went deep, and the blood came.

"Seize him," he screamed.

They seized the stranger. He didn't fight them. The blade was still in his hand, turned toward his own arm. Held between their hands, he went down on his knees again, right where he had knelt with his gift. I didn't understand it.

"Kill him," ▒▒▒▒ screamed.

They grabbed whatever lay to hand: sticks, and stones off the beach, and the boat-poles. One of them took my pole, the one I pushed the boats off with.

He never lifted his hand. If he cried out, it was in his own language, and I took it for the wind. I counted the blows. I still count them. When he lay still, ▒▒▒▒ kept on screaming, and some of them kept on. They broke both his arms: the one that had held the warden's neck so gently, and the one that had held the blade.

His men obeyed.

We obeyed.

I say *we*, because the post was one thing that day, and I was part of it. I never laid a hand on him. I did not stop them. I was a boy among the men.

Then it was quiet, and the gull was gone.

They dragged him down the beach by his heels, and his head struck every stone. They threw him into the bottom of his own boat, the way a fisherman throws in a torn net.

▒▒▒▒ held his arm, and didn't look at the boat.

"Burn it," he said, "and shove it off, and give it to the tide. Leave nothing to show."

They set it alight to hide what we had done, and it didn't burn the way wood burns. There was a screaming in it. The stranger was dead. It was no man screaming. It was the wood itself.

They shoved it off with the same poles. Old Tobe waded into the sea up to his knees and washed the poles. The rest of us stood on the beach, and wouldn't look, and looked.

Well out, the boat turned. It went into the wind and against the current, into the grey, burning.

That same day ▒▒▒▒ had the clerk write a letter to the Council. I stood by while it was written, and I can say it by heart:

*Unknown intruder. Hostile intent. Neutralized. Vessel burned and released.*

Not a word about the gift. Not a word about the kneeling.

The next morning, before anyone else was awake, I picked the stone out of the dust. I don't know why I did it. At his door, ▒▒▒▒ gave me the letter. So the gift and the lie went into one pack, and I ran it.

I ran for a day and a night, and every mile of it I said the oath over.

At Eldhythe an old clerk of the Council, with ink on his fingers, read the letter and looked up at me.

"Is it so?"

I didn't say it was so. I said, "It is written." That was true, and it wasn't an answer. For forty years I've weighed that small cleverness.

I carried the lie there in a day and a night. I've carried the truth for forty years, and given it to no one.

That autumn the whole shore laughed at three axemen from the felling. They had come home clinging to a spar, a broken piece of mast, and they swore they had seen a burning boat come out of the grey. I didn't laugh, because I had seen it turn. But I said nothing. The Council had it in writing.

▒▒▒▒ wore long sleeves for the rest of his life, even through the hot summers of Eldhythe, and I know why. Last winter he died in his bed of black wood. I am the last of the twelve.

For forty years the stone has lain at the bottom of my pack, and not once have I held it. I give it up now with these words, and I ask God to forgive me, because there is no one left to ask but God.

<!-- HALYNA END -->

That is the end of Brenn's words. At every blot, {{Halyna}} were silent.

The warden's arm kept one mark: the half of something, cut off in the middle. The Council kept the letter.

Brenn died like a man finally putting down a heavy load. The guild buried him under his own name. But it cut the warden's name away, out of all its records, and that is why only a blot stands wherever his name was written.

{{Wendhessa}} sealed Brenn's words and his stone together, and told no one.

Then the Fall came. For twenty months the enemy's grey ships burned the havens, our ten cities on the coast, one after another. {{Wendhessa}} carried the archive chest out of Eldhythe, his hand on one handle and hers on the other. On the Road, as we call our long march over the mountains to this fortress, their arms gave out. Our masons said there was nothing of any worth in it, and gave it to the two youngest, Tarnel and me. Tarnel was a boatwright's son. He and I had shared a cradle, and he would have been my other half if we had both lived to be sealed as a pair. He knotted his net with his free hand as we walked. I watched the knots.

On the night of the pass, the coldest of the whole Road, {{Wendhessa}} lay down, and never got up again. Tarnel and I slept beside the chest, each of us with a hand on a handle. In the morning there was frost on us both.

Tarnel did not wake.

That's where I stopped tonight, with my hands on the gate-stone. Then {{Halyna}} laid theirs beside mine.

<!-- HALYNA -->

"She carried it every step,"

"and after the passes she carried it alone."

<!-- HALYNA END -->

There is no older law among any people than this: whoever kneels at your door is under your roof. He is yours to keep from harm. We were a people of doors.

We have laid this tale the way it was laid for us.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

*This hearth had never said it before over a killing done by our own people. Old Ebba, who said it first on our very first night here, began it, and the rest were slow to follow. But they followed.*

*And the Captain, who leads us and had kept his hood up through every tale before, put his hood back.*

*In the morning the runners, the young men who carry word along our wall to the Captain, swore their oath again. It was the same oath Brenn had sworn, and they added four words to it: Not as Brenn carried. Wick, the youngest of them, swore it loudest.*

### Declined, and why
- **The letter's modern tone** (*Neutralized*, *Hostile intent*): it's Jack's fixed line, and it's the one passage the Book itself keeps in modern English. The jolt is the point.
- ***harder than oak and lighter than iron***: these are the Book's and the lexicon's own words.
- **Nine havens, not ten:** the Book's cargo list names nine and leaves out Glasspire. Adding it would add a fact, and nothing in the Plain asks the reader to count.
- **Why "Two-Inks":** it's colour. The reason (Seren writes Halyna's two halves in two inks) belongs to the wood tales and the foreword, and this headnote is already full.
- **How Seren knows the mark on the warden's arm:** the Book's annal states it flatly. Giving a source would mean inventing one, and the mark is a plant (§4) that IV.4 recalls.
- **Why the fleet is called "the Tide of Remembering":** I kept the name, because it's the Book's dateline and the custom is now clear. Its meaning (that year's ships remembered our wall) isn't known until V.5's third count (H22), so the name stays a plant.
- ***Leave nothing to show for it*** (the newcomer's suggestion): *Leave nothing to show* is a fixed echo, locked to IV.6's *Wood can hide nothing*, so it stays exact.
- **Eldhythe, Halyna and the grey glossed in both tales:** each tale has to stand alone (brief rule 4, primer §1.1). Each is glossed once per tale, and only in the headnote where Brenn is speaking.
- ***God*** (capital G): faith is plain in this world (primer §1.9), and Brenn's last line is the Book's.
- **The Captain's hood, *not once have I held it*, *the half of something*:** these are plants, and they stay plants.
