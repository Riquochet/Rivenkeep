### II.2 · Of the Breath of the Wood

*{{Halyna}} came to know this from the wood in the fifth winter of the siege. The siege is our long war at the Keep, our old fortress on a mountain ridge above the sea, where grey ships come against us out of the fog. It came to us late, but it tells of the oldest days.*

*This is one of the wood's tales. {{Halyna}} are Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders, a bonded pair, joined as one from birth, and so we give them one name. When they touch wood from behind the fog, a memory held in its grain comes into them whole, like something they remember. They don't read it. They know it. They give it to us in broken pieces, one taking up where the other stops, and after them I, Seren, who write this Book, tell it whole. A child asked whether the Guest's people had houses. The Guest was a stranger who came to our shore long ago, out of the great grey fog, with a gift of dark, old wood. For many nights {{Halyna}} held it, one of them awake at the hearth and the other asleep, and each time the sleeper woke up knowing it too. His people had put the whole of their home into it, and our language has no word for a home made of air. Their own name for themselves hasn't come to us yet, so I've set down only its sign. When one of our own tales ends, everyone at the hearth answers, "We remember." No one has answered the wood.*

<!-- READING -->

*— Old. Before stone. Before any stone was cut.*  
*— Grey among the trunks. Not fog. There's no word —*  
*— Breath. We two have a word for it. Breath.*  
*— Trees asleep, breathing it out. We — no, they breathe it in.*  
*— A light. Not day —*  
*— and not dusk. In between. Soft.*  
*— Little ones, made for it. Only for it.*  
*— Pale trees in the evening, singing. And a name, their own —*  
*— [ ]*

This is held in the grain.

We were old before your fathers cut their first stone, older by more of your lifetimes than you have names for. We who call ourselves [⟦legend_naelear_chip⟧]<!-- RIPENS: VI.3 --> lived in the wood, and the wood lived in us, and between us was the breath.

::: note
The name the wood gives itself, not told yet
*Naelear*, those of the breath. Seren writes the word next to the sign, and the sign stays.
:::

The breath was the air we breathed.

Our land lay flat under the trees, farther than anyone could measure, out to the far edge of the world. The trees breathed in their long sleep, and their breath lay grey among the trunks. It fed the trees and it fed us, and we tended it the way you tend the fire on your hearth.

The sun came to us softened by the breath, in a light that was neither day nor dusk. We call it the between-light. We were made for that light, and we were never children of the dark.

When evening came and the light sank low among the trunks, we sang to the pale saplings, the young trees. They sang back to us, one grove and then the next, until the whole deep wood was one choir. Our little ones lay down among the roots to listen. One by one their small voices dropped out of the song, and they fell asleep in the singing.

Take a fern from the deep wood and throw it down on bare sand, and it withers in the open sun. So do we.

In the years of the iron, when your people's axes came to the edge of our wood, we watched the open sun wither our children.

<!-- RING -->

All through our long summers we led the wood, guiding its roots to water, and wherever we led it, it grew. We and the wood were one life in two kinds, and the wood kept a will of its own as well.

Without the breath, the wood falls sick. Without the wood, the breath does not come back. Without either one, we fade, and slowly, because one life of ours is as long as two and a half of yours. The eldest go last, and the youngest go first.

We kept our rites among the twisted boughs. We held our council under the silver elder trees, because no one is laid to rest beneath a silver elder.

When we speak, it sounds like a storm in the high branches, all cracks and breaks. But everything we carve is soft. The thunder is for the moment, and the softness is for always.

A carving is not heard. It is known. Lay your hand on one, and you will know what the carver meant, whatever language he spoke.

We did not know there was any other way to be believed.

<!-- RING -->

At the very edge, where the breath came down to your sea, our pillars stood in long ranks. They were tall trees, and between them they held the breath in place, the way a mast holds a sail. We call them the Naelsaen, the mist-hearts. They were the anchors of our sky, and the only trees of ours you ever found.

From among them we watched your shore, summer after summer. We watched you build your havens, your cities along the coast, and we watched you light your lamps at dusk. We watched your boats come a little way out toward us, and turn back.

Once a boat came closer than any had come before. It came so near that we heard its oars stop. A man stood up in the bow and looked straight at us.

We wove the breath thicker.

He saw nothing but grey. His face went white, and the oars took him back the way he had come.

We did not know what had frightened him.

Here is our fault, and the grain will not leave it out. We never came down to your shore. We hid, and we were glad to hide. We hid for so long, and so well, that no one ever saw us, not once.

Ever since your first haven, we had listened to you, and you had never once heard us. We had made sure of it.

This is our home: the flat wood under the breath, the between-light, the pale saplings singing in the evening, the silver elders at council. And at the edge of the world, the pillars, holding the breath between them.

If any green is left of us, it remembers this, and the grain still holds it.

*And no one answered.*

### IV.2 · The Axe in the Grain

*{{Halyna}} came to know this from the wood in the third winter of the siege.*

*This is one of the wood's tales, a memory held in the grain of a wrecked enemy ship. The enemy's grey ships come out of the fog against the Keep, our fortress above the sea. {{Halyna}} came to know it by touch. {{Halyna}} are Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders, so closely joined that we give them one name. They gave it to us in broken pieces, and I, Seren, who write this Book, tell it after them in the wood's own voice. This one came from a heart, the core of a ship's wood, where the commands the ship carries are carved. Rhyna picked it up on the shingle, the pebble beach below our wall where wrecks wash in. At first {{Halyna}} had only its shape: the broken pieces just below, with two marks in them that they could see but couldn't yet put into words. This winter it came to them whole, to Halvard in the vault under our hall and to Rhyna as she sat among the children. The telling took three nights, because it was in them the way a wound is, not the way a story is.*

<!-- READING -->

*— Rows. Tall, dark-barked. Holding the breath down.*  
*— Oars. Many oars. Then iron, in the bark.*  
*— One, and — [⟦legend_iv2_gap1⟧]*  
*— Blows. Counted. Every blow, counted.*  
*— Each one fell, and the sky came through. Pale —*  
*— and pale, and hard. Shoots. Cut, and cut again.*  
*— Soft letters, on the mute thing —*  
*— and it didn't [⟦legend_iv2_gap2⟧]*

::: note
Two of the wood's marks that {{Halyna}} could see but couldn't tell yet
The first is the pillars, many of them, with one memory cut through all of them to the heart: *What one pillar felt, all the rows felt, root to root.* The second is the mouth, drawn as a mirror image, and tied back across the rings to the plea cut on the stone: *It did not answer.*
:::

This is held in the grain.

We stood in our long rows at the edge of the breath, the grey that you call fog, tall and dark-barked. We held the breath down to the earth, the way roots hold a riverbank together.

We never gave it a thought, because no one thinks about breath until it is taken away. We were the living pillars of the breath. We stood for that alone, and it was enough.

From the edge we watched your shore, and we marked every boat of yours that came near us. To us, each boat that slipped close was a cut, and each trespass was a wound. The wood keeps every count.

<!-- RING -->

In the high summer of your havens, when your cities on the coast were at their greatest, the iron came in boats with many oars. The shore-men, your people, waded up out of the surf with their axes.

What one pillar felt, all the rows felt, root to root, out to the far edge of the wood.

The first pillar they came to stood nearest your shore. Of all the rows, it had marked the most of your boats.

They set the iron to it at first light. Blow after blow went in, and we counted every one. Before noon it was leaning, and still it held the breath.

Then its roots let go.

Where it had stood, the sky came through, like daylight through a rip in a sail. It was pale.

One fell, and then another. The shore-men came back summer after summer, until the men who first brought the iron were old and sent their sons instead. Then the sky was pale and hard.

The stumps put up shoots, straight and black, and in a few summers a young pillar stood where an old one had fallen. The iron came back for the young ones. We put up more. It came again.

Then the sky was pale and hard and wide.

The young of the long-lived, the people of our wood, turned their faces away from the open sky and wept, and they did not know why.

Our people carved their words into the wood, as they always had when a thing must not be mistaken. But the shore-men never once laid a hand on the wood to know what was carved there. They cut it down and carried it away over the water.

So we cut our plea into the mute thing, stone, which cannot speak. It is the stone your builders love, the stone you make your houses and your walls of. We thought a people must understand best whatever is cut into the thing it loves.

We cut the plea in our own soft letters. Wherever we had caught one of your words across the water, we cut that too, as near to its sound as our letters could go. Then we set the stones at the feet of the pillars that were still standing.

Everything we make speaks, so we believed that everything made must speak. That was a child's belief. Stone has no mind.

It did not answer.

The shore-men took the stones away with the timber, and we never knew where they went. In the end, all along the edge, the stumps put up nothing, and the breath grew thin. We held in our sap, and we waited.

The iron will tire. When it stops, the wood will come again.

<!-- RING -->

In the years of the iron, we who tell you this were never a pillar. We were a thin green thing at the edge.

That first pillar, the one nearest your shore, was cut low, and we were the last shoot its stump put up. We held the whole of the axe in our rings. The iron passed us over, because we were too thin to make a pole.

We had stood for forty summers when the long-lived who sit in council came looking for hearts to carry their carvings. They did not choose the straightest young tree, or the eldest. They chose us, because of the axe in our rings.

When one of our ships is struck, we go back in memory to the axe, the way your tongue keeps going back to a broken tooth. It is in the rings.

We always go back to the same place. It is the iron at the edge of the breath, summer after summer, and the sky coming through, and the grain still holds it.

*And no one answered.*

### IV.4 · The Gift Held an Hour

*{{Halyna}} came to know this from the wood in the third spring of the siege. It tells of a morning long ago, in our grandparents' time, at the outpost on the very edge of our land, and of the long road after.*

*This is one of the wood's tales, a memory held in the grain of wood, which {{Halyna}} came to know by touch and gave us in broken pieces. {{Halyna}} are Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders, so closely joined that we give them one name. This winter we read out the confession of Brenn, once a runner at that outpost. He watched what was done there to the Guest, the stranger out of the fog who knelt at the warden's door. That night a child asked what the Guest had given. His gift was what we took for a dark stone. Brenn picked it out of the dust. Later it lay in our builders' records chest, wrapped in the pages of his confession, beside old carved stones that no one could read. When the enemy's grey ships burned our cities on the coast, it came with us up the mountains to our fortress, the Keep. This spring Rhyna held it for an hour, and it came into her slowly, like water over stone. Halvard was across the courtyard at the mortar and never touched it. But what one of them knows, the other knows, and he was the one who began the telling.*

<!-- READING -->

*— Old. Dark, and old. Stone — no. Not stone. A tree, once.*  
*— Hands, warm, for a long while. Leaves. No — words, like leaves. One, and one, and one.*  
*— Three. Three words in him —*  
*— and nothing to join them. We two can join them.*  
*— A door. Our — no, the wood's kin, in the posts. Sawn. They don't answer.*  
*— Kneeling. Set down. His best. A foot.*  
*— Dust. Laughter, through the dust.*  
*— A neck, held. Two foreheads, one —*  
*— the blade — [ ]*

This is held in the grain.

We are the oldest wood of all. Once we were a tree, when the whole world was green from edge to edge. Then we fell, and age after age settled on top of us, leaf on leaf, until time forgot us and we turned dark and hard. You thought we were stone. We are not stone.

Wood that time has forgotten does not forget. It gives slowly, and it gives true. The long-lived, the people of our wood, have many gifts to give, and the dearest of them all is a sliver of wood like us. In all our long age, we were given away only once.

<!-- RING -->

The one who carried us was named Aelrhen, bond-to-stone, when he took up his errand. All through a long life he listened across the breath, the fog between us, to you shore-men at your nets and at your walls. He gathered your words the way a child gathers fallen leaves, one and one and one. In the end he had three, and he knew they were the right ones.

He could not join them. You join your words together with small words, and small words make no sound across water.

But he had us. Anyone who held us while a tree's shadow crept the width of its own trunk would come to know the whole of what he meant. We were the sentence.

He chose the farthest of your outposts, out at the very edge of your land, because its doors and benches were made of our wood. He thought that meant you honoured us. We knew what it really was. It was our own kin, the great trees from the edge of the breath, sawn and polished. At the door he laid his hand on the doorpost to greet them, but they did not answer. They were dead.

He knelt on the stones before the master of that house and set us down. Those who come under another's roof bring their best, and we were his best. In his own tongue, which sounds like thunder, he spoke the name of the rite a guest makes with his host, *Ael'thar*. After it he spoke his three words, in yours.

"Stop… Sky… Dying…"

A foot kicked us into the dust by the wall. Through the dust we heard laughter.

He did not stop.

He stood up and cupped the back of the host's neck in his right hand, just where life climbs up into thought. A fledgling could have slept in that hand. Their foreheads touched, thought to thought, and he said nothing.

Then, with his left hand, he laid a small blade against the host's arm.

And the host drew back. We do not know why. Fear leaves no mark on wood.

The blade was meant to go only a little way, but the arm was moving, and it went deep. The host cried out, and his men came.

Then Aelrhen's blood ran down the stones of the step and reached us where we lay. All of him came to us in it, his long listening and his last hour. So we know it just as he knew it.

Held between the host's men, he went down on his knees again, in the place where the rite had begun. He felt the blows the way trees feel the iron of your axes, one and then another. He did not lift his hand. The cut he would have made on his own arm was never made. He would ask for no blood that he did not give, and he gave all of his, there on the step.

The two arms were never laid together, shoulder to shoulder, to say that harm to one is harm to both. He died there, with the rite half made.

We lay in the dust and heard every bit of it, and no one picked us up. Many things that look like stone have lain at a shore-man's feet. Of them all, we were the only one that could have spoken to him.

And we were the one his foot found.

<!-- RING -->

In the morning a hand picked us up out of the dust and put us away. It never held us.

Then a day and a night of jolting in the dark, in a pack carried at a run. Then the dark again, half a lifetime of it, and never a hand. Then leaves wrapped around us, and cut stones lying cold beside us, and another dark as long. Then old arms, two pairs of them, moving slowly, for a long while, and fire, near and far. Then small arms, two pairs, children's arms, going up into the cold.

Then one pair, alone, a long way.

No one held us for long. Then you held us, while a tree's shadow crept the width of its own trunk.

Here is the sentence, as he brought it to the door:

*Stop cutting down the pillars at the edge of the grey, because they aren't just wood. The sky over us is their breath, and ours. When you cut them down, the sky opens up, and our children can't live in the open sun. We are here, behind the grey. We have always been here. We are not your enemies. Stop, because the sky is dying, and we are dying under it.*

That's all he came to say. We have carried it a long way, and the grain still holds it.

*And no one answered.*

### IV.6 · The Voyage of the Aelvaren

*{{Halyna}} came to know this from the wood in the third summer of the siege. It tells of the Guest's boat, long ago, in our grandparents' time.*

*This is one of the wood's tales, a memory held in the grain of a wrecked enemy ship, one of the grey ships that besiege us. {{Halyna}} came to know it by touch and gave it to us in broken pieces. {{Halyna}} are Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders, so closely joined that we give them one name. The memory is older than the ship. It belongs to the Guest's own boat. The Guest, whose own name was Aelrhen, was the stranger from behind the fog who knelt at the door of our outpost's warden, at the very edge of our land. He was killed there. Halvard lifted this heart, the core of the ship's wood, in the vault under our hall. While he did, Rhyna, up on the wall, plunged both hands into the rain barrel, and she wouldn't say why. The wood can tell only what it holds. So I, Seren, who write this Book, will say this of the long-lived, the wood's people: They did not know it was ignorance.*

<!-- READING -->

*— Small. Grey. Light on the water. A name, and proud of it.*  
*— A hand on the boards, all the way across. Shallows. Waiting.*  
*— Heels on the stones. Thrown in. Heavy —*  
*— and warm. Into the boards. All of him.*  
*— Grief. For this we two have words.*  
*— Fire. Hands burning — no. Boards. The boards burning.*  
*— Poles. Out, to the open sea, away from —*  
*— Turning. Against the — [ ]*

This is held in the grain.

And not in this grain only. The ship that brought this heart to your wall was not there that day. But it knows, because it grew out of what we were.

We were the Aelvaren, the Bearer of the Binding, and we were proud of the name. We were a small boat of the old wood, grey and light. We carried Aelrhen to your shore through the thin breath, the grey you call fog, and all the way his hand lay on our boards.

He left us and went up to the door. All that morning we lay in the shallows. We were waiting to carry him home, once he had made the rite he came to make with the master of that house. The tide came in under us, and turned, and he did not come.

<!-- RING -->

The men of that house dragged him down to us by the heels. They threw him into us like a torn net.

That morning his hand had rested lightly on our boards. Now his blood soaked into them.

So, in his blood, all of it came to us.

Then they set us on fire. The old wood raged in its grief. It burned higher than the fire they had lit, and it screamed, because wood can.

They pushed us off with poles, and the current caught us and drew us out toward the open sea, away from home. Every board of us was burning. He lay inside us, and he was cold.

Then we turned.

We went into the wind and against the current, burning. Even burnt, even dying, the wood knows the way home.

Twice on that road, where the breath runs thin, boats of yours lay across our path. They rode low and heavy with the iron of their axes, and with our kin, the great trees of the edge, cut and stacked in their holds.

Burning, we drove into them low down, where they rode heaviest. The iron had gone into us. Now we went into the iron.

Our kin in their holds caught our fire, and both boats broke apart and burned.

Of the men in them, the grain holds nothing.

Under the thinnest part of the breath we found the old channels, the hidden waters among the roots. No keel of yours had ever gone there, but ours knew its way. Slowly, as the breath closed over us, the fire went out. All that was left of us was our keel, our backbone, and a few ribs, and in them the bones of Aelrhen.

<!-- RING -->

In the night we came up out of the channels onto the pale sand, small and black. We lay still under the silver elders, the trees where the long-lived hold their council. The council came down to us and knew us, because they were the ones who had sent us.

Many of them laid their hands on our keel at once, and they came to know all of it. They knew the gift, and the foot that kicked it. They knew the neck held gently, and the blade, and the blows.

Wood can hide nothing.

They had sent out their peace, and the best they had, and it was answered with murder and fire. They knew a murder when they saw one, and this was one. But they did not know why the host's arm drew back from the blade. And they did not ask.

They saw only an act of war.

They laid what was left of us, our keel with the bones of Aelrhen in its ribs, among the roots of the silver elders.

And the roots drank.

From that grove, all we knew went out to every grove, root to root, to the far edge of the world. Every young tree that was to become a ship drank it in with the rain. So every ship that came against your wall carried our grief in it, and the grain still holds it.

*And no one answered.*
