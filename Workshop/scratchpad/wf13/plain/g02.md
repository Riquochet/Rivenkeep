### I.2 · The Cry of the Bonded

*Told in the first winter of the siege, a few nights after the enemy's first attack on our wall. It tells of the evening we first came to the Keep, the old fortress high on the mountain ridge.*

*This tale is my own. I'm Seren, and I write this Book, and this was the first tale I ever laid. We say a tale is "laid," like a stone in a wall, because a laid tale stays. Whoever holds the old gate-stone by the fire is the one who tells, and every tale ends with the same words, whether the teller lived it or heard it. Only one tale had been laid at this fire before mine. On the day I'm telling about, I was twelve years old, one of the guild's children.*

Tonight I was afraid to speak, and {{Halyna}} took my two hands and laid them on the stone. That's one name for two people, Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders here.

<!-- HALYNA -->

"Hold the stone."

"Hold it, and it will hold you."

<!-- HALYNA END -->

So I'm holding it.

At dusk on the day of the banner, the guild sat in the snowy grass of the Keep's old courtyard. The day of the banner is what we call the day we reached the Keep, because of what happened that evening. The Captain, who back then was only the captain of one small company of soldiers, climbed the broken wall and tore his cloak in two. On one half he wrote in coal: *In memory of our home, our families, our God, our freedoms, our peace.* Then he raised it on a pike as our banner.

We were the Stonewrights, some hundreds of us, the guild of master builders who had raised every wall on our coast, and we sat with our chisels in our laps. The last of the daylight lay on the snow, as thin as ash. Up on the wall the soldiers were cheering the banner. Down in the grass, we said nothing.

I sat there too, with no craft yet except carrying water. All the way over the high mountain passes I'd walked beside a boy of the guild who knotted a net as he went, and at night I slept beside him.

I came into that courtyard alone. He never reached the Keep.

A little after me, two children came in holding hands. They were Enno and Della, both fifteen. Our guild works in bonded pairs, two builders joined as one from birth, who work as one, and we call all such pairs the Bonded. Even the guild's children come in pairs. Of all the children's pairs, Enno and Della were the only one that came up the mountain whole, both of them alive. They sat down together and held on.

We had watched ten havens, our ten cities along the coast, die in twenty months. The grey sails, the enemy's ships out of the fog at sea, had burned them one after another. We walked away from every one of them with our chisels packed. We never lifted a hand to fight, because our creed forbade it: *Build, do not destroy. Protect, do not attack.* Mortar and chisels were the only weapons we had, and they hadn't been enough.

Twelve generations of our faith still sat on us like a wet cloak. Every elder there was turning over the same hard thought: that walls built for peace must in the end be held with blood. And if that was so, whose blood? Whose hands?

Back then I didn't know {{Halyna}}, except as two elders who sat apart under the broken inner gate and spoke to no one, because they didn't need to. Long ago, when the Keep was first built, Rhyna's family had laid the very gate they were sitting under, and Halvard's family had cut its stone out of the mountain. The two of them had been one pair since the cradle.

Then the Captain came down from the wall. He leads us all now. I had watched his cloak go up, and I didn't know yet what it meant for us. He wasn't tall. His fingers were still black from the coal, and half of a cloak hung on his back. He looked at the elders, and past them at the broken gate, and he asked one question. It's the only speech of his that anyone here can repeat word for word:

"Your walls are falling. Will you build, or will you watch and die?"

No one answered him. I remember the wind in the grass.

Then {{Halyna}} stood up.

They looked up at the torn cloak on the wall and bowed to it, both in the same breath, and raised their chisels as one.

The last of the light caught on their two chisels.

She began the cry, and he finished it:

<!-- HALYNA -->

"The mortar cries out from the ground. Rise, Stonewrights! Lay stone upon stone. Stand every wall to the very end."

"Rise! Rise! Rise!"

<!-- HALYNA END -->

It was one cry, from two throats. Hers came first for no reason except that a cry has to begin somewhere. Some people tell it as if she called and he answered, with a silence in between.

There was no silence.

Then the guild stood up, all of us at once. It was like an arch when the builders knock away its wooden frame, and every stone holds up the one beside it. I stood with them, though I don't remember standing. We took up the cry. The soldiers on the wall took it from us, and the column, all the townsfolk who had walked up the mountain with us, took it from the soldiers.

Old stone doesn't shake. I felt it shake under my feet.

That night this hearth, the great fireplace you're sitting at now, had its first fire in more years than anyone could count. {{Idrenna}}, the oldest pair in our guild, sat by it and made the terms with the Captain, and I carried in the water. They spoke slowly, the way old people do, and the Captain didn't hurry them.

The Stonewrights wouldn't carry weapons, and he didn't ask them to. But they would build between the volleys of the guns, and mend a breach, a hole broken in the wall, while the fighting was still going on. The terms still stand, and they say in the guild's own formal words how that work would be done: *with the speed and precision that only Aetherbonded pairs could achieve*. *Aetherbonded* is the guild's word for its bonded pairs. Then {{Idrenna}} put the whole bargain to the Captain in two halves, each of them saying one:

"Not the sword, but the stone."

"Not the killing, but the keeping."

The Captain bowed his head. Those two lines are the guild's word now.

Before that fire burned down, {{Orvenna}}, the pair who are our wall-masters, split the siege day into three parts, the laying, the hauling and the holding, the way a quarryman splits a block of stone along its own seam. Then they told the Captain:

"Every gun you haul is a row of stone we don't lay,"

"so spend a gun the way a careful man spends silver."

They meant that every hour spent moving guns was an hour taken from building.

*Then the masons sang, as they still sing it:*

> *Lay the stone while the sea is empty.*  
> *Haul the guns while the guns are cold.*  
> *Hold the wall when the grey sails come,*  
> *and the wall will hold.*

And wherever you see a Stonewright on the wall in the holding, while the wall is under attack, look again. You'll see two.

Late that night, by torches that hissed as the frost fell on them, we laid the war's first row of stone across the gap in the inner gate, with each stone set across the joint below it, so that no crack could run straight through. {{Orvenna}} asked for the clearest voices to lead the havens' old gate-song, *Two at the Gate*, which is sung in two voices. Enno and Della stood up and took the lead between them. In every line, one of them sang up to the comma, and the other sang the rest.

*Then they sang, and the guild sang after them:*

> *Stone on stone, and the stone stays true.*  
> *Hand over hand, and the hand holds on.*  
> *Two at the gate, when the grey sails gather.*  
> *Two at the gate, and the gate stands strong.*

I sang up to the comma, and then, with no one beside me, I sang the rest.

When the torches had burned down to stubs, Enno and Della fell asleep against the new row of stone, still holding hands, the way they'd come up the mountain. I lay down a little way off, by the water buckets, and slept.

By morning the mortar had set.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

### I.3 · The Captain's Long Breath

*Told in the second month of the first winter of the siege. It tells of the enemy's first attack on our wall.*

*Hale the runner laid this tale. He was seventeen and in his first weeks on the wall. Our runners are the young men who carry word to the Captain, who leads us: whatever they see on the wall, they run to him. Here, whoever holds the old gate-stone by the fire is the one who tells. We say a tale is "laid," like a stone in a wall, because a laid tale stays, and every tale ends with the same words. Hale told it standing up, as fast as he runs. I, Seren, who write this Book, have set it down slower.*

Give me the stone, then. I'm not a storyteller. I run.

Every haven, as we called our ten cities on the coast, had a runners' stone of its own, where the runners swore their oath. We made ours in our first week here, out of a fallen block by the inner gate, and on it we swore the old oath:

"What I saw, I carry. I carry nothing else."

Wick swore it last. He's the youngest of us and the fastest, and there's nothing he'd rather do than carry a word to the Captain himself.

At first grey light on the morning of the first attack, I was standing by Kael's guns. Kael commands them, and they're still the only guns we have. He's Kael the Counter, who counts everything twice, and he's one of the True Men, the Captain's own company of twelve. The Captain gave Kael his orders: where to watch, what shot to fire, and when to hold fire. Then he went on to the far end of the wall. Kael put his hand on my shoulder.

"Nothing changes those orders but his own voice, here at my side, lad, and a man can only stand in one place."

*Then the troops drilled the three calls, the orders the Captain gives in his own voice, as they still drill them:*

> *Shift your ground, and watch new water.*  
> *Change your shot for the sea, the shore, the sky.*  
> *Hold your fire, or loose it, when the Captain is by.*

When the drill was done, Jory of Eldhythe, a soldier from our oldest harbour city, leaned on the parapet of the east wall and whistled a tune the old ship-masters hate, the tune of an old harbour rhyme. The ship-masters are the men who used to captain our boats. Merrick, the eldest of them, was coiling rope at the foot of the stair, and he told Jory to stop. Jory laughed down at him.

"It's only a rhyme."

Merrick didn't laugh.

Before the morning was half gone, the enemy's sails came out of the grey, the great fog bank out at sea, faster than I could count them. That was my first holding, which is what we call the hours when the enemy attacks and the wall has to be held. In the laying, when our builders are at work, the hours go slowly, because stone is heavy. In the hauling, when the guns are moved, they go slower still. In the holding, an hour is gone in a heartbeat.

Late in the holding, when the fight with the other ships was over, the masons, the Stonewrights who build our wall, came out to mend the breach where the wall had been broken. Then Kael's lookout saw an enemy hull through a far-glass, one of the spyglasses Pellow of the True Men made for us. It had hung back in the grey, and no ship had ever done that before. Now it came in, late and alone.

The smoke of the guns lay between the Captain and that hull, so he couldn't see it. Kael gave me three words.

"Hull hangs back."

I ran it.

I run. Under the smoke, head down, past the masons, past the ropes. I don't speak to anyone, and I don't stop. Up into the clear air above the gate.

The Captain is standing still.

That's what I remember first. Everything on that wall was moving except him. He had one hand on the parapet and his face to the sea. He didn't turn round to look at me. But he heard me, to the last word.

Then he walked toward Kael's guns at his own pace, with me at his shoulder. And as he walked, the hour began to stretch.

It didn't stop dead. It slowed, like a stone-cart on a long hill, until a boy walking behind it could count the spokes in its wheels. I haven't got a better way to say it.

I saw the water pour off the late hull's bow in sheets. I counted the ripples running down its grey sail, the way you count the rings on a cut log: one, and then the next. Each one, I think, was a minute. The gulls hung in the air. The smoke stood still. And on he walked.

I had time to be afraid. Then I had time to be ashamed of it. Then I had time to stop being afraid.

He stopped at Kael's shoulder. Kael was counting under his breath. Then the Captain gave the call, in his own voice.

"Hold."

The guns held their fire. Below us the masons kept on laying stone, two by two, and never looked up. The late hull came on through the smoke, closer and closer, until I could see the wet on its timbers.

"Loose."

The hour let us go, slowly, like a held breath going out. The guns fired. Out in the smoke the hull broke apart in the one stretch of that water where it couldn't hurt anyone, not even the masons out on the breach. It broke right where the Captain had meant it to.

I don't know what the Captain thought. I know what he did.

That evening I walked the tide-line, the pebble beach under the wall where the sea washes in the wreckage, as we runners do after every holding. In the shallows I saw marks cut into a plank, the first carved plank the sea had ever brought us. I carried word of them up past the soldiers' fires to {{Halyna}}, one name for two people, Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders:

"Marks on the wood."

I ran it.

That night I found my way back by Hesk's lamp on the north wall. Hesk is one of the True Men, and the lamp was his mother's. By its light I knew the north wall was awake.

Now the men say it all along the wall: "For the Captain, an hour lasts as long as a day." I think it started with me, though Voss, a soldier from Eldhythe, will tell you it's older. They say it as if the Rite of the Cornerstone had given it to him. That's the ceremony the Keep's first builders left behind them, and soon after we came here it made the Captain our Commander, though we still call him the Captain. I used to believe that about the Rite myself, and I know better now. Kael set it right, and I carry it the way he said it, and nothing else:

"It's not the world that slows, lad. It's the Captain who won't hurry. The moment he chose was the moment that came."

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*
