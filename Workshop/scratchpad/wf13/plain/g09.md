### V.7 · The Mirror in the Grain

*Told in three parts, each on the night we came to know what a carving in the enemy's wood said: in the fourth summer, the fifth spring and the sixth winter of the siege.*

*I, Seren Two-Inks, who write this Book, laid this tale on three nights. (At our hearth, whoever tells a tale holds the old gate-stone, and I still have to tell myself to hold it. We say a tale is laid, like a stone in a wall, because a laid tale stays.) All three nights I held the lamp down on the shingle, the pebble beach below our wall. It's this same lamp, the one here tonight.*

**Part I · Three Roads, One Hour**

Hold the stone, Seren.

It was the fourth summer of the siege. All that short summer night the drums knocked out in the grey, the fog bank at sea where the enemy's fleets come from. It sounded like mallets in a far-off quarry.

All through it, Enno and Della sat up in their house of one door. They were the youngest bonded pair among the Stonewrights, our guild of builders. A bonded pair is two people joined as one from birth.

A pair's own mark is cut face down under the last stone of its finished work. But on the night of their sealing, our guild's wedding of a bonded pair, Enno and Della said they couldn't wait for the last stone. They'd build a house of one door and cut their mark under its first stone. Enno set the chisel, and Della struck it. The house stood one course high, one row of stones. It never stood any higher.

They'd come into the courtyard of this Keep, our old fortress on the mountain, hand in hand, on the evening we first reached it. I came in alone. Tarnel, the boy I'd shared a cradle with, had died of the cold on the road. We hadn't been sealed yet, so the bond between us wasn't whole, and I lived.

At dawn the enemy's fleet came at us by three roads across the sea at once, all in the same hour, and broke our wall in place after place, east and north. There were more breaches than there were pairs to mend them. Our wall-masters are a bonded pair too, Ormund and Penna, and like every pair we call them by one name made from both their names: {{Orvenna}}. They never raise their voices. That morning, without a word, they did what no masters had ever done before.

They sent a pair apart.

Ormund gave Enno the east breach, and Penna gave Della the north. At the head of the stair Enno called back their old joke from the night of their sealing: "It is like being one person." And Della, already running, threw him the rest: "with four hands and no excuses."

Bonded pairs are seldom apart, and never far apart. When one of a sealed pair dies, the other dies too. A mile of wall is far.

In the east a shot hit the wall and took Enno with it. I didn't see it. Hale did.

In the north I was beside Della with the mortar. She'd turned the last stone of the breach over, to cut their mark on its underside. She set the chisel herself, and struck it, and her hand didn't shake.

Then her mallet stopped in the air.

She looked east, toward Enno. Then she bent down again, cut the last stroke, laid the chisel beside the mark, and lay down with her cheek against the stone.

I pressed my thumb into the mortar we'd just laid, the way masons do to test it. It was still soft.

She was gone.

Hale, one of our runners, who carry word along the wall to the Captain, came running with the word from the east, and didn't say it. Never before had a runner come too late.

In all the guild's records, theirs is the only mark that one hand finished. By the last of the daylight I looked a long time for the stroke Della cut alone.

You can't tell.

At nightfall Stannard of the ridge, one of the True Men, the Captain's own company of twelve, carried Enno the whole mile and laid him beside her. With their own four hands {{Orvenna}} set the marked stone over the two of them, mark down. It's their grave, and we call it the north stone. {{Idrenna}}, the oldest pair in our guild, who had sealed them, prayed over it in two halves:

"God, you made these two one before either drew breath."

"They had one cradle from you and one stone from us, and you won't part them."

Over the stone Ormund began Enno and Della's old joke, and Penna finished it. Then the Captain, who leads us, cut a strip from the half of his torn cloak that he still wears, and tied it on a pike over them. It was the first strip he had tied for Stonewrights. They were his company too.

Kael the Counter, who counts everything, stood beside it and counted them.

"One," he said.

When the tide turned, a plank came in from the bearer, the fleet's leading ship, which our guns had sunk. The enemy's orders are carved into the wood of their ships. Rhyna, who leads our builders here with her husband Halvard, went down to the plank on the shingle, and I went with the lamp.

Up on the wall, where Enno had knelt, Halvard was mending the breach while the masons sang *Two at the Gate*, the old gate-song. Its first line is sung by two voices, one up to the comma and the other the rest. On our first night at the Keep, Enno and Della had sung it. That night no one took the first line, neither before the comma nor after it. On the second line everyone came in, as though the first had been sung.

Rhyna laid her hand on the grain of the wood and went pale. Up on the wall, at the same moment, with his trowel in his hand, Halvard went pale too. Together they are {{Halyna}}, and they're the ones who know the wood for us. When one of them touches the enemy's carved wood, its message comes into both of them whole, like something remembered. We call that a knowing, and we trust it, because the wood can't lie. Halvard came down, and they told it: the order for the whole fleet, every ship bound to the others.

*Three roads, one hour, and east and north go as one. The south waits at the dread's edge until the south's reach goes east, and then it goes.*

The dread is the wood's word for water where its ships have died: the water under our guns. The south's reach was our own south stretch of wall. The fleet in the south was to wait just out of range until our people there had gone east to the breach, and then come in.

For a long time neither of them spoke. I asked them what was wrong, though I think I knew.

<!-- HALYNA -->

"That's how we two work a wall."

"Each of us waits for the other's stroke, and doesn't need to be told."

<!-- HALYNA END -->

The wall calls each year's enemy fleets a Tide, and the carving gave this one its name: *Aelthae*. That night I recognized its first half. I had written *Ael* before, in *Ael'thar*. That was the name of the ceremony the Guest began as he knelt at the door of our outpost, in our grandparents' time. He was a stranger who had come out of the fog. I have written the name, and I won't write what I thought.

On Halvard's trowel, the mortar was drying.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

**Part II · What We Taught Them**

It was the fifth spring of the siege. On a grey morning a fleet ran at our wall the old way, the way the young ships of our first winter had come: each ship alone, loud and straight. The whole wall laughed, and every gun on it fired, Pellow's with the rest.

Pellow of the spire, from Glasspire, the proudest of our ten cities, was the youngest of the True Men. He'd ground our spyglasses out of his city's glass. He had two words for every man on the wall.

"Look close."

What happened next, I have from Hale the runner. When Pellow's own guns had fired, he lifted his glass, and in it a sail went pale. Then a second. Then a third. We'd seen that before: when one of their ships is struck, the sails of others far off go from grey-green to white, like a man's face. The young ships of our first winter had never done that.

"They are not green."

Green wood is young wood. He meant these ships weren't young, though they'd come at us the way the young ones did.

Hale ran it. Behind him, Pellow turned his guns on the grey without waiting for orders, and stood up on the parapet, the low wall along the top, with his glass.

The Captain went along the wall, and at each True Man's shoulder he gave the call: "Shift your ground." That means move your guns. Nothing may change a battery's orders but the Captain's own voice at its side, and a man can stand in only one place. He didn't hurry, the way a lamplighter doesn't hurry, in case his taper goes out.

Then out of the grey came the ships we call hunters. They went for every gun that had fired, and wouldn't leave it.

Every battery he had reached lived. One at a time is slow.

Pellow's was the farthest from him, and the nearest to the grey. The hunters came to his first, and it met them alone. The Captain didn't quicken his pace.

Pellow lay dead at the parapet.

His glass lay beside his hand, unbroken.

When the tide turned, {{Halyna}} laid their hands on a plank from that fleet.

<!-- W -->
*Come loud, alone and straight, like the young wood, and stop just before the fire. The hunters wait for the flashes.*

<!-- W: the italic line only -->
Under it: *they shoot first and far, and move any gun we find.*

The flashes were our guns going off, which show where each one stands. The line underneath was about us.

"The wood can't lie," I said, and then I saw it. The wood doesn't lie. It was ordered to make us believe a lie, and it obeyed.

<!-- HALYNA -->

"They learned it from us."

"We taught them, with every gun we kept quiet."

<!-- HALYNA END -->

For years we'd fooled them, holding our guns quiet to draw a ship in close, and moving our guns by night. They'd had no word for a lie. We gave them one. Small, in that same carving, was cut their word for us: *Rhenear*, the stone-deaf.

At dusk we raised Pellow's cairn, a heap of stones over his grave. The Captain tied a strip from his cloak over it. Then he held out Pellow's glass to Hale, and Pellow's battery with it, and with them a True Man's place.

"I'm not a True Man," Hale said. "I run."

So the glass and the guns went to Ruan of the spire, who had led Pellow's men under him, and the Captain raised him a True Man. Ruan put the glass to his eye and looked out past the cairn.

"Look close," he said.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

**Part III · The Plan That Carved Its Own Defeat**

It was the sixth winter of the siege. In snow up to our knees a bearer came, the leading ship of a fleet. In its heart, the core of its wood where a fleet's orders are cut, it carried a whole tomorrow: every order for the next day. We sank it before noon, and the tide brought the heart in.

It was the longest knowing {{Halyna}} ever held. Twice I filled the lamp before they'd told all of it, and at the end was this:

<!-- W -->
*If the bearer sinks, the next takes the flag, and if that one sinks, go home.*

They had cut their own defeat into the plan.

<!-- HALYNA -->

"There's one hand in all that carving, not a council's."

"And the hand is young."

<!-- HALYNA END -->

But it was the last two words that held us. To the young wood of the first fleets, *home* had only meant the place hurt things run to. Here it was carved in on purpose, as an order.

The three of us looked at one another across the lamp, the way wives look up when their men have lain a long time in fever and then ask for their boots.

<!-- HALYNA -->

"They carve a tomorrow now, the way we do."

"Then let's build a tomorrow they didn't carve for."

<!-- HALYNA END -->

The lamp went out on the shingle, and I didn't fill it a third time.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

## BOOK SIX · THE BOOK OF THE HOMECOMINGS

*How our ten cities on the coast came home to us, and how the war went home.*

### VI.1 · The Homecomings

*Told in the year we took back our ten cities, each part at that city's own fire, on the night we won it back from the great enemy ship we called its Throne.*

*The True Men, the Captain's own company of twelve, laid this tale at each city's own fire, one man for each city, and I wrote each part down right there beside him. (At our hearth, whoever tells a tale holds the old gate-stone, and we say a tale is laid, like a stone in a wall, because a laid tale stays.) Kael the Counter laid the first words and the last here at the Long Hearth, the great fireplace in the Keep's hall, once the tenth city was ours again. Before he'd begin, he counted my pages.*

Give me the stone. No, I'll stand.

That last winter, whenever we sank a fleet's leading ship and the one that took its place, the fleet went home. Then at the winter's end ten great enemy ships of living wood came out of the fog and settled in our ruined cities, one to each. The soldiers called them Thrones.

So we went down from the Keep to take back the havens, our ten cities on the coast. We went by the old roads over the mountains, the True Men and their troops first and the masons behind. All the way down, the snow-water ran beside us. In every haven a Throne sat among the fallen walls, and waited.

Here at this hearth, years ago, each of us True Men swore what he would do for his own haven when it came home. I'm the oath-keeper, and I held all ten. Where a man had fallen, another spoke for him.

Now hear the names of our havens, and hold on to them, because they're ours again.

**Eldhythe.**

*Voss, speaking for Corlen.*

Eldhythe was Corlen's city, and mine. The first walls of all the havens were laid here, and here they stand again. The Tide-Heart, the Throne that sat here, sank into the sea it came from and never rose. Who should bring the first boat home? Corlen should have. He swore at our hearth that he would. But he lies under the east cairns now, our graves on the Keep's east face, and I hold his guns. So the tiller came to my hand.

I wet a finger and held it up to the wind, the way he did before every fight, though the wind told me nothing. I brought her in slowly on the evening tide. Then I waited, as he would have, till the sea had finished. That was his word to his gunners: "Wait. The sea hasn't finished."

On the quay my grandmother's harbour-house stood without a roof. Over the cold hearth, three grey stones were still set in the wall, old carved stones from the edge of the fog that no one could read. The lowest was still scarred where the ship-masters knocked out their pipes on it. They're in my pack tonight. They've hung over a dead fire long enough.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

**Tidesmeet.**

*Tamm of the three spans.*

Our city stood on both banks where the river meets the tide, and three great bridges joined it, the three spans. The Throne here, the Bearer of New Branches, had three heads. We struck all three in the same fight, and none of them grew back. It went down where the middle bridge had stood. Then on the bank I set out my three stones in their order, one from each fallen bridge, the way I set them out by my blanket every night of the war. One span is a plank. Three is a city.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

**Fenholm.**

*Marl the fen-born.*

Fenholm stood on wooden piles in a marsh, and it sank a hand's breadth every lifetime, so each generation laid a new course of stone on top of the old. We fen-folk always joked about it: "A town is just the last course on the old." Tonight we said it again, and laughed. The Grain That Rots went down into the fen like a log gone soft, and the rot went out of our mortar.

My grandmother, Old Ebba, carried a stone of our house's last course out of the fen on her back. She didn't live to see it home. I laid her stone on the old course, as she told me to. If you'd asked her whether it would hold, she'd have said, "Perhaps."

It hasn't sunk.

*At that fire, too, everyone waited a breath for Ebba, because most nights at the Long Hearth she had begun our answer a breath before the rest.*

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

**Holtward.**

*Aske of the canopy.*

Holtward was built so close among the trees, up under their leaves, that a traveller could walk right beneath it and never see a wall. Among those trees we found the Root-Heart standing still. It had put down roots, and it wouldn't rise. "These were trees once," I told my men. "Mind your hands." Not one axe was lifted. We left it standing. It had come home.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

**Carnhold.**

*Garvel of the smithies.*

Carnhold rang all day with the hammers of its smiths. Now it was quiet. The Stone-Grain crumbled away, stone into stone, and the cairns of the first quarrymen, the old stone grave-mounds our town was built among, took it in. In a cold smithy I struck the anvil once, just to hear it. It rang true. The first thing I make on it won't be a gun.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

**Emberhythe.**

*Harl of the burning shore.*

Emberhythe stood where the mountain smokes down into the sea. When it fell, one of our masons carried a charred plank of their wood out of it in his pack, and I told him then to burn it. When at last we learned what its carving said, it was one word: *Burn*.

The Burning, the Throne there, went out like a forge nobody tends. Our mason went down to the black crust where it died, and on that crust he laid his plank.

I didn't burn it.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

**Sandreach.**

*Ulden of the galleries.*

Sandreach was dug, not built, gallery below gallery into the wall of a red canyon. For an hour the canyon shook, when the Deep-Beneath burst up under the city's eastern stronghold and broke apart. When it was still, I went down to the lowest gallery and laid my ear to the ground. I heard nothing. Every night since my city fell, I've slept out on the open ground with my ear to it. Tonight I sleep under my own roof of stone.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

**Rimewatch.**

*Kael, speaking for Hesk.*

I don't tell other men's cities. This one I have to.

Rimewatch stood beside a frozen lake, the mere, and all winter its great hall stood out on the ice. Hesk's mother kept that hall's lamps, and at dusk the fishers out on the ice steered home by them. On the day we came to take it back, the mere lay white all morning, and in the white the Long Winter, the Throne there, waited.

Hesk stood on the shore with his mother's lamp lit in his hand, in broad daylight. For six winters he had lit that lamp at dusk on our north wall, and the night-watch steered by it. "Someone has to keep a light," he told us once at our hearth.

Before noon he went out onto the ice alone. From the shore I counted his steps. At two hundred and twelve he stopped, on the spot where the great hall had stood. When Rimewatch fell, that hall burned, and he went in through the smoke and carried his mother's lamp out of it, still lit, across ice only a night old. Now the ice groaned under him, the way it had groaned on that night.

That night, it had held.

At noon the white lifted, and the ice broke under the Long Winter and took it down.

The ice took Hesk too.

At dusk we went out along the break with ropes, and found his lamp on the ice, still burning. I swear to that.

He came home.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

***Added in Seren's hand:*** *That night, at the Keep, no lamp burned on the north wall. Then a fisher-girl set one there and lit it, and said, "Someone has to keep a light."*

**Highreach.**

*Lanner of the crags.*

Highreach was a tower on crags so high that every stone of it went up by crane. When it fell, the wind came a whole day ahead of their sails, and I smelled the grey on it.

The Blood of the Sky came down out of its cloud and never went up again. Then I climbed the highest crag, as I swore at our hearth I would, and whistled for the wind. The wind came. It brought no sails.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

**Glasspire.**

*Ruan, speaking for Pellow.*

Glasspire was the last and proudest of our cities. In the dark of its cellars the Silvered, the Throne there, broke like a mirror, and every piece of it showed us our own lamps. Pellow should have seen it first. It was his city, and he swore at our hearth that his would be the first eye on it. But he died on our wall, and I was raised a True Man in his place. I have his glass. "Look close," he always told us. I looked close, and I looked for him.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

*Then Kael took the stone in his hands again:*

Tonight in the ward, the Keep's courtyard, I called the roll of the True Men. Fourteen names, the Twelve and the two raised since in dead men's places, and eleven to answer. At Corlen's name the ward was quiet for a breath, and at Pellow's. At Hesk's name a lamp was lit on the north wall, and no answer came down.

I come from no haven, because the Captain found me on a road. This year I sat at ten fires. At every one, when the answer came, *We remember*, it was this hearth I heard. In our first winter here, we gave the Keep's name to our whole land. Every haven is Rivenkeep. I swear to that.

Ten havens. I counted them twice, and it didn't take long.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

**Declined, and why**
- **A gloss for *Ael*, *-thae* or *-thar*.** The newcomer felt the gap next to *Rhenear, the stone-deaf*, and Jack wants the romanized words to carry their English. But taking these names apart is the one thing §4.2 forbids, and Seren's *I won't write what I thought* is the hook. *Aelthae* is the name of a Tide and *Ael'thar* is the name of a ceremony. That is all the Plain may say. Jack's word-by-word gloss belongs to the Original tab's interlinear team.
- **Why the Thrones fall, and why Hesk walked out.** VI.1 tells how each Throne went down, not why. VI.2 is the wood's side. Hesk's reason is a §7 keep-dark. I gave the reader the bridge (fleets going home) but no cause.
- **Whether the south was struck, and whether Part III's plan worked that winter.** The Book doesn't say inside V.7. Kael's bridge in VI.1 answers the second in his own words.
- **Why Pellow didn't move his own guns.** The Captain's rule now explains the slowness. Pellow's own choice stays as the Book leaves it.
- **How Seren knew about the north wall that night** (she was at the city fires). The Book's line says *At the Keep that night*, and I kept it rather than invent how word reached her.
- **The repeated headnote glosses in VI.1** (*the Captain's own company of twelve*, *laid*). The newcomer skimmed them when reading back to back, but every tale must stand alone (§1.1).
- **The forge simile in Emberhythe.** It's the Book's picture (*like a forge left untended*), so it stays. I moved Carnhold to *the hammers of its smiths* so the two don't echo.
- **"Tide" and "the tide turned".** Both stay. *The wall calls each year's enemy fleets a Tide* is said once, and the literal tide is the Book's clock for when wreck-wood comes in.
- **Glasspire retelling Pellow's death.** It's needed for VI.1 to stand alone, so it stays in two short sentences.
