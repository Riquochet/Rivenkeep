### IV.5 · The Burning Boat

*Told in the second summer of our siege at the Keep, the old fortress on a mountain ridge above the sea where we made our stand. That was the year of the Second Tide, as we on the wall called that year's enemy fleets. The tale goes back to a harbour story from our grandparents' time, and it tells of Voss's friend Jory.*

*Voss who asked laid this tale. At our hearth, whoever holds the old gate-stone is the one who tells, and we say a tale is laid, like a stone in a wall, because a laid tale stays. Voss is a soldier from Eldhythe, the oldest of the ten cities on our coast, which the enemy burned, every one, before we fled up here. We call him Voss who asked for a question he once put to this hearth, why we fight for ice and stone. Tonight's tale answers an older question of his, one he asked as a boy. His grandmother Elspet told it to him by her fire on the quay at Eldhythe. The old ship-masters would never tell it, and the children would never stop singing its rhyme. Everyone on shore laughed at it. The sea didn't.*

Give me the stone. This tale isn't mine. It's my grandmother's. She had it from her brother, and he had it from the sea.

It was in the first holding of this war, the first time the enemy's grey sails came against our wall and we had to hold it. I heard one of their ships burning on the shingle, the pebble beach below the wall. It screamed. All along the wall the old ship-masters, who had captained our cities' boats their whole lives, turned their faces away from the sea, and Merrick, the eldest of them, turned first. Over on the east wall, my friend Jory was whistling.

A rhyme started going round and round in me, like a skipping rope. I'd skipped to it when I was seven. At forty, I understood it.

When I was a boy at my grandmother's table, I asked her why the ship-masters wouldn't listen to that rhyme. She answered me with a tale. You can weigh it for yourselves.

When she was twelve, she kept the fire in her mother's harbour-house, an alehouse on the quay where the ship-masters drank and traded. They came in out of the rain with the weather still in their beards. Over the fire hung three flat grey stones, carved with letters as fine as the tracks of birds on wet sand. Nobody knew what they said. Only the children ever asked. The ship-masters knocked out their pipes on the lowest one.

Whenever some bold man came home from out by the grey, the great fog bank out at sea, with a tale to tell, my grandmother laughed and filled his cup. "The Mystlands make liars of honest men," she said. The Mystlands was the old harbour name for whatever lies out there inside the grey.

She said it about her own brother.

Daveth was an axeman of the felling. In those days our cities sent men by boat out to the edge of the grey, where tall black trees grew, and the axemen cut them down for timber. That summer Daveth was coming home in one of the last timber-boats, riding low in the water with its load of black wood, and a second boat was following in his wake.

Out of the grey, a boat came at them, burning. It came into the wind and against the current, which no boat can do. It came screaming, the way gulls come screaming at boys who rob their nests. It struck both boats low down, where the load rode heaviest, and the black wood in their holds caught fire from it. After that came the rain, falling on the heads of two crews in the water.

Three men came home.

Daveth came home clinging to a spar, a broken length of mast, with the two other men who lived, and the sea ran out of his coat onto the floor my grandmother had swept. That's how she told it to me. All that autumn the three of them told their tale, and none of them ever set foot in a boat again.

Soon the men of the outpost were swearing to things too, over their drinks. The outpost was our last post at the edge of the land, out toward the grey. They swore a boat had been pushed off burning, with a dead man in it, a stranger who'd come out of the grey. Far out, they swore, it turned. Most of all they swore to the scream, and to three words of our own language that the grey man had spoken. But which three words, no two of them agreed.

My grandmother counted the boats for me on her fingers, the way her brother had counted them for her. One finger, then another. Then she laid her hand flat on the table.

There were two boats.

Now, when the ship-masters speak of it at all, they say twenty.

Everyone on shore laughed at the three from the spar. By day my grandmother laughed along with everyone else, and filled the three men's cups under the lowest stone.

At night she didn't laugh.

Daveth took to drink over his tale, and the harbour drowned him a stone's throw from his mother's harbour-house.

So why wouldn't the ship-masters listen to it? I've seen one of them leave his cup and walk out into the rain rather than hear it. Since then I've heard three of them speak of it, on the Road, our long march over the mountain passes to this Keep. Each of them set his few words down carefully, the way you'd set down a full cup.

"A small boat burning, far out in the grey," said the first. The second said, "It turned into the wind, and after it came weather." The third said, "It's the boat that comes for you when you die." And the first one spoke again: "Let the shore laugh. The shore will never have to meet it."

The cities had it written down in their records: an armed stranger had come and been put down, and his boat had been burned and set adrift. And who's going to argue with writing?

But the children had a rhyme for it, and children will argue with anything. We skipped to it on the quay, two turning the rope and one jumping. Jory and I turned the rope, because we were the biggest and the slowest, and for the littlest ones Jory turned it slower still.

*Then the children sang, as they still sing it:*

> *Grey boat, grey boat, out of the grey,*  
> *knelt at the warden's door one day.*  
> *A stone for a present, a cut for a kiss.*  
> *Who ever heard of a greeting like this?*  
> *Burn it and push it and give it to the tide.*  
> *But the grey boat turned, and the grey boat cried.*

There were two more lines, and we never sang them, in case a ship-master heard.

> *Grey boat burning, and after it, the rain.*  
> *And the one it comes for won't come home again.*

One evening Merrick heard us at it. He took our rope and coiled it over his arm, the way a sailor coils a line he means to keep. "Not that one," he said.

His father was Daveth, my grandmother's brother, one of the three who came home on the spar. Merrick and I are kin.

Merrick is sitting by the door tonight. He hasn't gone out.

All that first winter, Jory whistled it on the east wall, the tune and never the words. He meant to drink at my grandmother's fire again when the havens came home, when we'd taken back our ten burnt cities. Every morning Merrick told him to stop, and every morning Jory laughed. "It's only a rhyme."

On the morning he died, I was at his side, and the tune went ahead of him along the wall-walk. He whistled it slowly, the way he used to turn the rope for the littlest of us. Then the rhyme pulled tight in me, like a mooring-rope when the tide comes in, and I heard the two lines we never sang.

Merrick told him to stop.

I did not tell him to stop.

Below us a fire-ship came in onto the stones, burning. It screamed as it came.

The tune stopped.

His is the first cairn on the east face, where the ground is too hard to dig a grave.

If my grandmother's house still stands in burnt Eldhythe, the three grey stones are hanging there over a cold hearth, with no roof over them but the rain.

We forget. We've always been good at that. But Mystwood, the great wood out there in the Mystlands, remembers.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

## BOOK FIVE · THE BOOK OF THE TIDES

*The war: a doom set in motion by the dead, crewed by the living, against a people who had never known they were the foe.*

### V.1 · The First Glyph

*Told in the first winter of the siege, at the end of our very first month at the Keep, the old fortress on a mountain ridge above the sea. It tells of the night, a few days before, when we beat back the enemy's first attack on our wall.*

*I, Seren, who write this Book, laid this tale. (We say a tale is laid, like a stone in a wall, and whoever lays one holds the old gate-stone by the fire.) They call me Seren Two-Inks, for the two inks I write {{Halyna}}'s words in, one ink for each of the two people that name stands for. That night I held the lamp. There's still sand from that beach caught in the binding of this Book.*

Hold the stone, Seren.

Down on the shingle, the pebble beach under our wall, {{Halyna}} held the wood, and I held the lamp. {{Halyna}} are Halvard and Rhyna, who lead our guild of master builders here at the Keep. They're a bonded pair, joined as one from birth, and they work as one, like the two hands of a single mason. They grew up to be husband and wife, and the guild gave them one name, made from both of theirs. That night their four hands lay on the grain, and my one hand held the light. It began that way, and it has gone on that way ever since.

For nearly a month the grey sails, the enemy's fleet, had lain off this wall, and none of us knew what they were. We knew only that they'd come out of the great fog bank at sea, burned our ten cities on the coast one after another, and never shown a living soul on their decks. On the night we broke their first attack, the tide brought one of their wrecks in under the wall: grey planks, broken ribs, and a keel as long as a gate. It rolled over and over in the shallows, as if the sea were turning each piece to look at its underside.

The old ship-masters on the wall wouldn't go near it. But the soldiers went down and made fires of it all along the tide-line, because firewood was scarce up on our mountain ridge. "Burn it," snapped Harl of the burning shore. He's one of the True Men, the Captain's own company of twelve, and he comes from a forge-town on a volcanic coast. He broke one of the wreck's ribs across his knee and fed it into the flames. One by one the fires went up, the way lamps are lit along a quay at dusk. The Captain, who leads us all, let them burn most of it. He stood among the fires and didn't move.

While the first fires were catching, Hale the runner came up from the water's edge. He was seventeen that winter, and the fastest on the wall. He went past the fires, and past the Captain himself, straight to {{Halyna}}.

"Marks on the wood."

When the tide turned, {{Halyna}} went down, Halvard's hand under Rhyna's elbow on the wet stones. I came behind them with the lamp. {{Halyna}} had taken me into their household as their third, and the light was mine to carry. The men hauled one plank up out of the surf, and I set the lamp down beside it.

One shape ran along its grain, as fine and shallow as the first light line a mason scratches on a stone before he cuts it. One long edge of the plank was raw, split along the grain, as if the rest had been torn away.

Until then, the only things we had that might speak to us, if we could ever get at their words, were the mute stones and a sealed bundle, both in the archive chest, the guild's old chest of records from Eldhythe, our oldest city. The mute stones are carved with letters no one can read. The bundle was sealed with the mark of {{Wendhessa}}, the bonded pair who kept the guild's records there, and no bonded pair would break another pair's seal. Now we had this plank. In our grandfathers' day, the axemen who cut black timber at the edge of the fog used to swear over their drinks that the black wood would put a thought into a man's hand, if he rested it there long enough. Everyone on shore had laughed at them. This wood was grey, not black. But we had nothing else left to try.

So Rhyna sat down beside the plank and laid her palms flat on the grain. Halvard knelt behind her and laid his hands over hers. Neither of them spoke. The tide came and went. The lamp burned down a finger's width.

Then the knowing came up into Rhyna's palms. That's what we call it now, a knowing, because it isn't reading: what's carved in the wood comes up into the hands whole, and you know it the way you know a thing you remember. It burned, she said, but not like fire. It burned like cold iron in the depth of winter. And its shape was in the burning: one stroke, curling forward, like a wave about to break. She hadn't said a word, but Halvard lifted his head.

"Shore," he said.

Rhyna said he was right.

Halvard couldn't say how he knew it. It was as if someone had put the word into his mouth, he said, and then gone away. They knew more than that one word. But the rest was thought the way a forest thinks, and all they had to hold it with was the way we think, we people of the shore. They held on until the lamp was low, and one word was all they could tell us.

They got up off the stones, stiff, and others came down to try. First came Jory of Eldhythe, a soldier of the east wall, who's sitting tonight at the back of this hall, beside his friend Voss. He laid his hand on the plank, laughing. "Wet planks," he said, and he went off up the shingle whistling an old harbour tune that the ship-masters can't bear, the tune and never the words.

Then came Enno and Della, the youngest bonded pair in our guild, who were fifteen when they walked up the mountain to the Keep hand in hand. They laid their four hands on the plank together. He said the wood was cold, and she said it wasn't, and they were both right.

When they'd gone, I sat down where Rhyna had sat, and alone I laid my own palm on the grain. Something came up into it out of the wood, far too big to hold. It was a river in flood, and I was a girl with a cup.

I let go.

Halvard put the lamp back in my hand.

Before we went back up, I opened this Book on my knees to a clean page, though pages were precious then. In the middle of it I wrote one word, *shore*, and nothing else. Before the ink touched the page, I said the word over three times, as I do with every word, to hear whether it would hold. It held. It was short and square, and it sat in the mouth the way a well-cut stone sits in its bed of mortar.

On the facing page I drew the sign. In the sea air the ink wouldn't dry.

The word looked very small on its page.

We came back up with the fires burning low behind us. At the gate {{Halyna}} stopped, and spoke the way they do, one of them beginning and the other finishing.

### What I declined, and why

- **Mention the warden in the outpost paragraph** (newcomer fix 7). The outpost's warden is H16 (primer §3.3), and the fidelity critic counted *no warden beyond the rhyme* as a pass. *Warden* is a plain English word for a keeper, and the newcomer guessed right (*the commander of the outpost*) without help. The rhyme can be a little cryptic, as a children's rhyme is.
- ***A stone for a present* as a hint at the three grey stones.** The rhyme is fixed verse, and the gift is H16 and H19. The newcomer's half-guess is a reader's own, and the Plain neither feeds it nor closes it off.
- **A physical cause for Jory's death** (newcomer L81). The Book leaves *Below us a fire-ship came in on the stones, burning* as it is. The newcomer read it as the rhyme coming true, and that's the Book's weight. Adding a cause would be adding a reason the Book doesn't give (primer §1.2).
- **Joining the mute stones to IV.5's harbour stones** (newcomer L121). At H3 the hearth knows only that the chest holds stones with letters no one can read. Their link to the harbour-houses is H11, and Elspet's three are a plant that the Last Note pays off (fidelity fix 1). The newcomer may wonder, and that's allowed.
- **The Argument** (newcomer: TWICE). It's fixed matter (primer §1.7), and the newcomer took it as a riddle worth reading on for.
- ***The tide came and went*** (newcomer: tiny). The Book means a long wait, and the lamp's oil burning down a finger's width can take that long. *They held on until the lamp was low* follows. I kept the Book's line.
- **Making Hale or Harl nameless to thin the Ha- names** (newcomer fix 3). Every name stays (brief rule 6). I thinned the sentences around them instead: Harl's temper sentence is gone and he gained no city name, and Hale's gloss is one short sentence.
- **The shingle and the grey sails glossed again in V.1** (newcomer: BORED). Each tale must stand alone (brief rule 4), so these short glosses stay. Both are half a clause.
- **More about *their third*, *pages were precious then*, and Enno and Della's cold and not-cold.** The newcomer was intrigued, not stopped. *Third* is H7, and the other two are the Book's own small mysteries.
