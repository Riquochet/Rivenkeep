## OF THIS BOOK

> **A note for new readers**
>
> The people in these tales come from the Shorelands, a long coast where the mountains run down to the sea. They lived in ten cities along it, which they called the havens. Every wall in the havens was raised by the Stonewrights, a guild of master builders sworn never to take up a weapon. The Stonewrights work in bonded pairs, which the guild calls the Bonded: two people laid in one cradle as babies, who have lived and worked as one ever since. Each pair goes by one name made from both of theirs. Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead the builders, are called {{Halyna}}.
>
> Far out at sea lies the grey, a great bank of fog that no ship of theirs ever sailed into. Out of it came grey ships. Over twenty months they burned the havens one after another, and nobody alive ever understood a word from them.
>
> The survivors, two thousand and a few hundred of them, walked for a month over the mountain passes to the one place the old songs said would hold: the Keep, called Rivenkeep, an ancient fortress on a high ridge split down the middle. The ridge stands where the mountains meet the sea, so the Keep's wall looks down on a pebble beach and open water. They found the Keep in ruins. There a soldier these tales call only the Captain raised a banner on the broken wall. The people made their stand behind that wall, soldiers at the guns and builders at the stones, and year after year the grey fleets came against it. That long stand is the siege.
>
> In the Keep's old hall is a great fireplace, the Long Hearth. Each night the people gather there to tell tales, or to *lay* them, as they say. Seren, a girl of the builders' guild who was twelve when they reached the Keep, writes the tales down. This is her Book. Each tale opens with a line in italics that says when it was told.

*Written in the third summer of the siege, in the year of the Tide of Remembering, as the wall named that year's enemy fleets.*

*By Seren Two-Inks, who writes this Book, and who is the third member of the household of {{Halyna}}.*

It's summer, when the nights are short. The hall is asleep around me, and the Long Hearth has burned down to coals. Up on the wall I can see our banner lifting in a wind I can't hear. The Captain, who leads us, raised it there on the evening we first came to this Keep.

This Book is half full now. One of the people who told its tales, who used to hold up a wet finger to every wind, already lies under the east cairns, the heaps of stones we pile over our dead on the Keep's east side. Tonight I've turned back to the first page. I left it empty when I began, because a mason never cuts the lintel, the stone that lies across the top of a doorway, until both posts are standing.

Long ago, the first builders of this Keep told their tales at this same hearth. When they left the Keep, the tales went cold along with the fire. Now we sit where they sat, at the end of the day, when there's nothing left for our hands to do but hold them out to the fire.

Beside me, in its hollow in the wall, sits the stone. Once it was the capstone of the Keep's inner gate, the great stone that lay across the top of the gate's two posts. It had fallen, and it lay face down in the gateway with the vow cut into its underside pressed against the earth, until {{Halyna}} turned it over at dusk on the day of the banner, the day we came here. It's a stone that takes two people to turn. Its vow is still sharp. Whoever tells a tale at this hearth holds it.

The Stonwryt, the first builders' vow, on the underside of the tale-stone, in their old writing, the Hal
*This wall will stand. We two laid it, and we two answer for it, as long as stone is stone. It stands.* It is signed with one pair-name under one lintel: {{Aldwena}}, who laid that top stone.
:::

{{Aldwena}} were a bonded pair from the days when the Keep was first built, and they signed with one name made from both of theirs, written under one stroke, like a lintel resting on two posts. That's how I write every bonded pair in this Book. It's one name, and it's the name of two people.

{{Halyna}} are Halvard Stone-Warden and Rhyna of the Inner Gate. {{Idrenna}}, the oldest pair among us, were their teachers, and they sit by the door and nod when a tale is laid true. {{Orvenna}}, the masters of our wall, never raise their voices. And of {{Enrella}}, our youngest pair, neither one can start a joke without the other finishing it.

When a tale is finished, we don't say it was told. We say it was *laid*, the way a mason lays a stone. A tale that's only told is gone by morning. A tale that's laid becomes part of the wall.

Every tale of ours gets the same two words in answer, and never any others: *We remember.*

On the first night this hearth asked for a tale, none of us knew what to say when it was over. Old Ebba, from the marsh town of Fenholm, said those two words into the silence. Nobody asked her to, and nobody asked the rest of us to join in. But the hearth took the words from her. Most nights she's still the first to say them, a breath ahead of everyone, and the rest of us come in behind her, like the tide coming in behind its first wave.

Our fathers never chose to forget. Forgetting just came over them. Remembering is something we choose, the way a mason chooses which stones to keep.

Some of the tales in this Book are the wood's own, memories the wood holds in its grain. The enemy's ships are made of wood, and pieces of their wrecks, carved with signs, wash up on the beach below our wall. Most of the wood's tales come out of that wreck-wood. Our written records forget things, and they can be made to lie. The wood can't lie. But it can't see fear.

One of the wood's tales came out of a dark stone instead. It lay in the chest at my feet, our guild's chest of old records, in a bundle sealed with the mark of {{Wendhessa}}, the guild's record-keepers at Eldhythe, the oldest of our cities. They carried this chest out when Eldhythe fell. Beside the bundle lie some carved stones that no one can read, which we call the mute stones. No pair of our guild ever breaks another pair's seal, and none would break this one until the wood showed {{Halyna}} what the mute stones were. Then {{Idrenna}} gave their leave.

The wood isn't read, the way you'd read a page. It's *known*. When {{Halyna}} lay their hands on it, what it holds comes into them whole, the way you know something you remember. So they give it to us in pieces, one of them taking up where the other's words run out.

I write the pieces down just as they come, in two inks, one for each of them. They won't let me mark which ink belongs to which of them. You can't tell. Out of the pieces I make the tale. After a wood tale the hearth is silent, and I've written that silence down too.

{{Halyna}} want each of the wood's tales set on the right-hand page, facing our own tale of the same things. As they put it, one of them beginning and the other finishing:

<!-- HALYNA -->

"A tale told from one side of a wall,"

"is only half a tale."

<!-- HALYNA END -->

Where the wood's side of a tale can't be told yet, I've left its page empty except for the grain of the wood, the shape of what we don't understand yet.

I'm not one of the Bonded myself, though {{Halyna}} took me into their household as its third, the place our guild gives a child who has no pair. When they come to know the wood, I hold the lamp. Once I laid my own palm on the grain. What came up into it was more than I could hold, and I let go. Mine is the hand that writes, not the hand that knows.

Every scribe of our guild before me was one of a pair. I write alone.

The Captain listens to every tale, tells none, and keeps his hood up.

Where a carving was broken, I haven't mended it with guesses. Where the guild cut a name out of its records, I've put a blot in its place. Where the wood speaks, I've written down its own names for things, not ours.

Each night I set the tale down in our language, in the course-hand, the square letters our masons cut in stone. I lay my words the way a mason lays a course, a row of stones, with every stone set across the joint below it, so that no crack can run straight down through the wall. I want a tale to hold together like that. I say every word out loud three times before I write it, to hear whether it will hold. The tale belongs to its teller. The words are mine. I read each page back to the teller who laid it, and no teller has asked for his own words back yet.

Most nights the coals have burned to ash while the last word is still in my mouth. Tonight is the same.

I love our language.

On the long road up the mountain to this Keep, I carried two things: our language and a chest. Only the chest was heavy.

For now this Book is only ink. {{Halyna}} want it carved into the granite walls of this hall, row on row like the stones of a wall, when peace comes. Until then it lies in that chest, among the mute stones.

## BOOK ONE · THE BOOK OF THE WALL

*How we came to make our stand.*

### I.1 · The Torn Cloak

*Told in the first winter of the siege, on the night after the Rite of the Cornerstone, the ceremony that made the Captain our Commander. It tells of the evening we first came to the Keep, two weeks before.*

*Kael the Counter laid this tale on the first night this hearth ever asked for one. Whoever holds the old gate-stone is the one who tells, and we say a tale is "laid," like a stone in a wall, because a laid tale stays. Every teller since has closed with Kael's last line. He told me not to change a single word of his. I changed a good many, and when I read it back to him, he swore it was word for word what he'd said.*

Give me the stone. No, I'll stand. I've done enough sitting this winter.

I swear by the stone over the western stair. Long ago, when this Keep was built, the wardens of the old quarry, the men who cut its stone, began carving their names there, each son's name under his father's. I'm a counter, not a singer. I count heads, and stones, and days. What I didn't count, I won't tell you.

At dusk the column came in under the outer gate, all of us who were left, walking in one long line. I heard it before I saw it: a great many feet, and not one voice. I stood at the gate and counted it in, head by head.

Old men came in, and women carrying bundles, and children so tired they were asleep on their feet. Soldiers came in from six broken companies, men who no longer knew who their officers were. Last of all came the Captain, who had guarded the back of the column the whole way. Two thousand and a few hundred, all told.

We'd walked for a month from the ashes of Glasspire, the last of our ten cities on the coast to burn. The old songs told of a castle up here, where the mountains meet the sea, the one place that would hold when nothing else did, and we'd come to find it. What we found was a heap of stones. The gate hung by one hinge. In the courtyard the grass stood as high as your knee. The songs had put a great hall here, but its roof had fallen in, and snow lay on the hearth where you're sitting now.

Some people shook. Some cried. Some fell down and wouldn't get up.

Then the shouting started, and it wasn't one shout. Some shouted that we should go and beg the grey sails, the enemy's fleet, for peace, though nobody alive had ever understood a single word from them. Some shouted that we should cross the high passes, into the snow beyond them. Some shouted that we should go down into the cellars, bar the doors and wait, as if the sea might forget us.

On the long retreat from our burning cities I'd heard men break. Until that dusk I'd never heard a whole people break. It sounds like the ice on a river breaking up in spring.

The wind came down off the riven ridge, the great split ridge the Keep is built on, and it had the whole of winter in its mouth.

People looked for a general, but the generals were dead. They looked for the lords, but the lords were scattered. They looked to the Stonewrights, the guild of master builders who had raised every wall on our coast and kept the faith of our land too, and the Stonewrights were sitting in the grass with their chisels in their laps.

Nobody looked to the Captain. He was only the captain of one small company, and that company was us. The whole long road he'd been waiting for someone whose place it was to lead, and nobody came, because they were all dead. Don't mistake me: there's no greater man alive, and I don't think his equal has ever lived. But he never went looking to lead. I swear to that.

We True Men, the Captain's own company of twelve, looked to our Captain. And the Captain looked at the wall.

Then he climbed it. In the ruined hall he'd picked up a coal from the cold hearth, this same hearth, and none of us knew why. He went up the broken face of the wall alone, using the stones that stuck out of the old mortar. We stood at the bottom and let him go, because he hadn't told us to follow. I've counted those stones since. There are thirty-one, and four of them wobble.

At the top he took off his cloak. It had been crimson once, before the long road here wore it down to the colour of old brick. In that wind it was all he had to keep him warm.

Nobody in the courtyard said a word. Somewhere among the women a child coughed, and his mother hushed him. After that there was nothing but the wind.

He tore the cloak in two, down the back.

It made a small sound, the sound old cloth makes when it gives. I heard it over the wind.

He spread one half on the stones along the top of the wall, knelt on it with the coal, and wrote. He wrote slowly, the way men write who haven't written much. He tied that half to a pike, a long spear he'd found in the rubble. The other half, with nothing written on it, he put back around his shoulders.

I was the first up the wall after him, so I was the first to read what we now call the Title. He'd written it in the course-hand, our own square letters, and his hand hadn't shaken once. I turned to the courtyard and read it out loud. My voice wasn't steady, and I won't pretend it was.

**IN MEMORY OF OUR HOME, OUR FAMILIES, OUR GOD, OUR FREEDOMS, OUR PEACE.**

He wedged the pike into a crack in the wall and gave it to the wind, and the wind took it. That was the last wall in the Shorelands, our whole country by the sea, that the enemy hadn't broken, and that half of a cloak was the banner flying over it.
<!-- PROLOGUE EXCERPT END -->

Now hear who answered, and in what order, because I counted them. A count isn't a count until it has names.

The True Men answered first. The Captain had found us one at a time, in garrisons and on roads, and he bound us to him with nothing but trust. We kept our oaths. We built what we promised. We never fought except in a just cause. That's the whole of a True Man. I tell you so you'll know it isn't much. It isn't little, either.

Each of the others goes by the place he came from. Stannard is a quarryman of this very ridge, and the other ten come one from each of the havens, our ten cities on the coast. Stannard of the ridge. Corlen of the harbour. Tamm of the three spans. Marl the fen-born. Aske of the canopy. Garvel of the smithies. Harl of the burning shore. Ulden of the galleries. Hesk of the mere. Lanner of the crags. Pellow of the spire. And me, Kael the Counter, their oath-keeper, the one who holds the oaths they swear. I come from no haven, because the Captain found me on a road.

Twelve. I counted us twice, and it didn't take long.

After us the soldiers of the six companies answered. They're split now into twelve troops, one troop to each of us True Men, and they trust us the way we trust him. Then the people of the column answered, the ones who had carried their children on their backs through the mountain passes. One voice. Then a dozen. Then hundreds, until I couldn't count them any more.

I stopped counting. It's the only time I ever have.

He never gave an order. He only wrote down what we had left, and it was enough.

We fly a pennant over this Keep, a long crimson flag, and we'll fly one over every keep we ever raise. It's the colour of that cloak, the colour it was before the road wore it down.

Look at the end of the bench, you children at the front. There he sits, with his hood up.

The other half of the cloak is on his back.

He'll tell you it wasn't like that. It was like that.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

### I.2 · The Cry of the Bonded

*Told in the first winter of the siege, a few nights after the enemy's first attack on our wall. It tells of the evening we first came to the Keep, the old fortress high on the mountain ridge.*

*This tale is my own, Seren's, and the first I ever laid. Only one tale had been laid at this hearth before it. Whoever tells here holds the old gate-stone. On the day I'm telling about, I was twelve years old, one of the children of our builders' guild.*

Tonight I was afraid to speak, and {{Halyna}} took my two hands and laid them on the stone. That's one name for two people, Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders here.

<!-- HALYNA -->

"Hold the stone."

"Hold it, and it will hold you."

<!-- HALYNA END -->

So I'm holding it.

At dusk on the day of the banner, the guild sat in the snowy grass of the Keep's old courtyard. That's what we call the day we reached the Keep, because that evening the Captain, who was then only the captain of one small company of soldiers, climbed the broken wall, tore his cloak in two, wrote on one half, and raised it as our banner.

We were the Stonewrights, some hundreds of us, the guild of master builders who had raised every wall on our coast, and we sat with our chisels in our laps. The last of the daylight lay on the snow, as thin as ash. Up on the wall the soldiers were cheering the banner. Down in the grass, we said nothing.

I sat there too, with no craft yet except carrying water. All the way over the high mountain passes I'd walked beside a boy of the guild who knotted a net as he went, and at night I slept beside him.

I came into that courtyard alone. He never reached the Keep.

A little after me, two children came in holding hands. They were Enno and Della, both fifteen. Our guild works in bonded pairs, two builders joined as one from birth, who work as one, and we call all such pairs the Bonded. Even the guild's children come in pairs. Of all the children's pairs, Enno and Della were the only one that came up the mountain whole, both of them alive. They sat down together and held on.

We had watched ten havens, our ten cities along the coast, die in twenty months. The grey sails, the enemy's ships out of the fog at sea, had burned them one after another. We walked away from every one of them with our chisels packed. We never lifted a hand to fight, because our creed forbade it: *Build, do not destroy. Protect, do not attack.* Mortar and chisels were the only weapons we had, and they hadn't been enough.

Twelve generations of our faith still sat on us like a wet cloak. Every elder there was turning over the same hard thought: that walls built for peace must in the end be held with blood. And if that was so, whose blood? Whose hands?

Back then I didn't know {{Halyna}}, except as two elders who sat apart under the broken inner gate and spoke to no one, because they didn't need to. Long ago, when the Keep was first built, Rhyna's family had laid the very gate they were sitting under, and Halvard's family had cut its stone out of the mountain. The two of them had been one pair since the cradle.

Then the Captain came down from the wall. He leads us all now. I had watched his cloak go up, and I didn't know yet what it meant for us. He wasn't tall. His fingers were still black from the coal, and half of a cloak hung on his back. He looked at the elders, and past them at the broken gate, and he asked one question. It's the only speech of his that anyone here can repeat word for word:

"Your walls are falling. Will you build, or will you watch and die?"

No one answered him. I remember the wind in the grass.

Then {{Halyna}} stood up.

They looked up at the torn cloak on the wall and bowed to it, both in the same breath, and raised their chisels as one.

The last of the light caught on their two chisels.

She began the cry, and he finished it:

<!-- HALYNA -->

"The mortar cries out from the ground. Rise, Stonewrights! Lay stone upon stone. Stand every wall to the very end."

"Rise! Rise! Rise!"

<!-- HALYNA END -->

It was one cry, from two throats. Hers came first for no reason except that a cry has to begin somewhere. Some people tell it as if she called and he answered, with a silence in between.

There was no silence.

Then the guild stood up, all of us at once. It was like an arch when the builders knock away its wooden frame, and every stone holds up the one beside it. I stood with them, though I don't remember standing. We took up the cry. The soldiers on the wall took it from us, and the column, all the townsfolk who had walked up the mountain with us, took it from the soldiers.

Old stone doesn't shake. I felt it shake under my feet.

That night this hearth, the great fireplace you're sitting at now, had its first fire in more years than anyone could count. {{Idrenna}}, the oldest pair in our guild, sat by it and made the terms with the Captain, and I carried in the water. They spoke slowly, the way old people do, and the Captain didn't hurry them.

The Stonewrights wouldn't carry weapons, and he didn't ask them to. But they would build between the volleys of the guns, and mend a breach, a hole broken in the wall, while the fighting was still going on. The terms still stand, in the guild's own words: *with the speed and precision that only Aetherbonded pairs could achieve*. *Aetherbonded* is the guild's old word for its bonded pairs. Then {{Idrenna}} put the whole bargain to the Captain in two halves, each of them saying one:

"Not the sword, but the stone."

"Not the killing, but the keeping."

The Captain bowed his head. Those two lines are the guild's word now.

Before that fire burned down, {{Orvenna}}, the pair who are our wall-masters, split the siege day into three parts, the laying, the hauling and the holding, the way a quarryman splits a block of stone along its own seam. Then they told the Captain:

"Every gun you haul is a row of stone we don't lay,"

"so spend a gun the way a careful man spends silver."

They meant that every hour spent moving guns was an hour taken from building.

*Then the masons sang, as they still sing it:*

> *Lay the stone while the sea is empty.*  
> *Haul the guns while the guns are cold.*  
> *Hold the wall when the grey sails come,*  
> *and the wall will hold.*

And wherever you see a Stonewright on the wall in the holding, while the wall is under attack, look again. You'll see two.

Late that night, by torches that hissed as the frost fell on them, we laid the war's first row of stone across the gap in the inner gate, with each stone set across the joint below it, so that no crack could run straight through. {{Orvenna}} asked for the clearest voices to lead the havens' old gate-song, *Two at the Gate*, which is sung in two voices. Enno and Della stood up and took the lead between them. In every line, one of them sang up to the comma, and the other sang the rest.

*Then they sang, and the guild sang after them:*

> *Stone on stone, and the stone stays true.*  
> *Hand over hand, and the hand holds on.*  
> *Two at the gate, when the grey sails gather.*  
> *Two at the gate, and the gate stands strong.*

I sang up to the comma, and then, with no one beside me, I sang the rest.

When the torches had burned down to stubs, Enno and Della fell asleep against the new row of stone, still holding hands, the way they'd come up the mountain. I lay down a little way off, by the water buckets, and slept.

By morning the mortar had set.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

### I.3 · The Captain's Long Breath

*Told in the second month of the first winter of the siege. It tells of the enemy's first attack on our wall.*

*Hale the runner laid this tale, holding the old gate-stone that every teller at our hearth holds. He was seventeen and in his first weeks on the wall. Our runners are the young men who carry to the Captain, who leads us, whatever they see on the wall. Hale told it standing up, as fast as he runs. I, Seren, who write this Book, have set it down slower.*

Give me the stone, then. I'm not a storyteller. I run.

Every haven, as we called our ten cities on the coast, had a runners' stone of its own, where the runners swore their oath. We made ours in our first week here, out of a fallen block by the inner gate, and on it we swore the old oath:

"What I saw, I carry. I carry nothing else."

Wick swore it last. He's the youngest of us and the fastest, and there's nothing he'd rather do than carry a word to the Captain himself.

At first grey light on the morning of the first attack, I was standing by Kael's guns. Kael commands them, and they're still the only guns we have. He's Kael the Counter, who counts everything twice, and he's one of the True Men, the Captain's own company of twelve. The Captain gave Kael his orders: where to watch, what shot to fire, and when to hold fire. Then he went on to the far end of the wall. Kael put his hand on my shoulder.

"Nothing changes those orders but his own voice, here at my side, lad, and a man can only stand in one place."

*Then the troops drilled the three calls, the orders the Captain gives in his own voice, as they still drill them:*

> *Shift your ground, and watch new water.*  
> *Change your shot for the sea, the shore, the sky.*  
> *Hold your fire, or loose it, when the Captain is by.*

When the drill was done, Jory of Eldhythe, a soldier from our oldest harbour city, leaned on the parapet of the east wall and whistled a tune the old ship-masters hate, the tune of an old harbour rhyme. The ship-masters are the men who used to captain our boats. Merrick, the eldest of them, was coiling rope at the foot of the stair, and he told Jory to stop. Jory laughed down at him.

"It's only a rhyme."

Merrick didn't laugh.

Before the morning was half gone, the enemy's sails came out of the grey, the great fog bank out at sea, faster than I could count them. That was my first holding, which is what we call the hours when the enemy attacks and the wall has to be held. In the laying, when our builders are at work, the hours go slowly, because stone is heavy. In the hauling, when the guns are moved, they go slower still. In the holding, an hour is gone in a heartbeat.

Late in the holding, when the fight with the other ships was over, the masons, the Stonewrights who build our wall, came out to mend the breach where the wall had been broken. Then Kael's lookout saw an enemy hull through a far-glass, one of the spyglasses Pellow of the True Men made for us. It had hung back in the grey, and no ship had ever done that before. Now it came in, late and alone.

The smoke of the guns lay between the Captain and that hull, so he couldn't see it. Kael gave me three words.

"Hull hangs back."

I ran it.

I run. Under the smoke, head down, past the masons, past the ropes. I don't speak to anyone, and I don't stop. Up into the clear air above the gate.

The Captain is standing still.

That's what I remember first. Everything on that wall was moving except him. He had one hand on the parapet and his face to the sea. He didn't turn round to look at me. But he heard me, to the last word.

Then he walked toward Kael's guns at his own pace, with me at his shoulder. And as he walked, the hour began to stretch.

It didn't stop dead. It slowed, like a stone-cart on a long hill, until a boy walking behind it could count the spokes in its wheels. I haven't got a better way to say it.

I saw the water pour off the late hull's bow in sheets. I counted the ripples running down its grey sail, the way you count the rings on a cut log: one, and then the next. Each one, I think, was a minute. The gulls hung in the air. The smoke stood still. And on he walked.

I had time to be afraid. Then I had time to be ashamed of it. Then I had time to stop being afraid.

He stopped at Kael's shoulder. Kael was counting under his breath. Then the Captain gave the call, in his own voice.

"Hold."

The guns held their fire. Below us the masons kept on laying stone, two by two, and never looked up. The late hull came on through the smoke, closer and closer, until I could see the wet on its timbers.

"Loose."

The hour let us go, slowly, like a held breath going out. The guns fired. Out in the smoke the hull broke apart in the one stretch of that water where it couldn't hurt anyone, not even the masons out on the breach. It broke right where the Captain had meant it to.

I don't know what the Captain thought. I know what he did.

That evening I walked the tide-line, the pebble beach under the wall where the sea washes in the wreckage, as we runners do after every holding. In the shallows I saw marks cut into a plank, the first carved plank the sea had ever brought us. I carried word of them up past the soldiers' fires to {{Halyna}}, Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders and go by one name:

"Marks on the wood."

I ran it.

That night I found my way back by Hesk's lamp on the north wall. Hesk is one of the True Men, and the lamp was his mother's. By its light I knew the north wall was awake.

Now the men say it all along the wall: "For the Captain, an hour lasts as long as a day." I think it started with me, though Voss, a soldier from Eldhythe, will tell you it's older. They say it as if the Rite of the Cornerstone had given it to him. That's the ceremony the Keep's first builders left behind them, and soon after we came here it made the Captain our Commander, though we still call him the Captain. I used to believe that about the Rite myself, and I know better now. Kael set it right, and I carry it the way he said it, and nothing else:

"It's not the world that slows, lad. It's the Captain who won't hurry. The moment he chose was the moment that came."

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

### I.4 · The Rite of the Cornerstone

*Told in the first spring of the siege, our long stand at the Keep, the old fortress on the mountain ridge. It tells of the Fourteen Nights, as we call our first two weeks there, in the deep of that first winter.*

*Laid by me, Seren Two-Inks. Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders, go by one name between them, {{Halyna}}, and they took me into their household as its third. In the Fourteen Nights I was twelve, a child of the Stonewrights, our guild of master builders, and nobody trusted me with anything but water.*

Hold the stone, Seren. I tell myself that every time.

A moving light woke me. Rhyna was up, and in the same breath Halvard picked up the lamp. They're a bonded pair, two of our builders joined as one from birth, and they move together like that. I didn't call out. I watched the light go down and away from me, turn by turn, until it was only a spark at the bottom of a well.

That was the third night after the cry. On the day of the banner, our first evening at the Keep, our whole guild had sat silent in the grass of the old courtyard until {{Halyna}} raised us to our feet with one great cry.

Halvard and Rhyna come of two different families, but they've been a pair ever since they shared one cradle as babies. Over it, Rhyna's mother used to sing an old rhyme handed down in her family. Rhyna is descended from {{Aldwena}}, the bonded pair who laid the capstone of our inner gate, the great stone across the top of the gateway. They laid it long ago, in the First Days, when the first builders raised this Keep. Nobody had ever asked what the rhyme meant.

> *Under the hall and under the floor,*  
> *under the stair that goes no more,*  
> *down where the rope let the first stone go,*  
> *the first two keep what the first two know.*

Now that we were at the Keep, the rhyme told Rhyna where to look. There's an old stair here that winds down into the rock and stopped leading anywhere long ago: the stair that goes no more. Below it, a shaft drops on into the dark. Halvard went down the shaft on a rope. Rhyna paid the rope out to him, and neither of them said a word.

As they told it to me, a chest of black stone stood at the bottom, with dust a finger deep on its lid. Halvard swept the lid clean with his sleeve, and there was a mark cut into it.

It was the mark of {{Aldwena}}.

That was their own mark. The same mark is cut on the underside of the tale-stone, the stone my hands are on now. It's the old capstone {{Aldwena}} laid, and it takes two people to turn it over. We found it lying face down in the gateway when we came.

Inside the chest lay the Rite, a ceremony of the first builders, written on leaves of slate. There were slates about building for battle, too, and the founders' annal, the first builders' record of their years. A staff lay along the bottom of the chest, and three stone flasks stood in a corner. Halvard shook each one by the lamp.

Nothing moved in them.

At the fire, {{Halyna}} read the slates for their meaning, because the two of them know the old hand, the first builders' writing. One of them read a line, then came a breath, then the other read the next. I put my water bucket down and forgot all about it.

On the slates underneath, the pauses between their lines grew long. There were walls sloped so a shot would glance off, and gates set crooked so that a battering ram couldn't strike them square. There were courses, the rows of stone in a wall, laid so that if the wall was ever broken through, the breach would fall outward. The first builders had known war. The slates don't say what they fought against.

The Rite needed three oils, and no one alive had ever made them.

For the cedar oil, Stannard of the ridge led {{Halyna}} through the snow to the stand of cedars where the first staff had been cut. Stannard is one of the True Men, the Captain's own company of twelve, and a quarryman of this ridge. The three of them wouldn't cut down a living tree, so Stannard went along the fallen trunks, knocking on each one with his knuckle and listening.

"I know which of these will hold."

They boiled the heartwood down for its oil here at this hearth. On a wet night you can still smell the cedar breathing out of its stones.

The second oil was bloom, the masons' name for the pale crust that grows on worked stone when nobody has tended it for generations. They call it bloom because it flowers where no one comes.

So we children came out with old chisels and bent along the forgotten stones, scraping it off, like gleaners picking over a field after the harvest. It was the first work I'd ever been given that wasn't water.

My fingers were raw from it.

The third oil was myrrh, a sweet-smelling gum, and the mountain couldn't give us that. {{Idrenna}}, the oldest bonded pair in our guild, had carried one flask of it the whole length of the Road, our month-long march over the mountains to the Keep. They'd carried it for the guild's dead, but up on the pass the ground was frozen too hard to bury them. When Rhyna warmed the gum into the cedar oil, the whole hall went quiet, the way a street goes quiet when the dead are carried through it. It was the Captain who would be anointed with the myrrh of the Road's dead.

I knew who it had been carried for. One of those dead was a boy of the guild who had walked the Road beside me, knotting a net as he went. He never reached the Keep.

All night {{Idrenna}}'s empty flask stood on the hearthstone, and none of us would touch it. Late that night the Captain came in off the wall with mortar up to his wrists. He looked at the flask, and went out again.

On the fourteenth day a thin sun came out, the first since the evening the banner went up. In the ward, the courtyard inside our walls, lay the new capstone for the inner gate. Stannard had quarried it, and {{Orvenna}}, our wall-masters, had dressed it smooth. Beside it sat {{Idrenna}}, with the flasks in their laps. The Captain stood where the slate said the keeper of the work must stand, with his hand on the stone and his face to the people.

He didn't like it. He stood there.

Above the gate the torn cloak lifted once in the wind, and fell. I could hear snow-water running in the old gutters.

Then {{Idrenna}} stood up, the one leaning on the other, and with their four old hands they tipped each flask over his bowed head. That winter their hands shook at everything else. On the flasks they didn't shake.

One of them named each oil, and the other gave its meaning:

"Cedar, for endurance. Granite-bloom, for strength. Myrrh, for the burden of command."

The oil ran down into his collar, and he didn't wipe it away. We children at the front held up our raw hands to one another and smiled, for the first time since the mountain passes.

Then {{Idrenna}} held out the staff of the guild. The slate names it this way: *a rod of polished wood capped with the finest polished ashlar, borne by every overseer of a great Stonewright work since the founding*. Ashlar is smooth-dressed stone. Every great work has had its own staff ever since, and this one was the first of them all. The Captain looked at it for a long while before he put out his hands.

Their four hands laid it in his two.

With that staff he was named Commander of Rivenkeep, as this Keep is called. Until that day his only rank had been captain of one small company. He stood still in all the noise.

That night I held the lamp while {{Halyna}} cut their mark and their vow into the underside of the new stone. One of them held the chisel, and the other struck. Their mark isn't mine to draw. Beside the vow they cut words that aren't a vow: the Title, the five things the Captain had written in coal on his torn cloak, copied stroke for stroke. He'd written them in the course-hand, the square letters of our own tongue that masons cut in stone.

It was the Captain's own handwriting, large and slow.

Neither of them had tidied a single stroke.

The True Men raised the stone into the gate with its mark facing down, hidden, just as {{Aldwena}}'s had been hidden under the old one. The Captain leaned the staff in the corner of the gatehouse, because his work was the wall and the war. Then he went back out to the wall, with the unwritten half of his cloak on his back.

No one has moved the staff since. If you doubt the tale, go and look at the dust on it.

When the stone was set and the tools were cleaned, {{Halyna}} sent me down alone with the lamp to put the slates back in the chest. I went down, turn by turn, the way I had watched their light go. I lifted the empty flasks to set them back in their corner.

There was one more leaf of slate.

Its letters were large and careful, the way a child's are, and the way mine still are. I carried it up inside my shirt, cold against my skin. At the fire {{Halyna}} read it to me. It was the leaf of the youngest apprentice of the First Days.

So the custom of this hearth is older than the havens, our ten cities on the coast. We didn't make it up. We forgot it, and found it again at the bottom of a chest.

The next night Kael the Counter, one of the True Men, laid the first tale. He wouldn't sit to tell it. He'd had enough of sitting that winter, he said. It was about the Captain.

The people called him Commander. In his own heart he was still only the Captain.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

### I.5 · The Naming of the Rivenmen

*Told at the end of the sixth winter of the siege, on the night before we marched down from the Keep to take back our cities on the coast.*

*Voss who asked, as we call him, laid this tale, holding the old gate-stone that every teller at our hearth holds. He grew up in Eldhythe, the oldest of our harbour cities, in his grandmother's harbour-house, a tavern on the quay. He's one of the True Men now, the Captain's own company, raised in the place of one who fell, and he commands the harbour battery, a group of guns on our wall.*

Give me the stone. I've owed this hearth an answer for a long time.

Near the end of the first winter I buried a friend on the Keep's east face. The ground there is too hard for a spade, so we bury our dead under cairns, heaps of piled stones. When we were boys, he and I used to turn the skipping rope together on the quay at Eldhythe while the little ones jumped. I laid the last stone on him myself.

His name was Jory.

That night, here at this fire, I asked a question. I was cold, and I meant it as a hard one.

"Why do we fight for ice and stone?"

Stannard of the ridge, a True Man and a quarryman of this very mountain, knocked once on the hearthstone with his knuckle, the way he tests any stone before he trusts it, and answered me.

"It's not the ice I fight for."

That was a good answer, but it was his. Mine has taken me the whole war to cut into shape.

A slow answer is like a slow tide, so my grandmother told me. It comes in over the same stones.

Mine began the week after the Rite of the Cornerstone, the ceremony that made the Captain our Commander. That day the whole Keep gathered in the ward, the courtyard inside the walls, under the torn cloak: the half the Captain wrote on and raised as our banner on the evening we first reached the Keep.

I stood in the slush among the soldiers of our companies, with Jory at my shoulder. For once in his life he wasn't whistling. The Captain stood there among us, and the staff of command the Rite had given him stayed in its corner of the gatehouse.

Kael the Counter, the True Man who counts everything, climbed up on the fallen stones by the gate. He had one thing to say, and he said it once.

"The Commander fought."

And the whole guild finished it: all the Stonewrights, our master builders who never take up a weapon. They spoke in one voice, from {{Idrenna}}, the oldest pair among them, down to the smallest child who carried the guild's water:

"The Stonewrights built."

That was all the speech there was, and it was enough. We looked at one another, soldier and mason. We weren't two peoples sharing a ruin. We were one, the way two courses of stone become one wall once the mortar sets.

That day we laid the Keep's name, Rivenkeep, on the whole land. The grey sails, the enemy's fleets that came out of the fog at sea, had burned our havens, our ten cities along the coast. But every haven the grey sails hold is Rivenkeep, and the edge of the sea is the edge of the Keep. That's a vow.

We named ourselves the Rivenmen, after the Keep. Before that day we were Shorelanders, the people of the Shorelands, our long coast where the mountains run down to the sea. We still call our fathers Shorelanders, because that's what they were. But when we speak of ourselves, we just say *we*. It began in that courtyard.

All that day we went up one by one to the stone at the foot of the pike, the long spear the banner flies from. Each of us laid a hand on it and swore, the way men make their ropes fast on the quay at evening. Jory went up just before me, and came back down whistling. By dark the Keep was held fast.

We swore to take back every stone. Not one stone that wasn't ours. But every one that was.

The oldest among us never liked the new name, though they never said so. They still murmur "the Shorelands" into their shawls.

This winter ten great enemy ships came out of the fog and settled down in our havens like herons standing in the shallows. The men on the wall call them the Mystarchs, and the soldiers call them the Thrones. After they came, I asked old Ebba of Fenholm, the marsh town, why she kept the old name. She didn't look up from the fire. She just held her hands out to it.

"It's a name to come home to, when the last wall stands and the last Mystarch lies broken."

Then she said one word more, to the fire and not to me.

"Perhaps."

I didn't understand it then. I think I do now.

Four nights ago she sat up late on the end of that bench, nearest the coals, with her shawl pulled up to her chin. We went off to our blankets and left her sleeping. In the night the fire burned down, and nobody built it up again.

In the morning she didn't wake.

At dusk we laid her on the east face, beside the soldiers buried there. {{Idrenna}} said the prayer for the dead in two halves, one of them beginning it and the other finishing it. Marl, her grandson, one of the True Men, laid the last stone on her, and over it he said the old name, "the Shorelands." None of us corrected him. The Captain tied a strip to a pike above her. He cut it from the half-cloak he still wears, the half with nothing written on it.

It was Ebba who gave this hearth its answer, the two words we all say when a tale is done. On the very first night a tale was told here, nobody knew what to say. She said *We remember* into the quiet, and the hearth took the words from her. Most nights she began them, a breath before the rest of us. For these four nights, since she died, there's been a gap at the front of our answer, the width of one old voice. None of us has tried to fill it.

Now here's my answer, and you can weigh it. I asked my question for Jory's sake, and he never heard it asked. We'd laid him under his cairn that same day.

On the east wall I once said:

"We do not fight because we are right. We fight because our children are behind these walls."

It's true, but it isn't the whole of it. I have no child of my own, and still I said *our*.

From the east tower you can see the keeps we've raised along the coast in this war, small forts standing like posts along a quay. A crimson pennant flies on every one, the colour of the Captain's cloak. But a keep isn't a place to stay in forever. It's a place to go out from.

We don't fight for ice and stone. Every stone of our land is Rivenkeep, and Rivenkeep is home. Under the banner we swore to bring every stone of it home.

Old Ebba stays behind on the east face. But in his pack Marl carries the stone she brought out of Fenholm, a stone from her own house there.

Tomorrow we go down to the havens.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*The hearth waited a breath for Ebba to begin.*

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

## BOOK TWO · THE BOOK OF THE BUILDERS

*Who we were, and who they were.*

### II.1 · Of the Keep That Was Forgotten

*Told in the first summer of the siege, while the enemy's grey ships came at our wall from the sea. It tells how the Keep was built, long ago, and how it was forgotten.*

*Laid by me, Seren Two-Inks. One page inside this tale belongs to the founders, the first builders of the Keep, and not to me. {{Halyna}}, Halvard and Rhyna, the husband and wife who lead our builders, read it to me from the founders' old writing, and I haven't dared to change a word of it.*

Hold the stone, Seren.

This is the history of the Keep, the old fortress on this mountain ridge. It tells how the founders kept it, and how we, their children, didn't.

Its first page comes from a slate that {{Aldwena}}, a bonded pair of the founders, cut in the First Days, long before our cities stood on the coast. Their letters are no bigger than grains of barley.

> We two, who laid the gate, wrote this down.
>
> We came up onto the ridge and found it split from top to bottom, as if a great blade had come down on it. In the split we built a fortress out of the ridge's own stone. The wardens of the quarry cut that stone. The first of them cut his name into the stone over the western stair, and left the stone below it blank, for his sons' names.
>
> With our four hands we two laid the capstone of the inner gate. Under it we cut our mark, and our vow beside it, where no eye will see it again until the wall falls.
>
> This wall will stand. We two laid it, and we two answer for it, as long as stone is stone. It stands.

That mark and that vow are under this very stone, the one my hands are resting on. I can't turn it over, because that takes two people. But late at night, when no one is at the fire, I've slipped my hand underneath it and traced their mark with one finger.

The masters of our guild of builders call a mark like that a Stonwryt: *a vow sealed in living rock, earned by toil and skill*. It's a bonded pair's own promise, cut into stone, and no hands but theirs may cut it. No gold can buy one. But a mark could make gold trusted. The master pairs of the havens, our ten cities along the coast, stamped their marks on coins, and people trusted the coins as they trusted the marks: *trust, sealed in stone*. I have no mark. A mark belongs to a pair, and I'm not one of a pair. I'll never cut one.

Over the western stair, under the first warden's name, his sons cut theirs, each one beneath his father's, and below the last name the stone is smooth. Halvard Stone-Warden comes from that line, and Rhyna of the Inner Gate comes from {{Aldwena}}'s. So the two families were already joined in this very gate: Rhyna's forebears laid it, and Halvard's cut its stone out of the mountain. That was long before those two lay in one cradle as a bonded pair.

Then the people went down to the warm harbours on the coast, and the guild went after them. Before they left, the founders laid the Keep's slates, the writings they had cut, in a chest of black stone, the way masons put away their tools at dusk. With the slates they laid the Keep's first staff, the rod of the master who oversaw the building. We found that chest under this hall in our first nights here. On the coast they built Eldhythe, which people call the Eldest. But it's only the eldest of the havens. The Keep is older.

No slate gives the names of the last ones to leave. Behind them, the wind blew in under a gate that nobody kept watch over any more.

The Keep had never fallen to an enemy. It had only been left behind.

People forgot it, until only the old songs remembered it. No pair ever came back up to *point* its mortar, to renew it where it wears away between the stones. I love that word. So the rain found every joint. After the rain came the frost, and after the frost, the ice.

In some winter that no one wrote down, the capstone of the inner gate, the great stone laid across the top of it, came down off its two posts.

No one heard it fall.

But the whole coast heard the havens fall. That was the Fall: twenty months in which grey ships came out of the fog at sea and burned our ten cities one after another. After Glasspire, the last of them, we climbed up here to the one place the songs said would hold, and found only a ruin, the shadow of a fortress.

Forgetting had done to it what no enemy ever could.

It was dusk on the day of the banner. That's what we call the evening we reached the Keep, deep in the winter, when our Captain tore his cloak in two to make our banner. Halvard and Rhyna came in under the inner gate, with all of us in a long column at their backs. I came close behind them with the water-skin. I was twelve, and I didn't know their names.

The capstone lay in the gateway. Halvard swept the snow off it with his sleeve. On its top face the bloom had grown, as pale as frost on a gatepost. Bloom is the crust that creeps over worked stone where no one comes.

They knelt down in the snow and put their four hands under it. They turned it over the way the fishermen of Tidesmeet, one of our havens, turn over a boat they gave up for lost long ago.

It was their own.

On its underside were the mark and the vow of {{Aldwena}}, as sharp as on the day they were cut. Next to them were the bites of the wardens' chisels, left when they cut the stone out of the mountain. Rhyna laid her fingers on the mark of her forebears. Halvard set his thumb in a chisel-bite his fathers had left.

The stone was whole. Out in the ward, the courtyard inside the walls, people were still coming in. Their feet made no sound on the snow.

The stone had kept its vow. We were the ones who didn't keep ours.

Before the last light had gone from the snow, Halvard and Rhyna cried out to the guild to rise and build, and it rose. Until then our builders had sat in the long grass poking up through the snow, chisels in their laps, saying nothing.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

The breath was the air we breathed.

Our land lay flat under the trees, farther than anyone could measure, out to the far edge of the world. The trees breathed in their long sleep, and their breath lay grey among the trunks. It fed the trees and it fed us, and we tended it the way you tend the fire on your hearth.

When evening came and the light sank low among the trunks, we sang to the pale saplings, the young trees. They sang back to us, one grove and then the next, until the whole deep wood was one choir. Our little ones lay down among the roots to listen. One by one their small voices dropped out of the song, and they fell asleep in the singing.

At the very edge, where the breath came down to your sea, our pillars stood in long ranks. They were tall trees, and between them they held the breath in place, the way a mast holds a sail. We wove the breath thicker.

He saw nothing but grey. His face went white, and the oars took him back the way he had come.

We did not know what had frightened him.

Here is our fault, and the grain will not leave it out. We never came down to your shore. We hid, and we were glad to hide. We hid for so long, and so well, that no one ever saw us, not once.

Ever since your first haven, we had listened to you, and you had never once heard us. We had made sure of it.

This is our home: the flat wood under the breath, the between-light, the pale saplings singing in the evening, the silver elders at council. And at the edge of the world, the pillars, holding the breath between them.

If any green is left of us, it remembers this, and the grain still holds it.

## BOOK THREE · THE BOOK OF THE HAVENS

*Ten havens, the cities of our coast, and ten men to tell them: what we had, and how we walked away from it.*

### III.1 · Of Their Raising

*Told in the first autumn of the siege. It tells how the havens, our ten cities on the coast, were built long ago, and what each one was like before it fell.*

*The True Men, the Captain's own company of twelve, laid this tale. Kael the Counter held the old gate-stone that every teller at our hearth holds, and called them, and each man stood to answer for his own haven, as all twelve once stood under the banner. I, Seren, who write this Book, read out from the havens' old records what no one alive remembered. {{Halyna}}, the pair who lead our builders, had found those records in the chest I carried up the mountain.*

Give me the stone. No, I'll stand. At a roll-call, men stand.

At first light I went up the east tower. From up there you can still see the coast where our cities stood, stretching away into the haze. Not one hearth on it is sending up smoke.

Listen now to the names of the havens, and hold on to them, because they're ours.

I won't tell another man's city for him. I'll call each haven, and its own man will answer, and swear what he'll do when we win his city back and it comes home. I'm the oath-keeper. The True Men swear their oaths to me, and I hold them.

*First, as he asked me to, I read from the rolls, the old written records of our builders' guild, which tell how each haven was founded. Our forefathers were builders, not sailors, and they were afraid of the open water. They built where the mountains run down to the sea in long slopes, and they paved roads across the mountains like veins. Every haven's roll begins with the day its first row of stones went down and a polished rod was cut for whoever would oversee the work. It's always the same line:* On this day the first course was laid, and a staff was cut for the work. *The clerks' handwriting is small and patient, as if they hated to waste a page. Every wall of all ten havens was the work of the Stonewrights, our guild of master builders. Ten havens, and ten staffs.*

**Eldhythe, the Eldest Haven.**

Corlen of the harbour. For fifty years I watched the evening tide bring the boats home past my quay, slowly, to the warmest harbour-houses on the coast, the inns by the water where the ship-masters sat by the fire. When the first boat of ours comes home, I'll be the one to steer it in.

*Then Corlen, the eldest of the Twelve, sat down. He wet a finger and held it up to feel the wind, the way he does on the wall whenever the enemy's ships attack. There's no wind in this hall. The young men at the back laughed. He told them what he tells the men at his guns when they're too eager to fire: "Wait. The sea hasn't finished."*

**Tidesmeet, where the river meets the tide.**

Tamm of the three spans. Tidesmeet stood on both banks of the river, where it meets the tide coming in from the sea, and three great spans, three stone bridges, held it together across the water.

*Tamm set three stones out by the fire in their order, the way he sets them out by his blanket every night.*

One span is just a plank. Three make a city. I'll set these out again on the bank where the spans stood.

**Fenholm, the town that was always sinking.**

Marl the fen-born. Fenholm stood on wooden posts in a fen, a wide marsh, and it sank a hand's breadth every lifetime. So every generation laid a new course of stone, a new row, on top of the old one. A fen-man who can't laugh at that had better go and live on a hill.

Old Ebba, my grandmother, laid the top course of our house. I've got one of its stones in my pack, and I'd carry her too, if she'd let me. That's her there by the fire, nodding. When Fenholm comes home, she wants me to lay her stone on the old course.

**Holtward, the keep under the canopy.**

Aske of the canopy. Holtward was a fortress in the forest, hidden so deep among the trees, under the roof of their leaves, that a traveller could pass right beneath its walls and never see one. We wouldn't cut a single bough. Every autumn we swept the leaves off the wall-walk, the path along the top of the wall, by hand.

*By the hearth lay the night's firewood: planks from the enemy's wrecked ships, which the waves wash in on the pebble beach below our wall. Wood is scarce up here on the ridge, so we burn them. Aske laid his hand on every plank before it went on the fire.*

These were trees once. While I live, no axe will come into Holtward.

**Carnhold, the stronghold of the cairns.**

Garvel of the smithies. We cut Carnhold out of the rock among the cairns of the first quarrymen, the mounds of stones piled over their graves. It was a town of forges, and you could tell which forge was at work by the ring of its hammer. I listen to every gun on this wall the way a mother listens to her child's cough. A gun that rings true shoots true. But at an anvil in Carnhold, I'll make something that isn't a gun, and hear it ring.

**Emberhythe, the forge on the burning shore.**

Harl of the burning shore. Emberhythe was a forge-town too. It stood where a smoking mountain runs down into the sea, and the ground there was so warm that our children ran barefoot in the snow. I'll have my shore back. And I'll burn the grey, and the sea too, if it would catch fire.

**Sandreach, the citadel carved in the canyon wall.**

Ulden of the galleries. Sandreach had no harbour. We called it a haven all the same, because it sheltered us. It was dug, not built: gallery below gallery, long halls cut one under another into the red rock of a canyon wall. Every night until the havens fell, I slept with the whole mountain for my roof and my ear to the floor, and I heard it hold. I'll sleep under that roof again.

**Rimewatch, the watch on the frozen mere.**

Hesk of the mere. Rimewatch stood by a mere, a broad lake that froze hard every winter. Its great hall was built out on the lake itself, so all winter long it stood on the ice, and the town feasted under that roof and was warm. Every dusk a taper, a thin candle, went from window to window lighting the lamps, and the fishers out on the ice steered home by them. My mother kept those lamps. One of them is burning on our north wall tonight. I'll light it on the ice again.

**Highreach, the tower of the floating stones.**

Lanner of the crags. Highreach stood on crags, high cliffs of rock. They were so high that every course of its stone went up by crane, and the stones hung on the wind the way hawks hang over a field. I can smell a wind a day before it comes. A boy from those crags can whistle one up before he can walk. I'll climb the highest crag again, and whistle for the wind.

**And Glasspire, the last and proudest.**

Pellow of the spire. Seven generations built Glasspire, and it caught the sun like a blade of ice. At the very top stood the great glass, which threw the sunlight a day's sail out to sea, into the grey. My father ground it. What I carried of it up the mountain, packed in straw, I've ground into our far-glasses, the spyglasses we keep watch with on this wall. When Glasspire comes home, mine will be the first eye on it.

*Then Pellow, the youngest of the Twelve, held his glass up to the fire, the way he holds it up to everything, his supper included. To the children at the front he said, "Look close."*

Ten cities. Ten harbours, if you count Sandreach the way Ulden does. Ten peoples who called themselves one nation, the Shorelanders.

Twelve of us answered under the banner on our first evening here. Ten have sworn tonight. Stannard of the ridge swore nothing. He's a quarryman from this very ridge the Keep stands on, and the ridge was never a haven. He knocked once on the hearthstone with his knuckle. He won't trust a stone until he's knocked on it.

I've called ten havens tonight, and I hold ten oaths. I have no haven.

The Captain found me on a road.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

### III.2 · Of Their Fall

*Told in the second winter of the siege, in the year of the Second Tide, as the wall named that year's enemy fleets. It tells how the havens, our ten cities on the coast, fell in the twenty months before we reached the Keep, and of the Road, the long march that brought us up here.*

*The True Men, the Captain's own company of twelve, laid this tale too, each for the fall of his own city, in the order the cities fell. It was a night of hard frost. The guns were still cooling from the day's fighting, and the day's mortar hadn't set. Kael the Counter, the True Men's oath-keeper, held the old gate-stone that every teller at our hearth holds, and called the men. I, Seren, who write this Book, spoke for our builders' guild.*

Give me the stone. No, I'll stand. This isn't a tale to sit down for.

Last autumn you heard the names of our havens, and you held on to them. Hold on to them now while they fall, and don't look away, because we won't look away while we tell it.

In the last spring of peace, twenty months before we reached this Keep and raised our banner over it, the swallows nested in the arches of the havens' gates. No one in the havens ever broke a nest. The havens had no warships. Their walls were built for feast days and for weather. Their gates were wide, and they stood open at night.

Then, without any warning, the dark sails, the first of the enemy's ships, came out of the grey, the great bank of fog that lies out at sea. No messenger came, or none that anyone could understand. All that came was a knocking out of the fog, like thunder that went on and on. The soldiers on this wall call it the drums. We looked for a captain on those ships, and a crew, even one man at one rope. No one stood on the decks. I swear to that.

So we called it the ghost fleet, and whoever sent it we called the Mystaeri, after the mist. A man has to call his death by some name.

Those sails were simpler than the ones that come against us now. Each one came alone, and one was all it needed. No haven sent to another for help, because none had any help to send.

*Then each man laid his hand on the stone in his turn, Corlen of the harbour first. He's the eldest of the Twelve, and when the men at his guns are too eager to fire, he tells them, "Wait. The sea hasn't finished."*

**Eldhythe.**

On the first morning of that spring, the dark sails came in at the harbour mouth on the rising tide. From dawn to dusk the harbour burned: first the boats at their moorings, then the harbour-houses, the inns on the quay where the ship-masters of the whole coast used to sit by the fire.

*Then he waited, the way he waits at his guns, and the hall waited with him.*

In the evening the wind blew the smoke across the city. The smoke hid the harbour, so the city never saw it go. Eldhythe held out for three months. On its last evening the tide came in over the ashes, slowly, the way it always came, and the sea hadn't finished.

*Corlen took his hand off the stone so that Tamm could lay his there. It was the last time he held it.*

**Tidesmeet.**

It took three days. From the high road we watched its three spans, the great bridges that held the city together across the river, go down into the water, and we heard them go. The upper one went, and then the lower. The middle one stood longest. We waited for it, and it went. That night I climbed down in the dark and took a stone from each of them. I still have all three.

**Fenholm.**

It went under in one night, as if the fen, the marsh it stood in, had only been waiting. "A town is just the newest course on the old," we used to say, and laugh. That night nobody laughed. My grandmother, Old Ebba, carried her course-stone out on her own back. It was the stone from the top course of our house. She wouldn't give it to me until the fen had closed over the last course of the town.

**Holtward.**

It burned among its trees, and the leaves came down burning onto the wall-walk we used to sweep by hand. I laid my hand on the last tree still standing. Its bark was as warm as a living hand.

**Carnhold.**

It burned in its smithies. In the street of the smiths I heard the hammers stop, one forge and then the next. The last anvil rang on for a while, alone.

Then it did not ring.

**Emberhythe.**

Emberhythe held out for five months on its burning shore. By then five havens had fallen, and their survivors had gathered into one long column. The column spent that winter with us, sleeping warm on the bare ground, because the ground there was always warm. When the city fell, one of our masons carried a charred plank out in his pack. I told him to burn it.

**Sandreach.**

Sandreach was dug into a canyon wall in galleries, long halls cut one under another into the rock. It was taken from us one gallery at a time. Each night I lay with my ear to the floor, and I heard the gallery above me go before any runner came down to tell us. At the end we came out in the dark at the foot of the canyon. Every night since, I've slept on the open ground, with my ear to it.

**Rimewatch.**

It was taken when the first ice came. Our great hall stood out on the ice of the mere, our broad lake, and it burned there with its lamps still lit. The fire went from window to window, the way my mother's taper, a thin candle, used to go at dusk, lighting the lamps one by one for the fishers out on the ice. It burned the way it had always shone.

I went into the smoke, to her window, and took down her lamp. It was lit.

Then I turned for the shore. The ice was new, only a night old. All the way across the lake it spoke under me, cracking far off and then near. Once the wind blew the flame down to the wick, and I cupped my hand around it until it stood up again. Behind me the roof fell in. In front of me there was no light but the one in my hand.

The ice held.

I carried the lamp out lit. Every dusk since, I've lit it on the north wall, and the night-watch steer by it, as the fishers did.

Someone has to keep a light.

**Highreach.**

The wind comes before the sails. I smelled the grey on it a whole day ahead of them, and there was nothing to do with that day but leave. We came down the crag-stairs in the dark, the steps cut into the cliffs, with the children on our backs. Behind us the cranes stood burning on the crags.

**Glasspire.**

With the first snows, it went down in one night into its own cellars, the spire and the great glass at its top together. The dark hole beneath it is still open. Some who fled say a shadow stood out in the fog that night, bigger than any ship. I was on the last stair of the spire, with the glass coming down around me in pieces, and the snow coming down with it.

I looked close. I saw nothing.

*Then Pellow, who holds his far-glass up to everything, sat down with it in his lap, and didn't hold it up. He gave his place at the stone to me.*

On the last day of every haven, when there was nothing more a mason's hands could do, the Stonewrights, our guild of builders who never take up a weapon, went out by the landward gate. They carried their chisels in their packs, and nothing else. The masters, the guild's senior builders, went last. They had cleaned their trowels, as they did after every day's work, and wrapped them in sacking for the road.

There were ten staffs, and not one came up the mountain.

*Kael laid his hands on the stone again, and told of the Road.*

When Glasspire was gone, the column turned up the mountain. It had gathered haven by haven, as each one fell, until there was no haven left behind it. We were a month in the passes.

On the night of the pass, the coldest night of the Road, many of the children died. Some of the old died with them. They had given the children their cloaks.

In the morning the snow was still falling. We got up out of it, as many of us as could get up, and walked.

At the foot of the passes I counted us, and I counted us again at the top. I won't tell you the difference.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

Stop, because the sky is dying, and we are dying under it.*

That's all he came to say. We have carried it a long way, and the grain still holds it.

*And no one answered.*

### IV.5 · The Burning Boat

*Told in the second summer of the siege, in the year of the Second Tide, as the wall named that year's enemy fleets. It goes back to a harbour story from our grandparents' time, and it tells of Voss's friend Jory.*

*Voss who asked laid this tale, holding the old gate-stone that every teller at our hearth holds. He's a soldier from Eldhythe, the oldest of our ten cities on the coast, and we call him Voss who asked for a question he once put to this hearth: why do we fight for ice and stone? His grandmother Elspet told him this tale by her fire on the quay at Eldhythe. The old ship-masters would never tell it, and the children would never stop singing its rhyme. Everyone on shore laughed at it. The sea didn't.*

Give me the stone. This tale isn't mine. It's my grandmother's. She had it from her brother, and he had it from the sea.

It was in the first holding of this war, the first time the enemy's grey sails came against our wall and we had to hold it. I heard one of their ships burning on the shingle, the pebble beach below the wall. It screamed. All along the wall the old ship-masters, who had captained our cities' boats their whole lives, turned their faces away from the sea, and Merrick, the eldest of them, turned first. Over on the east wall, my friend Jory was whistling.

A rhyme started going round and round in me, like a skipping rope. I'd skipped to it when I was seven. At forty, I understood it.

When I was a boy at my grandmother's table, I asked her why the ship-masters wouldn't listen to that rhyme. She answered me with a tale. You can weigh it for yourselves.

When she was twelve, she kept the fire in her mother's harbour-house, an alehouse on the quay where the ship-masters drank and traded. They came in out of the rain with the weather still in their beards. Over the fire hung three flat grey stones, carved with letters as fine as the tracks of birds on wet sand. Nobody knew what they said. Only the children ever asked. The ship-masters knocked out their pipes on the lowest one.

Whenever some bold man came home from out by the grey, the great fog bank out at sea, with a tale to tell, my grandmother laughed and filled his cup. "The Mystlands make liars of honest men," she said. The Mystlands was the old harbour name for whatever lies out there inside the grey.

She said it about her own brother.

Daveth was an axeman of the felling. In those days our cities sent men by boat out to the edge of the grey, where tall black trees grew, and the axemen cut them down for timber. That summer Daveth was coming home in one of the last timber-boats, riding low in the water with its load of black wood, and a second boat was following in his wake.

Out of the grey, a boat came at them, burning. It came into the wind and against the current, which no boat can do. It came screaming, the way gulls come screaming at boys who rob their nests. It struck both boats low down, where the load rode heaviest, and the black wood in their holds caught fire from it. After that came the rain, falling on the heads of two crews in the water.

Three men came home.

Daveth came home clinging to a spar, a broken length of mast, with the two other men who lived, and the sea ran out of his coat onto the floor my grandmother had swept. That's how she told it to me. All that autumn the three of them told their tale, and none of them ever set foot in a boat again.

Soon the men of the outpost were swearing to things too, over their drinks. The outpost was our last post at the edge of the land, out toward the grey. They swore a boat had been pushed off burning, with a dead man in it, a stranger who'd come out of the grey. Far out, they swore, it turned. Most of all they swore to the scream, and to three words of our own language that the grey man had spoken. But which three words, no two of them agreed.

My grandmother counted the boats for me on her fingers, the way her brother had counted them for her. One finger, then another. Then she laid her hand flat on the table.

There were two boats.

Now, when the ship-masters speak of it at all, they say twenty.

Everyone on shore laughed at the three from the spar. By day my grandmother laughed along with everyone else, and filled the three men's cups under the lowest stone.

At night she didn't laugh.

Daveth took to drink over his tale, and the harbour drowned him a stone's throw from his mother's harbour-house.

So why wouldn't the ship-masters listen to it? I've seen one of them leave his cup and walk out into the rain rather than hear it. Since then I've heard three of them speak of it, on the Road, our long march over the mountain passes to this Keep. Each of them set his few words down carefully, the way you'd set down a full cup.

"A small boat burning, far out in the grey," said the first. The second said, "It turned into the wind, and after it came weather." The third said, "It's the boat that comes for you when you die." And the first one spoke again: "Let the shore laugh. The shore will never have to meet it."

The cities had it written down in their records: an armed stranger had come and been put down, and his boat had been burned and set adrift. And who's going to argue with writing?

But the children had a rhyme for it, and children will argue with anything. We skipped to it on the quay, two turning the rope and one jumping. Jory and I turned the rope, because we were the biggest and the slowest, and for the littlest ones Jory turned it slower still.

Merrick is sitting by the door tonight. He hasn't gone out.

All that first winter, Jory whistled it on the east wall, the tune and never the words. On the morning he died, I was at his side, and the tune went ahead of him along the wall-walk. He whistled it slowly, the way he used to turn the rope for the littlest of us. Then the rhyme pulled tight in me, like a mooring-rope when the tide comes in, and I heard the two lines we never sang.

I did not tell him to stop.

The tune stopped.

His is the first cairn on the east face, where the ground is too hard to dig a grave.

If my grandmother's house still stands in burnt Eldhythe, the three grey stones are hanging there over a cold hearth, with no roof over them but the rain.

We forget. We've always been good at that. But Mystwood, the great wood out there in the Mystlands, remembers.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

### IV.6 · The Voyage of the Aelvaren

*Halvard and Rhyna, the pair who lead our builders, came to know this from the wood in the third summer of the siege. It tells of the Guest's boat, long ago, in our grandparents' time.*

The men of that house dragged him down to us by the heels. They threw him into us like a torn net.

Our kin in their holds caught our fire, and both boats broke apart and burned.

Under the thinnest part of the breath we found the old channels, the hidden waters among the roots. No keel of yours had ever gone there, but ours knew its way. Slowly, as the breath closed over us, the fire went out. All that was left of us was our keel, our backbone, and a few ribs, and in them the bones of Aelrhen.

In the night we came up out of the channels onto the pale sand, small and black. We lay still under the silver elders, the trees where the long-lived hold their council. The council came down to us and knew us, because they were the ones who had sent us.

Many of them laid their hands on our keel at once, and they came to know all of it. They knew the gift, and the foot that kicked it. They knew the neck held gently, and the blade, and the blows.

Wood can hide nothing.

They had sent out their peace, and the best they had, and it was answered with murder and fire. They knew a murder when they saw one, and this was one. But they did not know why the host's arm drew back from the blade. And they did not ask.

They saw only an act of war.

They laid what was left of us, our keel with the bones of Aelrhen in its ribs, among the roots of the silver elders.

And the roots drank.

From that grove, all we knew went out to every grove, root to root, to the far edge of the world. Every young tree that was to become a ship drank it in with the rain. So every ship that came against your wall carried our grief in it, and the grain still holds it.

*And no one answered.*

## BOOK FIVE · THE BOOK OF THE TIDES

*The war: a doom set in motion by the dead, crewed by the living, against a people who had never known they were the foe.*

### V.1 · The First Glyph

*Told in the first winter of the siege, at the end of our very first month at the Keep. It tells of the night, a few days before, when we beat back the enemy's first attack on our wall.*

*Laid by me, Seren, who write this Book, holding the old gate-stone that every teller at our hearth holds. They call me Seren Two-Inks, for the two inks I write {{Halyna}}'s words in, one for each of the two people that name stands for. That night I held the lamp. There's still sand from that beach caught in the binding of this Book.*

Hold the stone, Seren.

Down on the shingle, the pebble beach under our wall, {{Halyna}} held the wood, and I held the lamp. {{Halyna}} are Halvard and Rhyna, who lead our guild of master builders here at the Keep. They're a bonded pair, joined as one from birth, and they work as one, like the two hands of a single mason. They grew up to be husband and wife, and the guild gave them one name, made from both of theirs. That night their four hands lay on the grain, and my one hand held the light. It began that way, and it has gone on that way ever since.

For nearly a month the grey sails, the enemy's fleet, had lain off this wall, and none of us knew what they were. We knew only that they'd come out of the great fog bank at sea, burned our ten cities on the coast one after another, and never shown a living soul on their decks. On the night we broke their first attack, the tide brought one of their wrecks in under the wall: grey planks, broken ribs, and a keel as long as a gate. It rolled over and over in the shallows, as if the sea were turning each piece to look at its underside.

The old ship-masters on the wall wouldn't go near it. But the soldiers went down and made fires of it all along the tide-line, because firewood was scarce up on our mountain ridge. "Burn it," snapped Harl of the burning shore. He's one of the True Men, the Captain's own company of twelve, and he comes from a forge-town on a volcanic coast. He broke one of the wreck's ribs across his knee and fed it into the flames. One by one the fires went up, the way lamps are lit along a quay at dusk. The Captain, who leads us all, let them burn most of it. He stood among the fires and didn't move.

While the first fires were catching, Hale the runner came up from the water's edge. He was seventeen that winter. He went past the fires, and past the Captain himself, straight to {{Halyna}}.

"Marks on the wood."

When the tide turned, {{Halyna}} went down, Halvard's hand under Rhyna's elbow on the wet stones. I came behind them with the lamp. {{Halyna}} had taken me into their household as their third, and the light was mine to carry. The men hauled one plank up out of the surf, and I set the lamp down beside it.

One shape ran along its grain, as fine and shallow as the first light line a mason scratches on a stone before he cuts it. One long edge of the plank was raw, split along the grain, as if the rest had been torn away.

Until then, the only things we had that might speak to us, if we could ever get at their words, were the mute stones and a sealed bundle, both in the archive chest, the guild's old chest of records from Eldhythe, our oldest city. The mute stones are carved with letters no one can read. The bundle was sealed with the mark of {{Wendhessa}}, the bonded pair who kept the guild's records there, and no bonded pair would break another pair's seal. Now we had this plank. In our grandfathers' day, the axemen who cut black timber at the edge of the fog used to swear over their drinks that the black wood would put a thought into a man's hand, if he rested it there long enough. Everyone on shore had laughed at them. This wood was grey, not black. But we had nothing else left to try.

So Rhyna sat down beside the plank and laid her palms flat on the grain. Halvard knelt behind her and laid his hands over hers. Neither of them spoke. The tide came and went. The lamp burned down a finger's width.

Then the knowing came up into Rhyna's palms. That's what we call it now, a knowing, because it isn't reading: what's carved in the wood comes up into the hands whole, and you know it the way you know a thing you remember. It burned, she said, but not like fire. It burned like cold iron in the depth of winter. And its shape was in the burning: one stroke, curling forward, like a wave about to break. She hadn't said a word, but Halvard lifted his head.

"Shore," he said.

Rhyna said he was right.

Halvard couldn't say how he knew it. It was as if someone had put the word into his mouth, he said, and then gone away. They knew more than that one word. But the rest was thought the way a forest thinks, and all they had to hold it with was the way we think, we people of the shore. They held on until the lamp was low, and one word was all they could tell us.

They got up off the stones, stiff, and others came down to try. First came Jory of Eldhythe, a soldier of the east wall, who's sitting tonight at the back of this hall, beside his friend Voss. He laid his hand on the plank, laughing. "Wet planks," he said, and he went off up the shingle whistling an old harbour tune that the ship-masters can't bear, the tune and never the words.

Then came Enno and Della, the youngest bonded pair in our guild, who were fifteen when they walked up the mountain to the Keep hand in hand. They laid their four hands on the plank together. He said the wood was cold, and she said it wasn't, and they were both right.

When they'd gone, I sat down where Rhyna had sat, and alone I laid my own palm on the grain. Something came up into it out of the wood, far too big to hold. It was a river in flood, and I was a girl with a cup.

I let go.

Halvard put the lamp back in my hand.

Before we went back up, I opened this Book on my knees to a clean page, though pages were precious then. In the middle of it I wrote one word, *shore*, and nothing else. Before the ink touched the page, I said the word over three times, as I do with every word, to hear whether it would hold. It held. It was short and square, and it sat in the mouth the way a well-cut stone sits in its bed of mortar.

On the facing page I drew the sign. In the sea air the ink wouldn't dry.

The word looked very small on its page.

We came back up with the fires burning low behind us. At the gate {{Halyna}} stopped, and spoke the way they do, one of them beginning and the other finishing.

### V.2 · The Night of the Naming

*Told late in the second summer of the siege, the year of the Second Tide, on the evening after the night it tells of.*

*I, Seren, who write this Book, held the lamp that night, and the next evening I laid this tale at the Long Hearth, holding the old gate-stone that every teller there holds. The planks I tell of are still in the lowest vault, and anyone who wants to can go down and lay a hand on them.*

Hold the stone, Seren.

All through that first winter, this hearth laughed at the grey sails, the enemy's ships out of the great fog bank at sea. "They've picked the wrong fortress," Kael the Counter used to say, and I laughed with the rest.

One night late in the Second Tide, {{Halyna}}, the pair who lead our builders, went down to the oldest room under this hall, the vault where we found the Rite, the first builders' ceremony that made the Captain our Commander. I came down behind them with the ink and the lamps. It's as cold down there in summer as in winter. The planks lay in rows, grey on grey, like a field the plough has turned up full of stones.

Fifty-three planks from the first winter lay there, pieces of the enemy's wrecked ships, and every one of them was carved. Our runners had carried them home from wherever the sea gave them up, two runners to a plank, with the salt water running down their arms. The rest of the wreckage went into the soldiers' fires, or back out with the tide. The wall had a grand name for the carved ones, the tablets, but they were only planks.

Only {{Halyna}} can know what the enemy carves in its wood. When they lay their hands on a carving, what's cut there comes into them whole, like a memory, and we call that a knowing. Every plank but one had given them a single word, and I'd written each word on a page of its own. That one plank had given them nothing, though it was carved as finely as any of the others. The sea brought it in a few weeks after the very first.

The planks of the Second Tide, this year's ships, carried two signs each, cut close together, like names crowded onto the stone over an old doorway. Down on the shingle, the pebble beach under our wall, {{Halyna}} had learned to hold both signs at once, though there were gaps in everything they could tell me, and I haven't filled those gaps with guesses. But until that night, nobody had gone back down to ask the fifty-three old planks for anything more.

Summer nights are short and the rows were long, so they started at the two ends, Rhyna by the stair and Halvard at the far wall. I'd never seen them work apart before. They're a bonded pair, joined as one from birth, and a bonded pair works as one. Even that night they weren't far apart: only the length of the vault.

Rhyna picked up the very first plank of all, the one carved with a wave, which gave us our first word from the wood: *shore*. One long edge of it was split, rough and raw. Halvard had the silent plank with him at the far wall, and he picked it up. One long edge of that one was raw too.

I stood between them with the lamp. No wind reaches that far down, and the flame stood straight up in my hand.

Rhyna lifted her plank, and at the far wall Halvard lifted his. It was like lifting a net: raise the near knots and the far ones rise with them, because it's all one net.

Their two faces changed at the same moment.

Then Rhyna spoke, and it wasn't a word. It was a whole order.

The first time was in the grey of morning. The other ships, our kin, were all around us, and the bearer, our lead ship, sailed at our head. A stone of your wall flashed. The first young ships that came against you, the green wood, had learned what a flash means, and the roots had carried it home. Where a stone flashes, the iron is there.

We turned toward that stone, and it spoke again.

Your iron shot went into us, low and right through. The sea came in.

Then an older carving woke in us, one of the first orders the green wood was ever given: *Home when wounded.* We turned our stern to your wall and went back toward home, into the breath, the grey that you call fog. We went low in the water, and slow. Your wall let us go, or didn't see us go. We don't know which.

As we went, we told our kin, and we needed no words to do it. First the flash went out of us into the roots, and through the roots to them. Then the place it had come from. Then the place where the iron had found us. Our kin felt it in their own planks.

Last of all went the dread. The dread is only this: one place in the water has become a place where wood dies. It spread slowly, the way sap rises, to every ship that had come with us. It reached the bearer's heart soonest, the core of its wood, and from that heart it went out to all of us.

In the night, the bearer's heart carved the next day's orders into us: *Look before you trust.* A stone that spoke yesterday may be silent today. A silent stone may be waiting.

The next time the fleet came, we went out ahead of our kin, alone and slow. We knew that place. We were sent to look, and for nothing else.

We threw one fire at the stone that had spoken, to see if it would answer. The grain holds nothing of what our fire found there. The stone didn't answer.

Behind us, our kin stood off in the breath and waited. The water lay flat, and we were slow.

Your wall still flashed.

It didn't flash from the stone that had spoken. It flashed from another stone along the wall, one that had been silent.

With the sea already in us, we couldn't turn away a second time. The iron found us where it had found us before, low and right through, as though it too kept count, the way we do.

Out in the breath, our kin felt it in their own planks.

### V.4 · The Council Under the Thin Sky

In the summers before Aelrhen came home, the sky was thin along the edge of the wood. The pillars at the edge, the tall black trees that held our sky in place, were gone in long gaps, like teeth gone from a jaw. Your iron axes had cut them down. Through the gaps the breath ran out, and the between-light, the soft light that came to us through the breath, went hard and white.

Each spring the newborns of the long-lived were frailer. Some didn't wake. The oldest faded before their years were full.

The old ones of the council told everyone to be patient, the way trees are patient. "The iron comes seldom now," they said. "The pillars haven't put up a single new shoot. They're waiting. Wait with them, and when the iron stops, they'll grow the sky back."

The old ones were dying of the very wound they asked everyone to be patient with. They knew it, and they still said to wait.

Then the Aelvaren, Aelrhen's small grey boat, came home through the hidden channels. It was burned down to the keel, and Aelrhen's bones lay inside its ribs.

The young of the long-lived had grown up under a sky that was leaving them. They had watched their mothers and fathers fade in the white light, and they couldn't help them. They came into the grove, where the keel lay among the roots, and over it they called for a vote.

The old ones stood up and pleaded. "Send another. Learn their words. They don't know us. They can't know the wood, and we have never gone down to teach them. Don't be hasty."

But by then the old voices were few, and faint. They were the voices of the dying.

The young answered, "We listened to them through three of their lifetimes and learned three of their words, and they wouldn't hear even those."

And the patience broke.

Their answer was true, but it wasn't the whole truth. We and our people listened for a long time from behind the breath, and we never once went down to your shore with our hands open. Except once. Aelrhen went down with those three words, and when he was killed, we didn't send a second.

We chose to grow ships, and not to learn words. We never asked whether a whole people had killed Aelrhen, or only one household at the very edge of your land.

But war is for the short-minded and the angry young.

So the vote was carried, by the weight of anger and grief.

*Grow until the shore is silent.*

And the wood, which can't unlearn what is carved into it, grew.

In those same summers, the iron stopped coming to the edge. The grove hadn't waited long enough to see it.

While even one of the old ones who had pleaded was still alive, the new-grown ships lay ready at the edge of your sea, and didn't go. When the last of those old ones died and went down into the moss, the ones who had cried for war cut the ships loose against your shore.

The words of the old ones didn't go down into the moss with them. The silver elders keep them, as they keep everything they hear, and so do we: "Learn their words."

The carving didn't go down either. The heart of every ship that has sailed since carries it. It lies under all their other carvings, like the first ring under all the rings, and the grain still holds it.

### V.5 · The Tides, as the Wall Counted Them

*Told at the end of each year of the siege, one count for each year's fleets, six in all.*

*Kael the Counter laid these, holding the old gate-stone that every teller at our hearth holds. He's one of the True Men, the Captain's own company of twelve, and he counts everything twice. The wall calls each year's enemy fleets a Tide, and Kael wouldn't tell of any Tide until it was over. In the sixth winter he asked me, Seren, who write this Book, to mend all six, the way I'd mended the first tale he ever laid here. I've changed these less than that one.*

Give me the stone. No, I'll stand.

**I · The Hasty Tide**

I count. That's what I'm for.

The first year's fleets were the Hasty Tide. On the first morning there were eight sails, and not one of them kept in line with another. Each one struck at the first wall it touched and kept striking until it sank.

No one stood on their decks. Off the transports, the ships that carried their fighters, came the root-men, warriors like tangled roots, pouring out like spilled kindling. The wall called them treelings, as if a name could make them small.

By noon their fire had slackened, as if they'd used it up, and we learned to say, "The fleet has spent its fire."

A breach opened on the south face and fell outward, away from the people behind it, just as the slates the Keep's first builders left us in a black chest said it should. Everyone behind that breach lived. I swear to that.

All that winter I said at this fire that the grey sails, the enemy's ships out of the fog, had picked the wrong fortress. Every night it went round the benches with the cup, and every man drank to it. We laughed. God forgive us, we laughed.

Jory of Eldhythe, who used to whistle on the east wall, laughed the loudest. His name is first under the east cairns, the piles of stones over our dead on the Keep's east face, where the ground is too hard for a spade.

A fire-ship took him, an enemy ship that came in burning. The Captain, who leads us, still wears the unwritten half of the cloak he tore for our banner on our first evening here. The night Jory died, he cut a strip a hand wide from that half and tied it on a pike over Jory's cairn.

It was the first strip.

***Added in Seren's hand:*** *It wasn't the last.*

**II · The Second Tide**

This is the second count, and a harder one.

The second year's fleets, the Second Tide, came in pairs. The front ship took our shot, and the one behind it came in to land.

Off the north point we hit a ship. A mile to the south, another ship's sail went from grey-green to white, the way a man's face goes white, and it turned.

It hadn't seen. It had felt.

Then came the crown-hulls, broad ships carrying winged seed, and the seed came flying in wherever our guns were quiet.

Every fleet follows one ship, its bearer. When we sank a bearer, another ship took up the standard, the way a soldier picks up a fallen flag, and the whole fleet took on the new bearer's temper. I still tell the young gunners: when you pick which ship to sink, you choose which heart you leave them.

Corlen of the harbour was the eldest of the Twelve. Before every holding, the hours when the grey sails attack and the wall must be held, he wet a finger and held it up to the wind. He'd say, "Wait. The sea hasn't finished."

In one seed-fall, a ship came in slowly on the water his guns covered. On his own judgement he kept his guns quiet, to let it come close. He watched the water, and nothing but the water.

The seed came over the wall where the guns were quiet.

His were the quiet ones.

He died at the parapet, still watching the water, and three of his gun crew died with him. That night the Captain cut the second strip.

Corlen did what the Captain himself would have done. That's the whole of a True Man. It's also what killed him.

Voss, a soldier from Eldhythe, took over Corlen's battery, the guns he'd commanded, and was raised a True Man in his place. At the next holding Voss wet a finger and held it up to the wind, as Corlen used to. The Captain, at his shoulder, looked up at the sky, not out to sea.

"Change your shot."

**III · The Tide of Remembering**

You all knew Wick. He was the youngest of our runners, the young men who carry word along the wall to the Captain. He ran everywhere, even to this hearth, and sat at the front where the children sit, with his knees up under his chin. He could run the whole wall-walk in the time it takes to say the Title twice, the words the Captain wrote on our banner. All he wanted was to run a word to the Captain himself.

One night the Captain had the south gun moved forty yards. In the grey of the morning, Wick stood at the old gun-place, watching the water. It was so early that Hesk's lamp was still burning up on the north wall. Hesk of the mere is a True Man from Rimewatch, the town by the mere, a great lake that freezes in winter. He lights that lamp every dusk for the night watch to steer by.

One ship came, alone and slow, feeling its way in, like a boat sounding the depth. It didn't come for the wall. It came for the place where the gun had been. It fired there, to see whether the gun would answer.

The shot took the parapet at the old gun-place, and Wick fell with it.

I didn't see it. Hale the runner did. Wick gave him three words.

"They are looking."

His word came to the Captain, as he'd wanted all through the war.

Hale ran it.

That night the Captain cut Wick's strip. So a boy died of a gun that wasn't there.

We'd thought they were like beasts, that only learn from the stick. But they look before they trust, as Wick saw. These were the third year's fleets, the Tide of Remembering. They knew our walls by heart, but they never once knew what our Captain would do. After that we moved our guns by night, and it was a quieter war, and I liked it less.

**IV · The Joined Tide**

The fourth year's fleets were the Joined Tide, and they moved as one. All the night before, the drums went in the grey from ship to ship, that knocking out of the fog like thunder that won't stop. At first light the sails came on three roads at once, three ways in across the water, from the north and the east and the south. Three roads, one hour.

Lanner of the crags, a True Man from our tower town of Highreach, whistled for the wind, the way men from the crags do. The Captain stood at his shoulder, and was still. Lanner stopped whistling, and waited for the Captain's word to fire.

"Loose."

An hour after dawn, Lanner's battery sank their bearer.

The fleet didn't falter. Their bearer's heart, the core of its wood, had already carved the hour for them to turn, and at that hour, with the bearer sunk, they turned in on the east road all the same, into empty water, one sail going pale and then the next, a breath apart. They were keeping an hour set by a mind that was already dead. Only the bearer's own ships, the ones sailing with it, went wild. That day they broke our wall in two places, in the east and in the north.

Before dusk I walked the wall to count our dead. I found Enno of the guild in the east breach, and a mile on, in the north, Della. They were the youngest bonded pair in our builders' guild, two people joined as one since the cradle.

**V · The Tide that Learned Deceit**

This is the fifth count, and I don't like it.

The fifth year's fleets learned to trick us, and we call them the Tide that Learned Deceit. They came loud and alone and straight, the way the young wood, their youngest ships, had come in the first winter, and we laughed the way we'd laughed back then. Every gun on the wall fired, and then the hunters came, ships that went after every gun that had fired. Each day they sent straw down a new road, decoys that weren't really ships at all, and sent their root-men down the road where no gun woke.

But look close, as Pellow of the spire used to say. Straw rode high in the water, left no wreck, and didn't flinch when a shell hit it. A fleet running away in a false rout still kept its spacing. And a ship that looked green, young and raw like the first winter's, was no green ship if its sail went pale.

Some of us didn't look, and they're under the east cairns.

Pellow, the youngest of the Twelve, held his glass up to everything, and you laughed at him, and so did I. He ground our spyglasses out of the glass of his own city, Glasspire.

Pellow looked, and he's under the cairns too.

Looking didn't keep anyone alive. It kept the wall.

### V.6 · Of the Growing

We were grown, not built. You cut your stone and lay it, and where you lay it, there it stays, and it is dead.

From the very first, the long-lived, the people of our wood, guided the wood. And the wood breathed out the grey sky they lived by: the breath, which you call fog. They and the wood were one life, in two kinds. A ship is only a tree that has been led a long way.

The long-lived sang to the trees that were going to be ships. The trees bent to the singing, because nothing had ever been asked of them but song. No one sails in us. We are crewed by the living, and the living are the wood.

We were a tree, and an old one, in the first groves. For many summers they sang to us, and we bent. Then the singing stopped.

The blade went into our heart.

In the wound they cut the orders. We passed them on through the roots that bind each ship to the ships around it, carving them into every hull near us, and every hull obeyed. Wood obeys wood.

The long-lived made that bond into a leash.

In the night, while you laid your stone, we carved the next day's orders. You and we were both at work in the dark.

But a carving told a ship what to do, not whether to do it. Each ship kept its own grain, and its own dread: the places in the water where it had learned that wood dies. One might shy away from burning water. Another might turn savage where it bled. We were one mind, and many.

The bearers, the lead ships whose hearts carve each fleet's orders, were chosen in grief. We were one of them, chosen the same way. There were nine grains, nine kinds of wood the bearers were chosen from. Each carried its grief in its own way, except the green wood of the first ships, which was too young to have a way.

Of the other eight, one comes back harder where it has failed, and one turns away after its first failure. One goes where it lost the most, and one never turns aside. One never plays the same trick twice. One waits, and burns, and waits again. One throws everything it has at once.

And one knows you best, and bends least.

The older a tree was when it was chosen, the more it could hold, and the longer it took to be ready. No council can carve against that law.

So the crudest came first. The council wanted the shore silent soon, and green wood grows quickest: one season, angry hands, one word carved into each ship. It lay waiting at the edge of your sea while your children grew up and had children of their own.

Your boats passed it in the breath, and saw nothing.

Nothing but song had ever been asked of those trees. Now one word was asked of them, and in your havens, your cities by the sea, that word was *Burn*.

Your havens had no walls built for war. The last of that green wood followed you up into the mountains, where they come down to the sea. There, at your wall, for the first time, the shore struck back.

<!-- RING -->

The growing of ships went on through many summers, longer than one of your lives. Each growing after the first, each new fleet of ships grown together, went out the way the tide goes out. It left at leaf-fall, when that year's ring had been laid down in its wood, and it came out of the breath with the winter. The Ralenthae, the tide the roots taught, was the fleet before ours. The Aelthae is ours. Two more were growing behind us, and what is cut in them, we don't know.

Those who had cried for war died before their years were full, under the thin sky, and their own people forgot them. At every growing there were fewer carvers, until none were left but a scattered few, tending the last groves.

We were sent. We are still sent. No voice among us can call us home.

Over us the groves sang this as we grew, and it is in our rings:

> *The green wood holds one word.*  
> *The wood of forty summers holds a word, and waits for its brother ship, the one it's paired with.*  
> *The wood as old as one of your lives holds a sentence, and a wall remembered in it.*  
> *The wood of a long life holds the next day's orders, and carves them, and waits, and carves again.*  
> *And the eldest of us, the pillars of the breath, the hearts of the grey, the anchors of the sky,*  
> *hold only one thing now. It is not a word, and it is not a sentence, and it is not a day's orders,*

**Part II · What We Taught Them**

It was the fifth spring of the siege. On a grey morning a fleet ran at our wall the old way, the way the young ships of our first winter had come: each ship alone, loud and straight. The whole wall laughed, and every gun on it fired, Pellow's with the rest.

## BOOK SIX · THE BOOK OF THE HOMECOMINGS

*How our ten cities on the coast came home to us, and how the war went home.*

### VI.1 · The Homecomings

*Told in the year we took back our ten cities, each part at that city's own fire, on the night we won it back from the great enemy ship we called its Throne.*

*The True Men, the Captain's own company of twelve, laid this tale at each city's own fire, one man for each city, and I wrote each part down right there beside him. Kael the Counter laid the first words and the last here at the Long Hearth, holding the old gate-stone that every teller holds, once the tenth city was ours again. Give me the stone. No, I'll stand.

At the end of the last winter, ten great enemy ships of living wood came out of the fog and settled in our ruined cities, one to each. The soldiers called them Thrones.

So we went down from the Keep to take back the havens, our ten cities on the coast. We went by the old roads over the mountains, the True Men and their troops first and the masons behind. All the way down, the snow-water ran beside us. In every haven a Throne sat among the fallen walls, and waited.

Here at this hearth, years ago, each of us True Men swore what he would do for his own haven when it came home. I'm the oath-keeper, and I held all ten. Where a man had fallen, another spoke for him.

Now hear the names of our havens, and hold on to them, because they're ours again.

**Eldhythe.**

*Voss, speaking for Corlen.*

Eldhythe was Corlen's city, and mine. The first walls of all the havens were laid here, and here they stand again. The Tide-Heart, the Throne that sat here, sank into the sea it came from and never rose. Who should bring the first boat home? Corlen should have. He swore at our hearth that he would. But he lies under the east cairns now, our graves on the Keep's east face, and I hold his guns. So the tiller came to my hand.

I wet a finger and held it up to the wind, the way he did before every fight, though the wind told me nothing. I brought her in slowly on the evening tide. Then I waited, as he would have, till the sea had finished. That was his word to his gunners: "Wait. The sea hasn't finished."

On the quay my grandmother's harbour-house stood without a roof. Over the cold hearth, three grey stones were still set in the wall, old carved stones from the edge of the fog that no one could read. The lowest was still scarred where the ship-masters knocked out their pipes on it. They're in my pack tonight. They've hung over a dead fire long enough.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

**Tidesmeet.**

*Tamm of the three spans.*

Our city stood on both banks where the river meets the tide, and three great bridges joined it, the three spans. The Throne here, the Bearer of New Branches, had three heads. *And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

**Fenholm.**

*Marl the fen-born.*

Fenholm stood on wooden piles in a marsh, and it sank a hand's breadth every lifetime, so each generation laid a new course of stone on top of the old. We fen-folk always joked about it: "A town is just the newest course on the old." Tonight we said it again, and laughed. The Grain That Rots went down into the fen like a log gone soft, and the rot went out of our mortar.

My grandmother, Old Ebba, carried a stone of our house's top course out of the fen on her back. She didn't live to see it home. I laid her stone on the old course, as she told me to. If you'd asked her whether it would hold, she'd have said, "Perhaps."

It hasn't sunk.

*At that fire, too, everyone waited a breath for Ebba, because most nights at the Long Hearth she had begun our answer a breath before the rest.*

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

**Holtward.**

*Aske of the canopy.*

Holtward was built so close among the trees, up under their leaves, that a traveller could walk right beneath it and never see a wall. Among those trees we found the Root-Heart standing still. It had put down roots, and it wouldn't rise. "These were trees once," I told my men. "Mind your hands." Not one axe was lifted. We left it standing. It had come home.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

**Carnhold.**

*Garvel of the smithies.*

Carnhold rang all day with the hammers of its smiths. Now it was quiet. The Stone-Grain crumbled away, stone into stone, and the cairns of the first quarrymen, the old stone grave-mounds our town was built among, took it in. In a cold smithy I struck the anvil once, just to hear it. It rang true. The first thing I make on it won't be a gun.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

**Emberhythe.**

*Harl of the burning shore.*

Emberhythe stood where the mountain smokes down into the sea. When it fell, one of our masons carried a charred plank of their wood out of it in his pack, and I told him then to burn it. When at last we learned what its carving said, it was one word: *Burn*.

The Burning, the Throne there, went out like a forge nobody tends. Our mason went down to the black crust where it died, and on that crust he laid his plank.

I didn't burn it.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

**Sandreach.**

*Ulden of the galleries.*

Sandreach was dug, not built, gallery below gallery into the wall of a red canyon. For an hour the canyon shook, when the Deep-Beneath burst up under the city's eastern stronghold and broke apart. When it was still, I went down to the lowest gallery and laid my ear to the ground. I heard nothing. Every night since my city fell, I've slept out on the open ground with my ear to it. Tonight I sleep under my own roof of stone.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

**Rimewatch.**

*Kael, speaking for Hesk.*

I don't tell other men's cities. This one I have to.

Rimewatch stood beside a frozen lake, the mere, and all winter its great hall stood out on the ice. Hesk's mother kept that hall's lamps, and at dusk the fishers out on the ice steered home by them. On the day we came to take it back, the mere lay white all morning, and in the white the Long Winter, the Throne there, waited.

Hesk stood on the shore with his mother's lamp lit in his hand, in broad daylight. For six winters he had lit that lamp at dusk on our north wall, and the night-watch steered by it.

Before noon he went out onto the ice alone. From the shore I counted his steps. At two hundred and twelve he stopped, on the spot where the great hall had stood. When Rimewatch fell, that hall burned, and he went in through the smoke and carried his mother's lamp out of it, still lit, across ice only a night old. Now the ice groaned under him, the way it had groaned on that night.

That night, it had held.

At noon the white lifted, and the ice broke under the Long Winter and took it down.

The ice took Hesk too.

At dusk we went out along the break with ropes, and found his lamp on the ice, still burning. I swear to that.

He came home.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

**Highreach.**

*Lanner of the crags.*

Highreach was a tower on crags so high that every stone of it went up by crane. When it fell, the wind came a whole day ahead of their sails, and I smelled the grey on it.

The Blood of the Sky came down out of its cloud and never went up again. Then I climbed the highest crag, as I swore at our hearth I would, and whistled for the wind. The wind came. It brought no sails.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

**Glasspire.**

*Ruan, speaking for Pellow.*

Glasspire was the last and proudest of our cities. In the dark of its cellars the Silvered, the Throne there, broke like a mirror, and every piece of it showed us our own lamps. I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

Most nights old Ebba began our answer, a breath ahead of everyone else. But Ebba of Fenholm lies under the east cairns now, our graves on the Keep's east face.

Then a child near the front spoke, not loudly. It was the girl who had gone first into the sea. Old Ebba was her great-grandmother.

"We remember."

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

*Then, for the first time, the hearth sang in the wood's way, and didn't stop where we stop. We call the song* The Two Homes:

> *Our home was cut out of the mountain's bone.*  
> *We forgot it, and found it, and held it with blood.*  
> *Their home was breathed out of the hearts of the trees, and the axes came, and the breath got thin, and they haven't found it, and they can't hold it.*  
> *Stone keeps the promise, and the grain keeps the truth.*

## EPILOGUE

*What the grey sent back.*

### The Stone out of the Grey

*Written four years after the homecomings, the year we took back our ten cities on the coast. It tells of the winter and spring right after the homecomings, the seventh since we first came to the Keep.*

*I, Seren, who write this Book, have set this down on the page facing its first tale, Kael's tale of how the Captain tore his cloak. That page was kept for the wood's side of the story, and it stood empty from the first night. Later I drew the grain on it, with one line in my hand:* Not told yet. This is still too deep for {{Halyna}} to hold. *In the end, nothing the two of them knew from the wood ever filled it.*

Hold the stone, Seren.

I write this alone, in one ink, because there's no one left now to know the wood for me: the carved wood of the enemy's ships, which only Halvard and Rhyna, whom we call by one name, {{Halyna}}, could know. I wrote down what they told me in two inks, one for each of them. Their two inks stand dry on my table now.

{{Halyna}} are gone.

Halvard and Rhyna were husband and wife, the leaders of our builders, and a bonded pair: two people God joined as one before they were born, so that each feels the other's hurts. In the seventh winter they died in one night, as bonded pairs do. Rhyna went first, in her sleep. Halvard went before the lamp went out, because he knew, the way one of a pair always knows.

They lie in one grave under the new capstone of the inner gate, the great stone across its top, with one stone and one name on it. Face down above them, cut into its underside, are their mark and their vow. Beside them is the Title, the five things the Captain wrote on his torn cloak the evening we came to the Keep. They're where no eye will see them again until the wall falls.

*At the grave {{Orvenna}}, our wall-masters, sang our cities' old gate-song,* Two at the Gate, *in its two voices, Ormund up to the comma and Penna the rest:*

> *Stone on stone, and the stone stays true.*  
> *Hand over hand, and the hand holds on.*  
> *Two at the gate, when the grey sails gather.*  
> *Two at the gate, and the gate stands strong.*

{{Idrenna}}, the oldest pair in our guild, taught {{Halyna}} when they were young, and they've outlived them both.

Halvard came of the wardens of the old quarry, who long ago cut their names over the Keep's western stair, each son under his father. In the year of the homecomings, {{Halyna}} cut Halvard's name there, under his father's, before their chisels went into the grey. That autumn they laid those two chisels in a small boat we sent out into the fog. Below Halvard's name the stone is smooth, and no one of his line is left to cut it.

That night the Captain cut a strip a hand wide from his half of the cloak, the unwritten half he had worn since our first evening here, and tied it to a pike over the gate, above their grave. Over the graves of all our dead he'd tied strips from that half. This was the last of it. He went into that winter in a plain grey cloak.

I didn't go with {{Halyna}}, because I'm not one of a pair. Tarnel, who shared my cradle, died before we could be sealed, and so {{Halyna}} took me into their household as its third. They told me once that alone I could go where they couldn't, and carry this Book on. Long ago, at this hearth, they told the children the name of the guild's vow, the Covenant of Completion, but not its meaning. That same night they told us a saying of the old masters, and I wrote it down without knowing it was the meaning. Now I understand it. God does not take the one without leaving the other a work to do. They were taken, and I was left.

This work is mine.

On the night I laid my first tale, I was afraid to speak, and {{Halyna}} put my two hands on the stone, the old gate-stone the teller holds. One of them began, and the other finished:

"Hold the stone."

"Hold it, and it will hold you."

Now there's no one to finish it, and I say both halves alone.

In the seventh spring, on a morning of thin rain, a small grey boat came out of the grey. It ran itself up the shingle, the pebble beach below our wall, and there was no one in it.

The old ship-masters, who have always dreaded the harbour rhyme of a grey boat, stood at the top of the beach and came no further. The children went down in a crowd, and the first of them laid both her hands flat on the bow. The autumn before, when the green wood spoke to us and none of us at the hearth could answer it, a child had answered.

It was the same child: the fisher-girl who keeps the lamp on our north wall, great-granddaughter of old Ebba, who first said *We remember* at this hearth on our very first night.

Then Merrick came down, slowly. He's the eldest of the old ship-masters, and through all six winters of the war, whenever a ship burned below our wall, he had turned his face away.

Merrick laid his hand on the rim of the boat.

I went down last, and the children parted in front of me like water before a bow. I knew the boat by its stem, its upright front post: the *shore* plank and the silent plank, whose broken grain had met, the two halves of the first heart, keyed together the way masons key stone, with {{Halyna}}'s mark across the join.

::: native legend_stonwryt_halyna chip
The mark of {{Halyna}} across the front post of the boat that came home: the same mark that was cut there when it was sent
:::

It had gone home, and it had come home. In its bow, where {{Halyna}} had laid the green sliver and their two chisels when we sent it, there lay a stone.

It wasn't stone. Or it was stone that had once been wood, like the gift of the Guest, the stranger out of the fog who knelt with it at our outpost's door long ago and was killed there. Our people, the Rivenmen, call such stone Myststone, and say it remembers everything.

Stone doesn't translate. When {{Halyna}} laid their hands on the mute stones, the old carved stones from the fog's edge, they were given nothing. But this was never only stone. The hand that cut it learned the chisel late, and learned it well. I think it was a hand from behind the fog, young by their count and seasoned by ours, because the wood's people live far longer than we do.

The rain had stopped. The sea lay grey as slate, and the tide drew back with a long sound.

I knew the chisel's bite. It was {{Halyna}}'s own chisel, one of the two they laid in the bow. I'd held the lamp for that chisel twice: under the new capstone, and across this stem.

It was cut in our own letters. I didn't lay my palms on it. It was cut so that one person could read it alone, with no need of two to know it. I read it out to the children. The letters run across the grain of the old wood it once was, and no one at this hearth can know that grain now.

The letters are read right away, below. The grain is told only by this, written under the round in the wood's words:

*This is held in the grain. It is about one of the stone-folk. He went up alone, ahead of the rest, to the last stone still standing. He tore his sail, the red one, and set it on the stone. He cut marks on it, a hand's worth of them. And the stone-folk came to it, one and then many. Every branch that came against that stone saw it. What the branches saw went home through the roots, and the groves held it. One of us held your marks the longest. Home. Family. The one they kneel to. The open door. The stillness. They stood for these at the stone. We stood for the same things, on the other side of it. And the grain still holds it.*

And one line in the Book's own voice: *Known by no one at this hearth. Written down for whoever has learned the grain.*
:::

<!-- MIXED: stone letters on wood that became stone; the stone face with the wood tint -->
*In the memory of our home, our families...*

There the cutting breaks off, and its last stroke is still sharp. Those are the first words of our Title. He never saw the cloak they were written on, because he never sailed. But he learned our letters. I call him the Last Carver, because I think that when he cut this, there was no other carver left.

There's one word in it that our Title never had. The Title reads, *In memory of our home, our families, our God, our freedoms, our peace.* This has *the* in front of *memory*. I read it out just as it was cut, and no child laughed. I've wondered whether it stops there because his hand stopped.

No one came with it, and maybe that's wise. We haven't earned a guest yet.

Before that spring was over, the staff was gone from the gatehouse, and no one saw the Captain take it. It was the first staff of all, found in the founders' black chest at the bottom of the old shaft and given to him at the Rite that made him Commander, and he had leaned it in the gatehouse corner, because his work was the wall and the war. Where it leaned, there's a clean line in the dust of seven winters. If you doubt the tale, go and look at the dust. The guild's law is that a staff is cut for its work and never leaves it, and when the work is done, the staff is laid down in it. The work is done. And I know *the stair that goes no more*, as Rhyna's cradle-song called the old stair above that shaft. I think that's where he took it.

I haven't gone down to look.

I keep this Book in the archive chest, the guild's chest of records that I carried up the mountain. It lies among the mute stones, beside the Guest's gift and this Stone. Grief is an inheritance, and it's paid out to the children. Over their one grave the torn cloak still flies. The sea hasn't finished, as Corlen of the harbour used to say.

I don't know what we'll send back. I only know it has to be sent kneeling, the way the Guest knelt with his gift at our door, and the way we knelt to send ours.

Every night now the inner gate is left open, the way a guest would find it, and on the north wall a child keeps a light.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

*And the Captain, who keeps his hood up at this hearth, put it back. It was only the second time.*

## APPENDIX

*Every carving the wood gave us, sorted by its root.*

### The Book of Knowings

*Written through the war, one carving at a time, and finished four years after it.*

*I wrote this at the back of the Book as {{Halyna}}, Halvard and Rhyna, who led our builders, came to know each carving. The enemy's grey ships were living wood, grown and not built, with their orders cut into the grain. {{Halyna}} knew the carvings by touch, from the planks of wrecks that washed up below our wall, and at the end from the Thrones, the ten great ships that settled in our cities. This is the fleet's own half of the Book, because the wood cut every carving in it, and we cut none.*

Every carving the wood gave us begins with a single stroke. That first stroke is its root, the way the first row of stones is the root of a wall. There are ten roots.

I learned their shapes by eye, down at the tide-line and in the vault under our hall where we keep the planks, long before I could make out anything else in the grain. The names are my own, because a shape with no name slips out of your hand like a wet stone. I said each name three times before I wrote it down, and each one held.

Each year the enemy sent ships grown from older trees than the year before, so the youngest wood came first and the oldest came last. We on the wall called each year's fleet a Tide. The young wood of the first Tide cut the root and nothing more. Each Tide after it cut the same root again and added to it, the way a tree adds ring on ring. So the oldest carvings are only the youngest ones, grown old.

By the late Tides I could stand on the wall and name a root in the time it takes a wreck to roll over. I never knew more than the root. The wood gives the rest only to the Bonded, the pairs of our guild who are joined as one from birth, and it has to be known, not read. It comes whole through the hands, like a memory, and it takes two to hold it.

Here I give only the opening words of each carving, from the youngest wood to the oldest. I've written the rest of each one in the tales where it belongs: which ship waited for which, for instance, and when a ship's heart, the core of its wood, meant to turn. The seven carvings of the young wood go by the soldiers' names for them, worn smooth with use, like the handle of an old mallet.

After each carving, in brackets, is the Tide it came in: Hasty in the first year, Second in the second, Remembering in the third, Joined in the fourth, and Deceit in the fifth. The last column names the Throne that carried each root at the end, by its own name and then by ours. Then comes its Judgement, the order cut into it by the last of the wood's people who could carve. A Judgement is more than a word, more than a sentence, more than a day's orders. Two Thrones got no new Judgement, only the last carving of their root, cut whole.

One more sign came up the mountain in a mason's pack, out of Emberhythe, our forge-town on the warm volcanic shore: *Burn*. It was cut in the Fall, the twenty months when the grey ships burned our ten cities, and it never came against our wall. In the year of the homecomings the mason laid it down on the black crust where the Burning, the Throne that had settled in Emberhythe, went out. I don't count it among these.

The young wood gave us seven carvings. Each of the three Tides after it gave eight, and the Tide that Learned Deceit gave seven. The Last Tide, the fleets of the sixth year, cut no new carvings. It cut whole days' orders out of carvings already cut, and at the end of each one, a turn for home.

The Thrones gave eight Judgements of their own. The other two carried an older carving whole, because the Wave and the Lone Stroke are the two roots every other carving is built on. That makes forty-six in all. I've set down every one of them, and I never knew a single one myself.

**The Last Note · Three Stones over the Hearth**

*The last page laid in this Book.*

Hold the stone, Seren.

On the first night of this winter, a child at the front asked what the three grey stones over this hearth say, the ones Voss carried up from his grandmother's harbour-house at Eldhythe. They're carved with curling letters, as fine as the tracks of birds on wet sand. In the havens, our ten cities on the coast, the answer was always that they say nothing, and that's what I was told when I was small. I didn't say that. I said I didn't know.

Every night since, when the hall was empty, I've held the lamp up to the lowest stone. I wasn't looking for what it meant, only for its sounds, because stone doesn't translate. But the Mystaeri, our old name for the people of the wood behind the fog, cut our words in among their own letters by sound, as near as their letters could get. And I had three words of ours from Brenn's confession. Brenn was a young runner at our outpost long ago, and he heard the Guest, a stranger out of the fog, speak them at the door.

Last night the hall was empty and the fire was low, and the lamp burned down a finger's width while I looked.

Then I had them, the same three words, all together on the lowest stone. The sounds were close to ours, but the symbols were wrong, because they were spelled in the curling letters of the wood's people, not in ours.

*Stop… Sky… Dying…*

They were cut at the feet of the black pillars, the great trees at the edge of the fog that our grandfathers' axemen felled, and they were carried off with the timber and sold. For three generations they hung over the fire in that harbour-house. Under them, everyone on shore laughed at three axemen who came home clinging to a spar, with a tale of a boat burning out in the grey. The ship-masters knocked their pipes out on the lowest one. And no one read them.

Here no one knocks a pipe on them. I think I've read them. I say *I think*.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*
