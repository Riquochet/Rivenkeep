# THE VOICE OF THE BOOK · v3

*The binding voice guide for every writer of the Book of the Riven Stone and its Plain Words. It sets the register that `craft.md` left open, the HEAVY dial, as four judges adjusted it after the calibration round, and it fixes how each teller sounds. Jack's notes of 2026-10-02 and the orchestrator's reading notes (`wf11/jack_2026-10-02.md`) outrank this page. This page outranks `craft.md` wherever the two speak of voice, register or numbers. For the build (the shape of a tale, the death ledger, each tale's question, halt, turn and close) use `wf11/craft.md`. For the full grammar tables and their sources see `wf11/research/R3_archaic.md`. The models are the three calibration leaves in `wf11/samples/`: `I1.md`, `IV3.md` and `IV4.md`, each a Book leaf followed by its Plain Words twin.*

---

## 0 · The whole of it, on one page

Jack's notes, in four lines:
- The v2.0 Book was "a bunch of run on sentences". Bring back the v1.3 style.
- Make the Book hard for a first-time reader, "something that, if you read long enough, you would start to understand".
- Make the Plain Words easy, and truly different.
- Seren loves the tongue and writes every teller better than he tells it. The stories must build. "This is fiction and it is the funnest to write."

The answer, in eight lines:

1. **HEAVY.** Use Early Modern grammar in every line of narration and speech, as Malory and the King James Bible use it: *thou* for one near hearer, *ye* and *you* for many, *-eth* and *-est*, *hath* and *doth*, strong pasts such as *spake*, *sware* and *bare*, negation without *do*, the verb before the subject after a fronted word.
2. **Short clauses, full stops.** The difficulty comes from grammar, never from length. A Book sentence may be harder than its Plain twin, but never longer.
3. **A few old words, often.** A small stock comes back until the reader owns it. Never use a one-off antique word, and never an invented native word.
4. **Plain at the hinge.** The kick, the tear, the blow, the death and the first instant of a knowing are told in words that 1611 and today share. The archaism lives in the sentences around them.
5. **Thin the endings.** Allow one *-eth* to a sentence at most, and rarely more than one in a hundred words. If a passage wants more, recast it into the past or the plural. Never stack the endings.
6. **One register, many voices.** Seren's polish must never flatten a teller. Kael stays curt and counting, Brenn stays penitent and carrying, and the wood stays vast and slow.
7. **Fixed matter stays outside the dial.** The songs, Jack's lines, the hearth's liturgy, the Guest's sentence and the warden's letter are never archaized.
8. **The Plain Words is a retelling.** It uses easy modern English, flowing rather than choppy, and it is no longer than its Book leaf.

**What the judges found.** Register A (HEAVY) won on three of four lenses because it alone was really hard and still learnable. Its forms were consistent and correct, and its play on *tell*, meaning both to count and to recount, teaches itself. B's archaism was cosmetic: inversions and *ere*, with no old verbs. A first reader goes straight through it, and its Plain twin would have been re-punctuation again, which is the fault Jack named. C's invented native words (*Ston*, *trenn*, *Tumar*, *myst*, *haskard*, *keth*, *lodh*) muddied the scenes and added lore, so they are off. A's faults became the rules of this page:
- creed set before the story;
- the heaviest forms on the hinge words (*clomb*, *tare*, *brake*);
- fourteen *-eth* forms in 830 words, which tipped into pastiche;
- one-off antiquities;
- the risk that a uniform polish would make every teller sound alike.

## 1 · The grammar

### 1.1 Pronouns

| | Subject | Object | Before a consonant | Before a vowel or *h*; alone | Self |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | I | me | my | **mine** (*mine hand*, *it is mine*) | myself |
| one near hearer | **thou** | **thee** | **thy** | **thine** (*thine eyes*) | thyself |
| he · she · it | he · she · it | him · her · it | his · her · its | his · hers | himself · herself · itself |
| we | we | us | our | ours | ourselves |
| many; **any pair**; one held in honour | **ye** | **you** | your | yours | yourselves |
| they | they | them | their | theirs | themselves |

Who gets which, in this Book:

| Speaker → hearer | Form | Example |
|---|---|---|
| a teller → the hearth | *ye* (subject), *you* (object) | *snow lay on the hearth where ye sit now* · *He will tell you it was not like that.* |
| a teller → the children at the front | *ye* as a vocative | *Look to the end of the bench, ye children at the front.* |
| anyone → a bonded pair | *ye* / *you*; the pair answers *we two* | *"We two hold the stone,"* |
| the wood → the Shore | *ye* / *you* / *your* | *Ye took us for stone.* · *Ye join your words with small words* |
| the wood → the one hand that held it | **thou**, once, at the close | *None held us long, till thou heldest us…* (IV.4, to Rhyna) |
| anyone → God; in an oath; in a rite | *thou* | *God, thou madest these two one* |
| one comrade → another, in love or in scorn | *thou*, with full forms | rare, and always for a reason |
| the Captain → a True Man | *thou* (Orrowen *tho*) | |
| a True Man → the Captain | *ye* (honour) | |

- A switch from *ye* to *thou* in one speech is a rush of love or scorn. Use it at most once a tale, and only where the switch is the point. IV.4's *thou heldest* is the model.
- **Never** use a singular *they*. For an unknown person, write *whoso*, *he that* or *one*: *whoso kneeleth at your door is under your roof*.
- *Ye* is never an object. *Thee* is never a subject.

### 1.2 Verbs, present tense

| | be | have | do | say | go | most verbs | will · shall · can · may · must |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | am | have | do | say | go | love | will · shall · can · may · must |
| thou | art | hast | dost / doest | sayest | goest | lovest | wilt · shalt · canst · mayest · must |
| he · she · it | is | **hath** | **doth** / doeth | **saith** | **goeth** | **loveth** | will · shall · can · may · must (**never** *-eth*) |
| we · ye · they · **a pair** | are | have | do | say | go | love | will · shall · can · may · must |

- A pair takes the plural, always: *{{Halyna}} say*, never *saith*; *{{Halyna}} are come*; *{{Halyna}} were silent*.
- Every third-person singular present takes *-eth* in the Book. Mixing *-s* and *-eth* in one mouth is a fault. The fixed matter of §1.7 is the only exception: *and the grain holds it still* stays as it is.

### 1.3 Verbs, past tense

Most pasts are as today. *Thou* takes *-est* or *-edst*: *thou heldest*, *thou wast*, *thou didst*. *Wert* is used only after *if*, after *though*, or in a wish.

**The Book's strong pasts. Use one form for each verb, Book-wide.** This is the judges' hinge rule turned into a list. Where 1611 had two pasts in use, the Book takes the old form for the verbs of the road and the shared form for the verbs of the deed. A reader therefore never meets *brake* in one tale and *broke* in the next.

| Always the old form (these teach the pattern) | Always the shared form (these carry the deed) | Never |
|---|---|---|
| **spake** (spoken) · **sware** (sworn) · **bare** (borne: carried, wore) · **wist** (knew a fact) · **bade** (bidden) · **slew** (slain) · **kneeled** · **forgat** (forgotten) | **broke** · **tore** · **climbed** · **struck** · **kicked** · **fell** · **drew** · **took** · **died** | *clomb* · *tare* · *brake* · *holp* · *gat* · *drave* · *trode* · *smote* (one-offs and hinge-killers) |

- *Knew* stays for knowing a person, a skill or a thing by touch (*He knew the blows as trees know the iron*). *Wist* is for knowing a fact (*We wist not that it is but how they speak*).
- *Bare* is for carrying a thing or bearing a mark: *that bare their children on their backs* · *The warden's arm bare one mark* · *they bare the chest out*.

### 1.4 Syntax

- **Negation without *do*:** *I know not.* · *He fought them not.* · *it took not long.* Use *do* only for emphasis, and no more than three times a tale. The models are Jack's own *I did not stop them.* and the cold *Tarnel did not wake.*
- **The verb before the subject after a fronted word:** *Then laughed his men.* · *Then took the stranger a little blade* · *Then was it quiet* · *Down off the riven ridge came the wind*.
- **The object or complement first:** *Twelve were we, and the warden* · *Nineteen I was.* · *No hand laid I on him.* · *A boy was I among the men.* · *Join them he could not.*
- **The *be*-perfect for motion and change:** *the roof was fallen in* · *the hour is come*.
- **Questions by inversion:** *Is it so?* · *Came he alone?*
- **The subjunctive** goes only after *if*, *though*, *lest* and *till*, and in wishes: *A count is no count till it be named.* · *Had it stopped there, I think all our lives had gone otherwise.* Use it nowhere else.
- ***That* for persons:** *soldiers of six broken companies, that knew their officers no more* · *as men write that have written little*.
- **The imperative may take its pronoun:** *Go thou.* · *Stand ye fast.*

### 1.5 How much: the density caps

| What | Cap | The samples |
|---|---|---|
| *-eth*, *hath*, *doth*, *saith* | one to a sentence; about one in 100–150 words of narration | I.1: 1 in 1,075 words · IV.3: 7 in 1,280 · IV.4: 4 in 868 (A had 14 in about 830) |
| Inverted sentences | about one in three; never three in a row without weight behind them | |
| *do*-negation | three a tale at most, each for emphasis | |
| Stock words (§2.1) | spread out; never two in one clause (*ere yonder nigh*) | |
| *thou* forms | only where §1.1 allows them | IV.4: one, at the close |

**How to thin the endings without breaking the grammar:**
- **Put it in the past.** *while a tree's shadow moved the breadth of its own trunk* (not *moveth*).
- **Put it in the plural.** *as men write that have written little* (not *as a man writeth that hath*) · *as trees know the iron* (not *as a tree knoweth*) · *They that come under a roof bring their best* (not *One that cometh … bringeth*) · *Ye join your words* (not *Your tongue joineth*).
- **Use *is*, *was* or a noun.** *Slow is its giving, and true.* (not *It giveth slowly*).
- **Keep the one that plays.** *Wood that time forgat forgetteth not.* This keeps one *-eth*, and the play (*forgat*, *forgetteth*) teaches the form.

### 1.6 The hinge rule

At the halt the plainest word wins. A strike sentence is built from words that are the same in 1611 and today:

> *He tore it down the back.* · *▒▒▒▒ kicked it aside.* · *Both his arms they broke* · *Tarnel did not wake.* · *And the host drew back.* · *He stopped not.*

- No *-eth* or *-est* in a strike sentence. No form from the old-past column. At most one inversion, and only if it is the sentence's whole weight (*A boy was I among the men.*).
- The sentence *before* the strike may be as old as it likes. That is where the reader works, so the strike lands clean.
- Tolkien holds his grand style back for the turn of the Pelennor, which reads as exalted with almost no archaic words (R1 §1.12). Do the same.

### 1.7 Outside the dial: fixed matter, never archaized

- **Every song,** word for word.
- **Jack's lines:**
  - *In memory of our home, our families, our God, our freedoms, our peace.*
  - *The mortar cries out from the ground. Rise, Stonewrights! Lay stone upon stone. Stand every wall to the very end.*
  - *Rise! Rise! Rise!*
  - *We remember our home too.*
  - *We remember.*
  - *But war is for the short-minded and the angry young.*
  - *When the left hand dies, does not the right one die too?*
  - the Book Five line.
- **The hearth's liturgy:**
  - *This I lay as it was laid for me.* / *This we lay as it was laid for us.*
  - *And the hearth said: We remember.*
  - *This is held in the grain.* … *and the grain holds it still.* · *And no one answered.*
  - *We have carried it a long way* before the grain line.
- ***Stop… Sky… Dying…***
- **The Guest's sentence** (*Stop the felling of the pillars…*). It is the one long modern-English sentence in the wood's leaf, and it lands harder for standing plain against the archaism.
- **The warden's letter:** *Unknown intruder. Hostile intent. Neutralized. Vessel burned and released.* This is the only modern register in the Book, and it is a moral mark (R1 §1.14).
- **The runners' oath:** *"What I saw, I carry. I carry nothing else."* and its four words, *Not as Brenn carried.*
- **The Title** and every `::: native` block.

## 2 · The words

### 2.1 The stock: a few old words, often

Readers keep a strange word only after about eight meetings, and own it after twenty or thirty (R3 §1.6). So the stock is small and it comes back. Never gloss it in the Book. Let its slot in the sentence fix the sense.

| Word | Sense | Where a reader first meets it |
|---|---|---|
| **ere** | before | I.1 *I heard it ere I saw it* |
| **naught** · **aught** | nothing · anything | I.1 *naught but the wind* · IV.3 *Leave naught to show.* |
| **yet** | still, even now | IV.3 *I count them yet.* |
| **but** | only | IV.3 *words without sentences are but sounds, and we heard but sounds* |
| **for** | because (as a joining word) | I.1 *for he had not bidden us follow* |
| **that** | who, which | I.1 *soldiers … that knew their officers no more* |
| **whoso** | whoever | IV.3 · IV.4 |
| **bide** | wait, stay | I.1 |
| **feign** | pretend | I.1 *I will not feign it was.* |
| **wist** · **spake** · **sware** · **bare** · **bade** · **slew** · **kneeled** · **forgat** | §1.3 | the I.1 headnote's *sware* teaches the pattern before the tale begins |
| **morrow** | next day | IV.3 *On the morrow* |
| **scarce** | hardly | IV.3 |
| **nigh**, **oft**, **lest**, **save** (except), **yonder**, **hither**, **thither**, **whither**, **wherefore** (why, in questions only), **sore** (greatly) | R3 §3.2 | spread them through the Book |
| **tell** · **told** | to count, as well as to recount | I.1, all through. See §2.2. |

### 2.2 Words that work twice: Seren's play

The full table is in `craft.md` §4.2. These are the ones calibration proved:

| Word | The play | How the samples teach it |
|---|---|---|
| **tell / told** | count / recount | *A teller am I, and no singer. Heads I tell, and stones, and days. What I told not, that will I not tell.* Then *told it, head by head* · *all told* · *I have told those stones since* · *till I could tell them no more* · and, in the headnote, *as he had told it*. The reader stumbles once and then has it. This is the Book's learnable hardness at its best. |
| **but** | only / however | *Yet words without sentences are but sounds, and we heard but sounds.* |
| **on** | onward / on him | *When he lay still, ▒▒▒▒ screamed on, and some went on.* The juxtaposition carries the cause, and Brenn does not explain it. |
| **leaf, leaves** | a tree's leaf / a page | *the ages lay down on us, leaf on leaf* · *Then leaves about us* (the confession's pages, as the wood felt them) |
| **captain, company** | the rank / the men | *Captain was he of a small company, and we were his company.* |
| **name** | the name cut in / the name cut out | *The guild buried him under his name. The warden's name it cut away.* |

**Seren's turns.** Each tale needs turns of her own, not the guide's examples (judge 3). The samples' new ones are the measure:
- the litany that ends wrong: *The masons said it held naught of worth*;
- the mirror: *He would ask no blood that he gave not. He gave it all, there on the step.*;
- the weak man in one clause: *▒▒▒▒ stood within, and came no further.*;
- the witness's guilt without a verdict: *I stood among them, and I hear that laughter yet.*;
- the gaze by three that ends on the one no one looked at: *To the Captain looked none of them.*

### 2.3 Seasoning, two a tale at most

*ween* (think) · *me thinketh* · *what time* (when) · *it booted not* (it was no use) · *lightly* (quickly) · *eft* (again) · *ware* (aware) · *peradventure* (it may be).

### 2.4 Never

- **No invented native words in the prose.** The NATIVE stock is off. Orrowen stays in the `::: native` blocks, in canon names (*Aelrhen*, *Ael'thar*, the Thrones) and in the Title. If a native word is ever adopted, it comes from the orchestrator as a lock, never from a writer.
- **No one-off antiquities:** *clomb*, *tare*, *brake*, *holp*, *gat*, *drave*, *bigness*, *affright*. If a form will not come back, use the shared one.
- **No tushery:** *forsooth*, *prithee*, *mayhap*, *perchance*, *methinks* piled up, *marry*, *zounds*, *tush*, *ye olde*.
- **No scripture formulas:** *it came to pass*, *and lo*, *verily*, *begat*, *thus saith*.
- **No Christian marks:** *Lord*, *amen*, *saint*, *church*, *gospel*, *Christ*, *rood*. *God* is allowed, and only *God*. Never call the cross-sign holy: write *the half of something, cut off in the middle*.
- **No false friends**, unless the sentence itself fixes the old sense: *want* (meaning lack; write *He sought not to lead*), *still* (meaning always), *presently*, *prevent*, *let*, *suffer*, *meat*, *quick*, *sad*, *silly*, *nice*, *fond*, *naughty*, *awful*, *weird*.
- **No modern words in old dress:** *pretend* (write *feign*), *okay*, *really*, *focus*, *process*, *trauma*, *closure*.
- **No contractions** anywhere in the Book.
- **No *upon* where *on* will do.**
- **No American spelling.** Use the Book's British forms, in the Book and the Plain alike: *grey*, *colour*, *harbour*. The one exception is the warden's letter, which keeps *Neutralized* as it was written.

## 3 · The sentence: v1.3 made concrete

### 3.1 The numbers

| Measure | Target | Calibration samples (prose, fixed matter excluded) | v1.3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mean sentence, a teller | 10–13 words (Kael 9–11) | I.1 Kael 9.9 · IV.3 Brenn 10.4 | I.1 12.0 · IV.3 11.9 |
| Mean sentence, Seren or the wood | 11–16 | IV.4 wood 11.3 | IV.4 15.8 |
| Sentences under 10 words | 40–55% (a roll or a litany may push a teller higher) | 55% · 49% · 43% | 39% across the Book |
| Sentences over 30 words | one in twenty at most, and only an *and*-list | none (longest 25 · 24 · 28) | 9% |
| Semicolons | two a tale at most | 1 · 0 · 1 | |
| *and* per sentence | 0.5 or fewer | 0.45 · 0.42 · 0.49 | 0.69 |
| The crisis stretch | the shortest sentences in the tale | IV.3 thirds 10.3 / **8.7** / 10.8 | IV.3 13.7 / **9.5** / 14.8 |

The archaism makes short sentences read slower than their length, so the Book may sit a little under v1.3's means. It must not drop into a primer. When three short sentences in a row carry no weight, join two of them with an *and* that carries a deed. That is the King James and Malory habit v1.3 already had: *Old men came, and women with bundles, and children asleep on their feet, and soldiers of six broken companies, that knew their officers no more.*

### 3.2 How a strike sentence lands

1. **The breath before.** One beat of stillness, given as a thing seen or heard, never as a feeling:
   - *No man in the ward spake. Among the women a child coughed, and his mother hushed him. After that there was naught but the wind.*
   - *No man of us moved. Behind him a gull came down, and walked among the stones.*
2. **The strike.** It gets its own paragraph. It runs three to seven words, subject–verb–object, in shared words, with no adverb and usually no comma: *He tore it down the back.* · *▒▒▒▒ kicked it aside.*
3. **The aftermath.** One small physical consequence, with an old word order to make the reader slow down again. No feeling and no meaning:
   - *A small sound it made, the sound of old cloth giving. Over the wind I heard it.*
   - *Into the dust by the wall it went.*
4. **The pay, later and without a gloss.** *Then was it quiet, and the gull was gone.*

### 3.3 Six shapes Seren uses

- **The triple, the third broken:** *Some shook. Some wept. Some fell, and would not rise.*
- **The look by three:** *The people looked for a general… They looked for the lords… They looked to the Stonewrights…* Then the turn: *To the Captain looked none of them.* Then: *The True Men looked to their Captain. The Captain looked at the wall.*
- **The mirror:**
  - *Men had I heard break, on the long retreat. Never till that dusk a people.*
  - *I carried the lie in a day and a night. I carried the truth forty years, and gave it to no one.*
- **The pair on two lines,** where the change of one word carries the guilt:

  > *His men obeyed.*
  >
  > *We obeyed.*
- **The widening count:** *One voice. A dozen. Then hundreds, till I could tell them no more.* / *I stopped. It is the only time I ever stopped.*
- **The road by anaphora**, ending on the shortest line: *Then a jolting in the dark… Then old arms, two pairs… Then small arms, two pairs, going up, in the cold.* / *Then one pair, a long way.*

### 3.4 Paragraphs

- Give a great line its own paragraph. Expect four to eight such lines a tale.
- Keep the other paragraphs to one to five sentences.
- Two one-line paragraphs may stand together only when they are a pair, as *His men obeyed.* / *We obeyed.* are.

### 3.5 How a teller speaks to the hearth

- **The call** comes first: *Give me the stone. No, I will stand. Enough have I sat this winter.* A teller is a person within three sentences.
- **The oath or the method** follows, in one short paragraph. It tells the hearer how this man knows what he knows: *By the lintel of the western stair I swear it… A teller am I, and no singer.*
- **The first sentence of story** gives an hour, a place, one sense and the seed of something wrong:
  - *At dusk the column came in under the outer gate. I heard it ere I saw it: many feet, and no voice among them.*
  - *One morning at summer's end, out of the grey came a small grey boat. There was no wind, and the boat came anyway.*
  - **Never** open on a creed, a résumé or a custom. Calibration moved the True Men's creed from the top of I.1 down to the roll for exactly this reason.
- **The hall is *ye*.** Point at it in the present, at most twice in a tale, and only at the turn or the close:
  - *snow lay on the hearth where ye sit now*
  - *Look to the end of the bench, ye children at the front. There he sitteth, with his hood up.*
- **The close** is a flat fact or a picture, then the fixed words, and nothing after them: *He will tell you it was not like that. It was like that.* / *This I lay as it was laid for me.*

## 4 · The voices

Seren's hand is on every leaf. The register is hers, and so are the turns and the economy. But each teller must hear himself in her ink, bettered and not replaced. The idiom stays his: the rhythm, what he looks at, what he will not say. The tellers' verdict that her version is best is shown three times in the Book, lightly and never stated: the I.1 headnote, the V.5 headnote and {{Idrenna}}'s nod in II.3 (`craft.md` §4.4).

**Kael the Counter** (I.1, III.1, III.2, V.5, VI.1)
- *Sentence:* the curtest in the Book. He counts, names, swears and corrects. Numbers come first in his word order: *Twelve.* · *Thirty-one there are, and four of them move.* · *Two thousand and some hundreds, all told.*
- *Marks:* *I swear to that* (once or twice a leaf) · *It was like that.* · *I counted us twice, and it took not long.* · the play on *tell* and *told*.
- *What Seren gives him:* one picture a tale that he would not have made, and lets stand (*It is a sound like ice going out on a river.*), and the *tell* pun he did not know he was making.
- *Never:* a simile of his own, anything lyric, a reason he did not count, *Hear how*, or a gloss.

**Seren Two-Inks** (the foreword; I.2; I.4; II.1; IV.1; the annals; V.1, V.2, V.7, VI.3; the Epilogue; the Knowings)
- *Sentence:* a little longer than a teller's (12–16 words), with one painted picture to a movement, of fifteen words or fewer, from a trade.
- *Habits:* she holds the lamp, she withholds, she plays on the road and goes plain at the grave.
- *Marks:* *Hold the stone, Seren.* opens her **own** leaves. It does not open an annal written after another teller's words. In the IV.3 annal, *{{Halyna}} laid theirs beside them* does the work of handing over the stone.
- *Her annals:* short and cold, 150 words at most. They give the record and one kindness of the dead (*Tarnel knotted his net with his free hand. I watched the knots.*), and then they stop.
- *Never:* a lecture on a word, a footnote to her own Book, a verdict (*cruel*, *brave*), or a decoding of the facing leaf.

**Hale the runner** (I.3; V.5 Count III, through Kael)
- *Sentence:* short of breath. He slips into the present at the run, with *I run.* and *He standeth still.*, for three to five sentences, and keeps any *-eth* to one in a sentence. His messages are three words long.
- *Marks:* *I am not a teller. I run.* · *I ran it.*
- *What Seren gives him:* the slowed hour, told through his own things (a cart on a long hill, ripples counted like the rings of a log).
- *Never:* reflection longer than a line, or anything he did not see.

**Voss who asked** (I.5, IV.5)
- *Sentence:* he asks, and he circles back in the same words. He tells things at second hand and says so: *So my grandmother told it me.*
- *Marks:* *Ye may weigh it.* · *I did not understand it then. I think I do now.* He says the second only once, at Ebba's *Perhaps*.
- *What Seren gives him:* the ring (IV.5 opens and closes on the screaming hull) and the rhyme turning in him.
- *Never:* certainty, a custom of the coast, or a moral at the close.

**Corlen of the harbour** (I.1 roll, III.1, III.2, V.5 Count II, VI.1)
- *Sentence:* the oldest True Man and the fewest words. The sea is the subject of his sentences, and he himself is seldom their subject.
- *Marks:* *"Wait. The sea has not finished."* · the wet finger held up.
- *Never:* hurry, more than two sentences at a time, or the first shot.

**The True Men** (III.1, III.2, VI.1)
- *Sentence:* one man, one image and one want, in one to three sentences each. The v1.3 Homecomings captions are the base.
- *Marks:*
  - Tamm: *"One span is a plank. Three is a city."*
  - Pellow: *"Look close."*
  - Hesk: *"Someone has to keep a light."*

## 5 · The wood leaf

**The order** (the model is `samples/IV4.md`):

1. **Dateline and headnote.** Whose hand was on the wood, and where the other of them was, in one or two sentences. A listener's question may open it, so that the leaf answers someone at the hearth: *On the night Brenn was read, a child asked what the Guest had given.*
2. **`<!-- READING -->`.** {{Halyna}} piece the memory together as the wood gives it up.
   - Six to nine italic lines, 80 words at most. The inks alternate line by line, and neither is marked.
   - Show the piecing:
     - a mistaken image corrected: *Leaves. No — words, like leaves.*;
     - a slip into the wood's *our*, caught: *Our — the wood's kin*;
     - a thought broken across both inks: *Three words in him —* / *— and naught to join them. We two have it.*;
     - one gap, marked and never mended: *— the blade — [ ]*.
   - Stop at or near the halt, so that the story fulfils what the reading could not finish.
   - Never write a doubled dash where a line both opens and continues. The line's own dash is the continuation.
3. **The story, in Seren's hand and the wood's *we*.** It opens with *This is held in the grain.* and is laid in three rings parted by `<!-- RING -->`: the wood itself; the deed; and the road with the sentence.
   - It builds as a stone leaf builds.
   - The rite is told as it happens, never in advance. Its meaning arrives as each step is made and broken. In IV.4, *Gift, blood, kinship* is never stated: the gift lies in the dust, *He gave it all, there on the step*, and *Never were the two arms laid together*.
   - The deed's hard image sits alone: *And we were the one his foot found.*
4. **The return.** *That is all he came to say. We have carried it a long way, and the grain holds it still.* / *And no one answered.* This holds until VI.3, where a child answers.

**What the wood can know** (`craft.md` §5.2):
- touch, weight, cold, fire, blood, iron, the sound that passes through it, and a long dark;
- its kin in sawn timber, and that they are dead;
- what came to it through blood or roots: *with it came all of him: his long listening, and his last hour. So we know it as he knew it.*
- **Never** a Shore name, a Shore motive or a date. Shore facts go in the headnote, or nowhere. The gap between the two facing leaves belongs to the reader.

**Halyna discover, Seren writes, other hands act.** {{Halyna}} give the pieces, Seren makes the memory a story, and neither leaf ever explains the other.

## 8 · Plain Words, beside it

Plain Words is a real retelling in easy modern English (`craft.md` §7). Against a truly old Book the gap now opens by itself. Do not let it drift back toward the Book's word order.

- **Retell from the beats, not from the sentences.** No more than 20% of its four-word runs may appear in the Book (the samples: 17%, 16% and 13%). Fixed lines are the same in both.
- **Easy, not choppy.**
  - Sentences of 12 to 15 words on average (the samples, in prose: 13.6, 14.2 and 14.4).
  - 30% or fewer of sentences below 10 words, not counting fixed lines and readings (the samples: 30%, 27% and 28%).
  - Join with *because*, *so* and *but*.
  - No semicolons.
  - Contractions at 10 to 20 in 1,000 words.
  - A Flesch-Kincaid grade of 4 or under (the samples: 3.7, 3.7 and 2.4).
- **No longer than the Book leaf.** Modern English runs longer than old, so cut the way Seren cuts.
- **The voices survive in modern form.** Kael counts. Brenn confesses short. {{Halyna}}'s pieces stay pieces, in modern words (*Leaves? No — words, like leaves.*).
- **It may gloss once, in passing.** For example, *the Stonewrights, the builders*. It turns turns of phrase into plain meaning (*as a man setteth down a load* becomes *as if he had finally put down a heavy load*). It keeps the Book's own short pictures only where they carry the tale (the ice on the river, the mother's hand at the fever, the bird that could sleep in a hand). It adds none of its own, and never writes *Picture a…*.
- **It leaves dark what the Book leaves dark.**
- **It uses the existing Plain practice for the fixed matter:**
  - the Plain verse of each song, as v2.0's Plain has it;
  - *I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.*;
  - *And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*;
  - *and the grain still holds it*;
  - the Plain wording of the Guest's sentence;
  - the warden's letter and *Stop… Sky… Dying…*, word for word.
- **Its headnote is one or two plain sentences, and it has no dateline.**
- **No singular *they* in the Plain either.** *Whoever kneels at your door is under your roof.*

## 11 · PLAIN WORDS

*Binding for every Plain Words leaf. Jack, 2026-10-02: "The Plain Word is just a change in punctuations with a little rephrasing. I want there to be a real difference… The Plain Word should be easy to read." Where this section differs from §0 item 8, §8, §10 item 11 or `craft.md` §7, this section wins. The model is `samples/I1_plain.md`. It replaces the Plain twin printed inside `samples/I1.md`. The twins inside `IV3.md` and `IV4.md` run at 100% of their Book leaves and follow them paragraph for paragraph, so redo them to this section before anyone copies them.*

### 11.1 What it is

It is the same tale, told today. The Book is hard on purpose and the Plain is easy on purpose. Someone who has never opened the Book should be able to read it straight through and never stop on a word: a twelve-year-old, a tired player between assaults, or someone reading in a second language. Switching tabs should feel like hearing the same person tell the same story a few centuries later. The events, the people and the voice stay the same. Only the English changes.

### 11.2 The English: modern and everyday

- **Use common words.** If a twelve-year-old might stop on a word, change it, or gloss it once in passing. Some changes from I.1:
  - *ward* becomes *yard*;
  - *capstones* becomes *the wall-top*;
  - *lintel* becomes *the stone over the stair*;
  - *riven* becomes *split*;
  - *crimson* becomes *red*;
  - stones that *move* become stones that are *loose*.
- **Keep normal word order: subject, verb, object.** Don't invert anything. That rules out *Then laughed his men*, *Then came Harl*, *Last was me* and *Twelve were we*. Ordinary openings are fine: *From the gate I counted…*, *There were…*, *Here's…*.
- **No archaism at all.**
  - None of the Book's stock words (§2.1).
  - No *thou*, *thee* or *ye*, and no *-eth* or *-est* endings.
  - Don't use *for* to mean *because*, *that* for a person, *but* to mean *only*, or *yet* to mean *still*.
  - Don't make a negative without *do*: *he knew not* becomes *he didn't know*.
  - Talk to the hall as *you*.
- **Use contractions** wherever a speaker would, at 10 to 35 per 1,000 words. Keep the full form only where the stress falls on the *not*, as in *I did not stop them.*
- **Write short sentences that flow.**
  - Average 10 to 14 words a sentence.
  - About a third of sentences should be under 10 words. A curt teller (Kael, Brenn, Hale) can go up to 40%.
  - No sentence over 30 words, and only a plain list over 25.
  - Join sentences with *and*, *but*, *so*, *because* and *when*.
  - No semicolons. Use two or three colons in a tale at most.
  - Never put three short sentences in a row when none of them carries weight. Join two of them with the word that links them.
  - Keep the Flesch-Kincaid grade at 4 or under.
- **No slang and no modern jargon.** That means no *okay*, *yeah*, *kids*, *guys*, *stuff*, *focus*, *process*, *trauma* or *closure*. The Plain is today's English, but not today's chatter.
- **Keep British spelling**, as the Book does: *grey*, *colour*, *harbour*.

### 11.3 A retelling, not a paraphrase

**How to write it:**
1. List the Book leaf's beats. That means every event, fact, name and number, every line of speech, and every picture that carries weight.
2. Close the Book.
3. Tell the tale from the list, the way this teller would tell it today to a hall of tired people. Use your own sentences and your own paragraphs.
4. Check against the list that nothing is lost and nothing is added.
5. Check the fixed matter word for word (§11.5).

**The rules:**
- **Give it its own paragraphs and sentences.** Never follow the Book sentence by sentence or paragraph by paragraph. A modern teller keeps going where the Book pauses, so expect about a quarter fewer paragraphs than the Book.
  - Only the strike, the turn and the great closing lines stand alone.
  - Merge what a speaker would say in one breath. In I.1, the column and its count become one paragraph, *Some shook. Some wept. Some fell…* becomes one sentence, and *Twelve.* rides at the end of the roll.
  - Keep the order of events, but within a passage put each fact where a modern reader needs it. In I.1 the coal comes into the Captain's hand before he climbs.
- **Say what a figure means, and let its shape go.**
  - Seren's shapes belong to the Book: the look by three, the mirror, the triple with its third part broken, and inversion for weight. The Plain keeps what they say. A shape survives only where plain English carries it without strain, as in *The True Men looked at the Captain, and the Captain was looking at the wall.*
  - Turn a turn of phrase into its plain meaning. In I.1, *with the whole of the winter in its mouth* becomes *a bitter wind*.
  - Keep one or two of the Book's own pictures where they carry the tale, in plain words: *river ice cracking up in spring*. Add no pictures of your own.
  - Never write *Picture a…*, *Think of…* or *Imagine…*.
- **Don't explain the Book's wordplay.** The play on *tell* and *told* (to count and to recount) can't come across, so Kael simply *counts*. Where modern English has an easy play of its own at the same spot, use it: *A count doesn't count until it has names.*
- **Gloss once, in passing, the first time it's needed**, folded into the sentence: *the Stonewrights, our builders*. Never write a separate sentence to define a word, and never repeat a gloss in the same tale.
- **Leave dark whatever the Book leaves dark.** The Plain is easier, not more explained. Add no reason, motive, feeling, custom or lore. It may make plain what a sentence *says*, but never what a deed *means*.
- **Test the overlap.** No more than 20% of the Plain's four-word runs may appear in the Book (15% for prose alone). A paragraph that keeps failing is being translated: close the Book and tell it again.

### 11.4 Keep everything

- **Keep every beat in order**, and every fact, name, number and teller.
- **Spell names exactly as the Book does**: *Marl the fen-born*, not *Marl of the fens*.
- **Pair-names stay under the lintel**: *{{Halyna}}*, always with plural verbs (*{{Halyna}} are*, *{{Halyna}} say*). In their own mouths it is *we two*.
- **▒▒▒▒ stays a blot** everywhere the Book blots the name.
- **Never use a singular *they*** for one person. Write *whoever*, *anyone who* or *a man who*.
- **Every lock in §10 item 10 holds in the Plain too.**
  - The cross-sign is *half of something, cut off in the middle*, never a holy sign.
  - No Christian marks.
  - Brenn only watched.
  - There is no custom of burning.
  - The Captain stays unnamed.
- **Put the markers where the Book puts them:**
  - `PROLOGUE EXCERPT` (the Plain excerpt must also make sense on its own);
  - `HALYNA`;
  - `READING`;
  - `RING`;
  - `FACING LEAF`;
  - every `::: native` block. Inside a native block, the Orrowen line and the Title never change. The caption and the gloss line may be put in plain words.

### 11.5 Fixed matter

**Word for word, exactly as in the Book.** Don't modernize these even where they sound old:
- the Title, *In memory of our home, our families, our God, our freedoms, our peace.*, and its capitals;
- *The mortar cries out from the ground. Rise, Stonewrights! Lay stone upon stone. Stand every wall to the very end.*;
- *Rise! Rise! Rise!*;
- *We remember our home too.*;
- *We remember.*;
- *But war is for the short-minded and the angry young.*;
- *When the left hand dies, does not the right one die too?* (it stays as it is);
- the Book Five line;
- *Stop… Sky… Dying…*;
- the warden's letter;
- the runners' oath, and *Not as Brenn carried.*;
- *This is held in the grain.*;
- *And no one answered.*

**The liturgy, in its Plain form:**
- *I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.* (or *We have laid this tale the way it was laid for us.*)
- *And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*
- *That's all he came to say. We have carried it a long way, and the grain still holds it.*
- The Guest's sentence keeps the Plain wording it already has, as in `IV4.md`.

**Songs.** Never change the Book's song.
- Where the Plain already has a verse for a song, as in v2.0's Plain, keep it word for word. I.1 keeps *The gate was broken. The lords had run…*.
- Where it has none, write a plain short verse line for line: the same number of lines, everyday words, and no forced rhyme.
- A song too knotted for plain verse may become one or two plain sentences in italics that say what it says.
- The frame line around a song is plain: *Then the True Men sang, as they still do:*.

### 11.6 The voice: warm and clear

- **The teller is still the teller**, speaking today's English.
  - Kael counts, corrects and swears, and his jokes are dry: *I counted twice, and it didn't take long.*
  - Brenn confesses in short, guilty sentences.
  - Voss asks, and circles back to his question.
  - Hale runs in the present tense.
  - Corlen says little.
  - Seren writes a little fuller, with one clear picture.
  - {{Halyna}} speak in halves.
- **Warm** means a person talking to people he knows. He says *you* to the hall, speaks to the children at the front, and points at the bench. It does not mean added feeling.
  - No verdict words: *cruel*, *brave*, *amazing*, *tragic*.
  - No exclamation marks outside speech and fixed lines.
  - No *suddenly*.
- **Clear** means one thing to a sentence, the concrete noun, and the name again once *he* has gone a paragraph without one.
- **It still builds.** Keep the same shape in plain words: the breath before the strike, the strike alone in a short paragraph, then the small aftermath. In I.1 that is *The yard went quiet…* then *He tore the cloak in two, down the back.* then *Old cloth makes only a small sound when it rips*.
  - The Book's great lines that are already plain English stand alone just as they do there: *The other half is on his back.* · *It was like that.*
  - Explain nothing at the peak.

### 11.7 The wood leaf in Plain

- **The reading stays in fragments**, in modern words: *Leaves? No — words, like leaves.* Keep, from the Book's reading:
  - the same number of lines, give or take one;
  - the same mistake corrected and the same slip caught;
  - the same thought broken across the two inks;
  - the same gap, left open;
  - the same stopping point.
- **The story in the wood's *we* is the plainest voice of all.** Use short words, slow sentences and few contractions. The wood still knows only what the wood could know.
- **The rings and the return** follow §11.5.

### 11.8 The frame

- Keep the same heading, and leave out the dateline.
- **The headnote** is one or two plain sentences, 45 words at most. It keeps the Book headnote's facts and its human touch. In I.1, Kael forbids any change, Seren changes plenty, and Kael swears it is exactly what he said.

### 11.9 Length

- **The Plain's tale proper runs 70–85% of its Book leaf's**, counted the same way: everything after the headnote through the hearth's answer, songs included, native blocks left out. Most leaves will land at 75–85%.
- **Cut the joints, never a beat.** Drop the Book's shapes, say each thing once, and merge what a speaker would say in one breath. If a leaf can't get under 85% with every beat kept, it is still following the Book's sentences, so retell it again.
- **Fixed matter can't shrink**, so a leaf heavy with it sits near the top of the band. I.1 carries a roll of twelve names, the Title, a song and the liturgy, and it lands at 84.7%.

### 11.12 Before you hand in a Plain leaf

1. Is every item on the beat list ticked, with nothing added?
2. Is the checker clean, and has every flag been looked at?
3. Read it as a twelve-year-old would. Did you stop on a word anywhere, or read a sentence twice?
4. Lay it beside the Book. Does any paragraph follow the Book sentence by sentence? If so, tell it again.
5. Is it the same teller? Does it still build to the strike?
6. Are the fixed matter, the pair-names, the blots and the markers exact?
7. Would a child at the front of the hall lean in?
