# The Legends, v3: three sample tales

*These are samples of the new direction. They are written in the v1.3 voice, made hard through old English, and kept short. The full Book follows the same rules.*

## I.1 · THE BOOK

### I.1 · The Torn Cloak

*The first winter, the night after the Rite.*

*Laid by Kael the Counter, on the first night this hearth asked a tale. He forbade me to change one word of it; I changed a good many, and when I read it him, he sware it was word for word as he had told it.*

Give me the stone. No, I will stand. Enough have I sat this winter.

By the lintel of the western stair I swear it, where the old wardens cut their names. A teller am I, and no singer. Heads I tell, and stones, and days. What I told not, that will I not tell.

At dusk the column came in under the outer gate. I heard it ere I saw it: many feet, and no voice among them. From the gate I told it, head by head.

Old men came, and women with bundles, and children asleep on their feet, and soldiers of six broken companies, that knew their officers no more. Last came the Captain, that had kept the rear the whole road. Two thousand and some hundreds, all told.

A month had we walked from the ashes of Glasspire to come to a castle. We came to a heap of stones. The gate hung by one hinge, and in the ward the grass stood to the knee. Where the songs had set a great hall, the roof was fallen in, and snow lay on the hearth where ye sit now.

Some shook. Some wept. Some fell, and would not rise.

Then began the crying, and it was not one cry. Some cried that we should beg terms of the grey sails, though no man living ever understood one word they spake. Some cried for the high passes, and the snow beyond them. Some cried that we should go down into the cellars, and bar the doors, and bide, as if the sea would forget us.

Men had I heard break, on the long retreat. Never till that dusk a people. It is a sound like ice going out on a river.

Down off the riven ridge came the wind, with the whole of the winter in its mouth.

The people looked for a general, and the generals were slain. They looked for the lords, and the lords were scattered. They looked to the Stonewrights, and the Stonewrights sat in the grass with their chisels in their laps.

To the Captain looked none of them. Captain was he of a small company, and we were his company. All the long road he had waited for one whose place it was to lead, and none came, for they were dead. Mistake me not: there was no greater man, and his equal, I think, never lived. But he sought not to lead. I swear to that.

The True Men looked to their Captain. The Captain looked at the wall.

Then he climbed it. In the hall he had taken a coal from the dead hearth, this hearth, and why, none of us wist. Alone he went up the broken face, by the stones that stood proud of the mortar. We stood at the foot and let him, for he had not bidden us follow. I have told those stones since: thirty-one there are, and four of them move.

At the top he took off his cloak. Crimson it was once, ere the road wore it to brick. In that wind it was all he had.

No man in the ward spake. Among the women a child coughed, and his mother hushed him. After that there was naught but the wind.

He tore it down the back.

A small sound it made, the sound of old cloth giving. Over the wind I heard it.

The one half he spread on the capstones, and kneeled on it with the coal, and wrote. Slowly he wrote, as men write that have written little. That half he bound to a pike out of the rubble. The other half, whereon naught was written, he put back on his shoulders.

First up after him was I, and first to read it. Not once had his hand shaken. I turned to the ward and read it out, and my voice was not steady, and I will not feign it was.

*[The Title, as he wrote it in coal on the torn cloak: the course-hand: *Ul dumol ol varn, ol theldeth, ol Mardh, ol lunnath, ol sollan.* In memory of our home, our families, our God, our freedoms, our peace.]*

**IN MEMORY OF OUR HOME, OUR FAMILIES, OUR GOD, OUR FREEDOMS, OUR PEACE.**

He set the pike in a cleft of the wall and gave it to the wind, and the wind took it. That was the last unbroken wall of the Shorelands, and that was the banner over it.

Now hear who answered, and in what order, for I told them. A count is no count till it be named.

First the True Men. One by one he had found us, in garrisons and on roads, and bound us with naught but trust. We kept our oaths, and built what we promised, and fought never but in a just cause. That is the whole of a True Man. I say it that ye may know it is not much. Nor is it little.

Stannard of the ridge. Corlen of the harbour. Tamm of the three spans. Marl the fen-born. Aske of the canopy. Garvel of the smithies. Harl of the burning shore. Ulden of the galleries. Hesk of the mere. Lanner of the crags. Pellow of the spire. And Kael the Counter, their oath-keeper, of no haven, for he found me on a road.

Twelve. I counted us twice, and it took not long.

After us answered the six companies, that are our troops now, a troop to each True Man; and they trust us as we trust him. Then answered the people of the column, that bare their children on their backs through the passes. One voice. A dozen. Then hundreds, till I could tell them no more.

I stopped. It is the only time I ever stopped.

He gave no command. He had but written down what we had left, and it was enough.

Crimson is the pennant on every keep of ours. It is the colour of that cloak.

Look to the end of the bench, ye children at the front. There he sitteth, with his hood up.

On his back is the other half.

He will tell you it was not like that. It was like that.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*Then the True Men sang, as they sing it still:*

> *The gate was broken, the lords were scattered,*  
> *the grey sails gathered along the shore;*  
> *one man climbed where the stones lay loosened*  
> *and tore the cloak his shoulders wore.*  
> *He wrote five things a man might die for,*  
> *and none of us has needed more.*

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

> *On the leaf that faceth it there is naught but the grain of the wood, and one line beneath:*
>
> *[The wood's leaf, not yet known: Nothing is given here: these are the Stone's own rings, and no mark is cut in them yet.]*
>
> *[Seren's note, in the course-hand: Untold. Halyna cannot yet hold this deep.]*

## I.1 · PLAIN WORDS

### I.1 · The Torn Cloak

*Kael the Counter told this on the first night the hearth asked for a story, and he told me not to change a word. I changed plenty, and when I read it back to him, he swore it was exactly what he'd said.*

Give me the stone. No, I'll stand. I've sat long enough this winter.

I swear on the stone over the western stair, where the wardens' names are cut. I'm no singer. I count heads, stones and days, and what I didn't count, I won't tell.

The column came in at dusk, and I heard it before I saw it: many feet, and not one voice. From the gate I counted old men, women with bundles, children asleep on their feet, and soldiers from six broken companies who no longer knew their officers. The Captain came last, because he'd guarded the rear. Two thousand and a few hundred in all.

We'd walked a month from burnt-out Glasspire to reach a castle and found rubble. The gate hung off one hinge and the yard was knee-deep in grass. The great hall from the songs had no roof, and snow lay on the hearth, right where you're sitting.

Some shook, some cried, and some fell down and wouldn't get up. Then the shouting started. Some wanted to beg the grey sails for peace, though nobody understood a word they said. Others wanted to flee over the high passes into the snow, or hide in the cellars, as if the sea would forget us.

I'd heard soldiers break on the long retreat, but never a whole people. It sounds like river ice cracking up in spring.

A bitter wind came down off the split ridge. People wanted a leader, but the generals were dead, the lords had scattered, and even the Stonewrights, our builders, sat in the grass holding their chisels.

Nobody looked at the Captain. He only led one small company, and that was us. All the way here he'd waited for the real leaders to take over, but they were dead. Don't get me wrong, there was never a better man. He just didn't want to lead, and I swear to that.

The True Men looked at the Captain, and the Captain was looking at the wall.

He had a dead coal from this hearth in his hand, and nobody knew why. He climbed alone, using the stones that stuck out, and we let him, since he hadn't asked us to follow. I've counted them since: thirty-one stones, four of them loose.

On top he took off his cloak. It had been red once, before the road faded it to the colour of brick, and it was all he had against that wind. The yard went quiet. A little boy coughed, his mother shushed him, and then there was only the wind.

He tore the cloak in two, down the back.

Old cloth makes only a small sound when it rips, but I heard it over the wind.

He spread one half on the wall-top, knelt on it, and wrote with the coal, slowly, like a man not used to writing. He tied it to an old pike and put the blank half back on his shoulders.

I was next up, so I read it first. His hand hadn't shaken once, but my voice shook when I turned and read it out to the yard. I won't pretend it didn't.

*[The Title, as he wrote it in coal on the torn cloak, in the course-hand: *Ul dumol ol varn, ol theldeth, ol Mardh, ol lunnath, ol sollan.* In memory of our home, our families, our God, our freedoms, our peace.]*

**IN MEMORY OF OUR HOME, OUR FAMILIES, OUR GOD, OUR FREEDOMS, OUR PEACE.**

He jammed the pike into a crack and let the wind take it. That was the last whole wall in the Shorelands, and that cloak was its banner.

I counted who answered, and in what order. A count doesn't count until it has names. The True Men answered first. The Captain had found us one by one, in forts and on roads, and held us with nothing but trust. We kept our word, built what we promised, and fought only for a just cause. That's all a True Man is: not much, but not nothing.

Stannard of the ridge, Corlen of the harbour, Tamm of the three spans, Marl the fen-born. Aske of the canopy, Garvel of the smithies, Harl of the burning shore, Ulden of the galleries. Hesk of the mere, Lanner of the crags, Pellow of the spire. And me, Kael the Counter, the oath-keeper, from nowhere at all. The Captain found me on a road. Twelve of us. I counted twice, and it didn't take long.

Next the six companies answered. They're our troops now, one to each True Man, and they trust us as we trust him. Then the rest of the column, who'd carried their children through the passes on their backs. One voice, then a dozen, then hundreds, more than I could count. So I stopped counting, the only time I ever have.

He never gave an order. He only wrote down what we still had, and it was enough. That's why all our keeps fly red pennants, the colour of that cloak.

Look at the end of the bench, you children in front. That's him, with his hood up.

The other half is on his back.

He'll tell you it wasn't like that. It was like that.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*Then the True Men sang, as they still do:*

> *The gate was broken. The lords had run.*  
> *Grey sails were gathering all along the shore.*  
> *One man climbed up the loose stones*  
> *and tore the cloak he was wearing.*  
> *He wrote down five things worth dying for,*  
> *and none of us has ever needed more.*

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

> *The page facing it shows nothing but wood grain, and one line underneath:*
>
> *[The wood's page, not known yet: Nothing is given here. These are the Stone's own rings, and nothing has been cut into them yet.]*
>
> *[Seren's note, in the course-hand: Not told yet. This is still too deep for Halyna to hold.]*

## IV.3 · THE BOOK

### IV.3 · The Warden's Door

*The third winter, in the Tide of Remembering.*

*The words of Brenn of the outpost, as Wendhessa of the guild at Eldhythe took them from his mouth in his old age. Halyna read them at this hearth, and the annal after them is mine.*

*[The mark of Wendhessa, the archivists of Eldhythe, on the seal of Brenn's bundle, as it was broken]*

"We two hold the stone,"

"but the words are not ours. As they were written, so we read them."

Nineteen I was. Twelve were we, and the warden, at the last post of the land, where the rock runneth out toward the grey. I was its runner, sworn at fifteen with mine hand flat on the runners' stone: "What I saw, I carry. I carry nothing else."

Black wood had ▒▒▒▒ set everywhere: in the doorposts, in his table, in the frame of his bed. He held the post by his father's name, and was scarce older than his runner. For his temper they had sent him to the edge, where there were but the twelve of us for it to wound. He had never known battle. He had never known patience.

One morning at summer's end, out of the grey came a small grey boat. There was no wind, and the boat came anyway.

The stranger beached it, and walked up to the warden's door. ▒▒▒▒ stood within, and came no further. Tall was the stranger, and pale, and clad in a mist-cloth that moved when he moved not. No weapon saw I on him.

First he spake in his own tongue, loud, like a storm-wind in old trees, and we were afraid. We wist not that it is but how they speak. Then said he three words of ours, carefully, as a man setteth down a thing that might break.

"Stop… Sky… Dying…"

They were the right words, and truly spoken. I know it now. Yet words without sentences are but sounds, and we heard but sounds.

Then kneeled he on the step, and set before the warden a dark stone, the size of two fists. He set it down as ye would set down bread before a guest. No man of us moved. Behind him a gull came down, and walked among the stones.

▒▒▒▒ kicked it aside.

Into the dust by the wall it went. Then laughed his men. I stood among them, and I hear that laughter yet.

The stranger rose. His right hand he laid on the back of the warden's neck, gently, as my mother did when I had a fever. He drew their heads together till the foreheads touched, and was silent. ▒▒▒▒ stood and let it be done. Had it stopped there, I think all our lives had gone otherwise.

Then took the stranger a little blade in his left hand, and touched it to the warden's right arm.

▒▒▒▒ tore himself away. Never had he had a wound. In the tearing, the cut that should have been shallow went deep, and the blood came.

"Seize him," he screamed.

They seized him. He fought them not. The blade was yet in his hand, turned to his own arm. Between their hands he went down on his knees again, where he had kneeled with his gift. I understood it not.

"Kill him," screamed ▒▒▒▒.

They took up what lay to hand: staves, and stones of the shingle, and the boat-poles. One of them took up my pole, the pole I pushed the boats off with.

He lifted not his hand. If he cried out, it was in his own tongue, and I took it for the wind. The blows I counted. I count them yet. When he lay still, ▒▒▒▒ screamed on, and some went on. Both his arms they broke: the one that had held the warden's neck so gently, and the one that had held the blade.

His men obeyed.

We obeyed.

I say *we*, for the post was one thing that day, and I was of it. No hand laid I on him. I did not stop them. A boy was I among the men.

Then was it quiet, and the gull was gone.

By the heels they drew him down the shingle, and his head went on every stone. Into his own boat they cast him, as a fisher casteth a torn net into the bilge.

▒▒▒▒ held his arm, and looked not at the boat.

"Burn it," said he, "and shove it off, and give it the tide. Leave naught to show."

They set it alight, and it burned not as wood burneth. There was a screaming in it. The stranger was dead. It was no man that screamed. It was the wood itself.

With the same poles they shoved it off. Old Tobe went into the sea to his knees and washed the poles. The rest of us stood on the shingle, and would not look, and looked.

Well out, the boat turned. Into the wind it went, and against the current, and into the grey, burning.

That same day ▒▒▒▒ had the clerk write to the Council. I stood by, and I can say it by heart:

*Unknown intruder. Hostile intent. Neutralized. Vessel burned and released.*

No word of the gift. No word of the kneeling.

On the morrow, ere any woke, I picked the stone out of the dust. Why I did it, I know not. At his door ▒▒▒▒ gave me the letter. So the gift and the lie went into one pack, and I ran it.

A day and a night I ran, and every mile I said the oath over.

At Eldhythe an old clerk of the Council read it, with ink on his fingers, and looked up at me.

"Is it so?"

I said not that it was so. I said, "It is written." Forty years have I weighed that small cleverness.

I carried the lie in a day and a night. I carried the truth forty years, and gave it to no one.

That autumn the shore laughed at three axemen, home on a spar, that sware to a burning boat out of the grey. I laughed not, for I had seen it turn. The Council had it in writing.

▒▒▒▒ wore long sleeves the rest of his life, through the hot summers of Eldhythe, and I know why. Last winter he died in his bed of black wood. I am the last of the twelve.

Forty years the stone hath lain at the bottom of my pack, and not once have I held it. I give it up now with these words, and I ask God to forgive me, for there is none left to ask but God.

So far the words of Brenn. At every blot Halyna stopped, and ye heard a silence in the place of the name.

The warden's arm bare one mark: the half of something, cut off in the middle.

Brenn died within the year, as a man setteth down a load. The guild buried him under his name. The warden's name it cut away.

Wendhessa sealed his words, and told no one. When the havens fell they bare the chest out, his hand on one handle and hers on the other. On the Road their arms failed. The masons said it held naught of worth, and gave it to the youngest, Tarnel and me. Tarnel knotted his net with his free hand. I watched the knots.

On the night of the pass Wendhessa lay down under one cloak. At morning frost lay on both handles, and neither rose.

Tarnel did not wake.

There I stopped tonight, with my hands on the stone. Then Halyna laid theirs beside them.

"She carried it every step,"

"and after the passes she carried it alone."

There is no older law among any people than this: that whoso kneeleth at your door is under your roof. We were a people of doors.

This we lay as it was laid for us.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

*Never before had this hearth said it over a killing by our own. Old Ebba began it, and the rest were slow to follow. But they followed.*

*And the Captain put his hood back.*

*In the morning the runners sware their oath again, and added four words: Not as Brenn carried.*

## IV.4 · THE BOOK

### IV.4 · The Gift Held an Hour

*The third spring, in the Tide of Remembering.*

*On the night Brenn was read, a child asked what the Guest had given. This spring Rhyna held it an hour, and it came into her slow, as water over stone; Halvard, across the ward at the mortar, touched it not, and it was he that began the telling.*

*— Old. Dark, and old. Stone — no. Not stone. A tree, once.*  
*— Hands, warm, a long while. Leaves. No — words, like leaves. One, and one, and one.*  
*— Three. Three words in him —*  
*— and naught to join them. We two have it.*  
*— A door. Our — the wood's kin, in the posts. Sawn. They answer not.*  
*— Kneeling. Set down. His best. A foot.*  
*— Dust. Laughter, through the dust.*  
*— A neck, held. Two brows, one —*  
*— the blade — [ ]*

This is held in the grain.

Eldest wood are we. A tree we were when the world was green to its edges. We fell, and the ages lay down on us, leaf on leaf, till time forgat us and we grew dark and hard. Ye took us for stone. We are not stone.

Wood that time forgat forgetteth not. Slow is its giving, and true. Of all the gifts the long-lived give, the dearest is a sliver of such wood. We were given once.

Aelrhen he was named when he took his errand: bond-to-stone. A long life he listened across the breath, to the shore-men at their nets and at their walls. He gathered your words as a child gathereth fallen leaves, one and one and one. At the end he had three, and knew them for the right ones. Join them he could not. Ye join your words with small words, and small words make no sound across water.

But he had us. Whoso held us while a tree's shadow moved the breadth of its own trunk would know the whole of it. We were the sentence.

He chose the outermost of your holds, for its doors and its benches were of our wood. He took that for reverence. We knew it for what it was: our kin from the edge, sawn and polished. At the door he laid his hand on the post and greeted them. They answered not. They were dead.

He kneeled on the stones before the master of that house, and set us down. They that come under a roof bring their best, and we were his best. In the thunder of his own tongue he spake the name of the rite, *Ael'thar*, and after it his three words, in yours.

"Stop… Sky… Dying…"

A foot struck us into the dust by the wall. Laughter came to us through the dust.

He stopped not.

He rose, and took the back of the host's neck in his right hand, there where life goeth up into thought. A fledgling could have slept in that hand. The foreheads met, thought to thought, and he was silent.

With his left hand he set the blade to the host's arm.

And the host drew back. We know not why. Fear leaveth no mark on wood.

The blade that was to go a little way went deep, for the arm was moving. The host cried out, and his men came.

Then Aelrhen's blood ran over the stones of the step to where we lay, and with it came all of him: his long listening, and his last hour. So we know it as he knew it.

Between their hands he went down on his knees again, there where the rite began. He knew the blows as trees know the iron: one, and another. He lifted not his hand. The cut he would have made on his own arm was never made. He would ask no blood that he gave not. He gave it all, there on the step.

Never were the two arms laid together, shoulder to shoulder, that harm to the one should be harm to both. He died there, with the rite half made.

We lay in the dust and heard all of it, and no hand took us up. Of all things that ever lay at a shore-man's feet and looked like stone, we were the one that could have spoken to him.

And we were the one his foot found.

In the morning a hand took us out of the dust, and put us away, and held us not.

Then a jolting in the dark, a day and a night. Then the dark, half a life, and never a hand. Then leaves about us, and cut stones cold beside us, and another dark as long. Then old arms, two pairs, slow, a long while; and fire, near and far. Then small arms, two pairs, going up, in the cold.

Then one pair, a long way.

None held us long, till thou heldest us while a tree's shadow moved the breadth of its own trunk.

Here is the sentence, as it was brought to the door:

*Stop the felling of the pillars at the edge of the grey, for they are not timber. The sky over us is their breath, and ours, and when you cut them it comes open, and our children cannot live in the open sun. We are here, behind the grey, and we have been here always, and we are not your enemies. Stop, for the sky is dying, and we are dying under it.*

That is all he came to say. We have carried it a long way, and the grain holds it still.

*And no one answered.*
