# R1 · How Tolkien Tells a Tale

*Working research for the rewrite of the Book of the Riven Stone. Not Book text. The sample lines in Rivenkeep idiom are original and only show the method.*

---

## 0 · Jack's complaint, in Tolkien's own terms

Jack's notes on v2.0 name three faults: "run on sentences", tales that "don't build", and the feel of a "history text book". Tolkien diagnosed all three himself.

- **Run-on is not archaic.** In his unsent 1955 draft to Hugh Brogan (Letter 171), Tolkien says real archaic English is "far more *terse* than modern", and that modern idiom is the slack one. v2.0 tried to sound old by being long. Tolkien sounds old through word order, through choosing native words over borrowed ones, and through cutting.
- **Clinical means annal with no scene.** Scholars describe *The Silmarillion* as compressed and annal-like, with no hobbit to bring the reader close (Shippey; "Tolkien's prose style", survey). When Tolkien wants tears, he stops summarising. He stands in one place, at one hour, beside one person. v2.0 stays at annal distance throughout.
- **Lore that explains a deed weakens it.** Even a mythical age needs "some enigmas" (Letter 144). The depth of *The Lord of the Rings* comes from "glimpses of a large history" left unexplained (Letter 247). Jack's verdict on the burning custom is this same principle.

**Citation note.** The brief credits "Drout, Hitotsubashi and Scavera" with "Tolkien's Prose Style…" (*Tolkien Studies* 1, 2004). That article is by Michael Drout alone (pp. 137–63). It reads the Éowyn and Denethor passages against *King Lear*. It argues that grammar, syntax, diction and sound work together for "communicative economy". The three authors together wrote "Tolkien's Creation of the Impression of Depth" (*TS* 11, 2014). Both are paywalled; I used abstracts and summaries.

## 1 · Seventeen techniques

### 1.1 Old by order and word-stock, never by length

**What it is.** Steve Walker finds few true archaisms in *The Lord of the Rings*, yet the diction still feels old (*The Power of Tolkien's Prose*, 2009). The Rohirrim are given Old English syntax and compounds (Walker; Shippey). Rosebury shows Merry's stroke at the Witch-king beginning in plain syntax. One old word (*heed* for *notice*) is enough to tilt it (*Tolkien: A Cultural Phenomenon*, 2003).

**Tolkien.** He recast Théoden's speech in modern idiom ("Not at all, my dear Gandalf…"). The king's closing thought, that he shall sleep the better, then has no modern words to live in. The result is a "disunion of word and meaning" (Letter 171).

**For the Book.** Get the difficulty from grammar, and keep the sentences the same length:
- **Verb-second after an adverb:** *Then came the stranger.*
- **Object or complement first:** *Twelve were we, and the warden.*
- **Old negation and question:** *I knew not why. Saw ye it?*
- **A sparing old word-stock:** *ere, wist, naught, bade, wont, sore, yonder.*
- **The old subjunctive:** *Were it to do again…*

**Test.** A sentence of the Book may be harder than its Plain Words twin, but never longer. Kael, made old:

> *Twelve were we. Twice I counted us, and it took not long.*

### 1.2 Every voice its own idiom

**What it is.** Tolkien fits idiom to people. Shippey shows the hobbits, with their modern talk, carrying the reader from our world into the heroic one (*Road to Middle-earth*). Kullmann and Siepmann count eight speech-styles in the Fellowship alone. Sam is rustic and metaphorical. Aragorn inverts his sentences. Gandalf speaks plainly and gives orders (*Tolkien as a Literary Artist*, 2021).

**Tolkien.** At the Council of Elrond each speaker reports in his own register: Glóin is blunt, Boromir speaks the verse of his dream, Bilbo makes fussy asides.

**For the Book.** Seren improves each tale, and each teller's grain must still show through her ink:
- **Kael** counts, swears by things, and corrects.
- **Hale** speaks in short breaths, always in motion.
- **Voss** asks questions, and tells things second-hand.
- **Brenn** weighs. He circles the same small facts.
- **Halyna** split each line between the two of them.

Her own gift is the turn of phrase and the painted picture. Each teller should hear himself and feel *bettered*, not replaced.

### 1.3 Opening: an hour, a place, one sense, one seed

**What it is.** Tolkien opens on a body in a place at a time of day, with one sense and a faint wrongness. He does not open on an idea.

**Tolkien.** "The Ride of the Rohirrim" begins with Merry lying wrapped in the dark, hearing drums he cannot place. A small frightened listener comes before the great charge. "Of Beren and Lúthien" narrows from the annal of Beren's outlawry to one glade at evening.

**For the Book.** The hearth formula ("Give me the stone") is the *Hwæt!*: the hall's call to listen. Keep it. The first sentence of *story* after it must carry an hour, a place, a sense and a seed. v1.3's best openings already do: *The column came in at dusk.* IV.3: *There was no wind, and the boat came anyway.* That eleven-word sentence is the model for the whole Book.

### 1.4 The telescope: summary, one halt, summary

**What it is.** Tolkien covers years in a sentence, then stops dead on one exchange or one gesture. The change of speed is what makes the moment matter.

**Tolkien.** Beren's wanderings take a few lines. His meeting with Lúthien, and his answer before Thingol, take whole paragraphs. Days of riding to Gondor pass in summary, and then the last minutes before the charge are told breath by breath.

**For the Book.** One halt per tale. Brisk before it, quick and quiet after.

| Tale | Told fast | The halt |
|---|---|---|
| I.1 | the march and the ruin | the climb and the coal |
| IV.3 | the post and the warden | the kneeling |
| V.7 | four winters of Enno and Della, in two sentences | the mallet stopping in the air |

v2.0 halted everywhere, so never: one speed, like a textbook.

### 1.5 Plant without comment, pay without explaining

**What it is.** Small objects carry the plot. They are set down early with no stress and come back at the hinge.

**Tolkien.** Galadriel's phial and the Elven rope arrive as ordinary-seeming gifts. Pippin's dropped brooch tells Aragorn the hobbits are alive.

**For the Book.**
- **I.1:** Kael says the Captain *had taken a coal from the dead hearth… and I did not know why*. That is a perfect plant. The coal writes the Title. Add no gloss.
- **IV.3:** the stone kicked into the dust is paid when Brenn picks it up, and paid again when a child carries it up the mountain. Never give his reason. *I do not know why I did it* is stronger than any reason.
- **V.7:** the house of one door, one course high, is paid by never rising higher.

### 1.6 Love before the fall: the homely promise

**What it is.** Tolkien makes us love someone through small generous acts, often a promise about ordinary days. Then the death breaks the promise.

**Tolkien.** Théoden had promised to sit with Merry at Meduseld and hear his herb-lore. Dying, he grieves that he never will, and tells Merry to remember him over his pipe.

**For the Book.**
- **The Guest** is loved in three strokes, all in v1.3:
  - his words, set down *like something that might break*;
  - the stone, set down *as you would set down bread before a guest*;
  - his hand on the neck, *the way my mother did when I had a fever*.

  Cut anything that competes with these.
- **Enno and Della** need only the house and their jest: *It is like being one person… with four hands and no excuses.* When the wall-masters repeat the jest over the stone, that is Théoden's move. The homely line returns as epitaph.

### 1.7 Doom spoken early

**What it is.** Tell the end, or its shadow, near the start. Every hopeful step then reads under it, and suspense becomes dread.

**Tolkien.** Morgoth curses Húrin's kin before Túrin's tale is told. Ulmo warns Turgon of treason within his walls. Gondolin then falls on its feast-night, at the height of its joy.

**For the Book.**
- **IV.3:** *I laughed. I have not laughed at anything since.* This stands inside the scene and reaches forty years ahead. Keep it at the kick, and only there.
- **V.7:** give Seren one line on the night before: *One course high it was, and higher it stood never.* Then tell the morning as if she did not know.
- **I.1:** Kael's frame is doom turned to joy: *He will tell you it was not like that. It was like that.*

### 1.8 The witness who cannot act

**What it is.** Some of Tolkien's hardest scenes are seen by someone powerless to change them.

**Tolkien.** Húrin is set in a chair on Thangorodrim and made to watch his children's ruin. Sam hears the orcs say Frodo lives, and cannot reach him. Merry kneels by Théoden, ashamed that he could not save him.

**For the Book: IV.3, the corrected Brenn.** He struck no one and seized no one. He was a boy among the men. His guilt is the witness's guilt: he laughed, he watched, he did not cry out. So the scene fills with what a boy who did nothing would remember forever: the gull on the shingle, the poles, the Guest going down on his knees again between the men's hands. His *we* is the post, and he should say so once:

> *They seized him. My hands hung at my sides. ▒▒▒▒ screamed for the blow, and the men gave it, and I stood by the wall where the stone lay. A boy was I among them. Naught did I. We obeyed: I say we, for the post was one thing that day, and I was of it.*

Tolkien praised the Maldon retainers' "heroism of obedience and love". They died for a lord whose pride had doomed them (*Ofermod*, 1953). The warden's door turns that virtue inside out: obedience with no love, to a lord with no worth. That is why *We obeyed* is the worst line in the tale.

### 1.9 The rejected gesture

**What it is.** A slow, tender approach is told closely. One rough act ends it, in one short sentence.

**Tolkien.** On the stairs of Cirith Ungol, Gollum reaches out to touch the sleeping Frodo, almost a caress. Then Sam wakes and speaks roughly. Tolkien called this "the most tragic moment" of the tale (Letter 246). He said Gollum came "within a hair of repentance" but for one rough word (Letter 96).

**For the Book: IV.3.** This *is* the Warden's Door.
- Slow the approach with many small beats: the knees, the gift, the hand on the neck, the foreheads, the silence.
- Make the break one sentence: *▒▒▒▒ kicked it aside.* Start the next paragraph on the laugh.
- Build in threes. The Guest's *words* are not understood. His *gift* is kicked. His *touch* is torn away, and he is killed.

Three makes a tale build without one added sentence.

### 1.10 Enigma, not lore

**What it is.** Depth comes from things mentioned in passing by someone who assumes you know them, and from tellings that do not quite agree. Drout, Hitotsubashi and Scavera name four sources of it:
- a vast background;
- casual, incomplete references;
- inconsistent accounts;
- texts that seem to be in many hands.

Tolkien said much the same of *Beowulf*: its allusions to old tales create "the impression of depth" (*The Monsters and the Critics*, 1936).

**Tolkien.** Bombadil is an enigma "intentionally" (Letter 144). Lúthien's song before Mandos is called the fairest and saddest ever made, and it is *never given*.

**For the Book.**
- Strike the burning custom, Tobe's saying, and every echo of it. The burning was ▒▒▒▒'s order, so that nothing would be left on that shore to speak against his letter. Say it once, plainly.
- Leave unexplained:
  - the boat turning into the wind;
  - the warden's long sleeves (*I know why*, says Brenn, and no more);
  - the three grey stones over Voss's grandmother's fire.
- Report the Guest's own tongue by its effect, storm-wind in old trees, and never transcribe it.
- The parting of the peoples gets a glance and never a telling.
- Where tellings disagree, keep both.

### 1.11 Dread, a small homely sign, then the great answer

**What it is.** Dread builds through dark, silence and din. The turn comes first through something small and *indifferent to the war*. The great answer follows.

**Tolkien.** The Witch-king rides through the broken Gate of Gondor. A cock crows in the city, careless of war and wizardry, and then the horns of Rohan answer. Shippey hears dawn in it, and life after death. In Mordor, Sam sees one white star and knows the Shadow is small and passing.

**For the Book: I.1.** Kael has the dread already: *a people break… like ice going out on a river.* He lacks the cock-crow. Let it be the cloak tearing:

> *Then tore he his cloak down the back. A small sound that was, the sound of old cloth. Over all the crying I heard it.*

The great answer is v1.3's widening count: the Twelve, the six companies, then *one voice, and a dozen, and then hundreds, until I could not count.* That count is the horns. Do not lengthen it.

### 1.12 Hold the grand style in reserve

**What it is.** The Bible's "and… and… and" is a *marked* style, and it works only against plain prose. Allan Turner shows Tolkien saving that New Testament parataxis for the second turn of the Pelennor. There it reads as "exalted" with almost no archaic words ("Style and Intertextual Echoes", in Lee, ed., *A Companion to J. R. R. Tolkien*, 2020). Rosebury traces Merry's stroke rising from plain syntax to a heroic note.

**Tolkien.** Tolkien's images are mostly short, a few words of plain comparison, placed at the hinge. Long Homeric similes are rare.

**For the Book.** v2.0 used polysyndeton and long similes on every page, so none of them meant *this matters*.
- **The body of a tale:** short, end-stopped sentences in old order.
- **One "and"-chain per tale, at its halt:**
  - I.2: the guild rising.
  - IV.3: none at the killing, which must be bare.
  - V.7: Della lying down by the mark.
- **One short image per beat.** v2.0's fisher's-wife simile in V.7 is a fine sentence in the wrong place. Use *She looked east, as one looks after a sail.*

### 1.13 Wood leaves: the broken record and the maker

**What it is.** Tolkien shows fragments *as fragments*, then lets a shaping voice tell the whole.

**Tolkien.** In Moria, Gandalf reads the burnt Book of Mazarbul aloud: gaps, half-lines, the drums in the deep, until it breaks off with the enemy coming. The gaps frighten more than any full account could. Elsewhere Gandalf tells Sméagol's whole story, having got it from Gollum bit by bit.

**For the Book (Jack's fourth note).** These two models are Halyna and Seren.
- **Halyna** speak *as the wood gives the memory up*. They use phrases and half-ideas. They correct each other, and they say *we two*. Set this in italic.
- **Seren** then makes it a story, in her own voice.

> *"A door. Our kin in the door-posts. Dead — sawn."* / *"A foot. The dust."* / *"Laughter. And we two — no. We, the wood, in the dust, hearing."*
>
> So lay the gift in the dust by the wall, and heard the men laugh, and no hand took it up.

The reader watches the memory pieced together, then hears it whole. The wood leaves get a different *texture* from the stone leaves, as well as different content. v2.0's IV.4 already reaches for this (*Then a long jolting in the dark…*). Keep to the Mystaeri side.

### 1.14 High-style dialogue: few words, balanced, understated at the worst

**What it is.** In the high style people say little, in balanced halves or proverbs, and at the worst moment they understate. Tolkien's *thou* marks either formality or sudden intimacy. Rateliff warns that modern readers miss the intimacy.

**Tolkien.** Beren returns to Thingol without his right hand and says a Silmaril is in his hand even now. Hand and jewel are both in the wolf. Asked how a man should judge in such times, Aragorn answers with a principle. Weak and evil speakers get the modern clerk's idiom: Gorbag on the "Big Bosses", Saruman's reasonable voice (Rosebury).

**For the Book.**
- **Halyna's split lines** are balanced high style. Keep each half short.
- **The Captain** has one question in I.2, and that is right.
- **▒▒▒▒** gets no speeches: screams, "Seize him", and his letter. *Unknown intruder. Hostile intent. Neutralized. Vessel burned and released.* That letter should be the one modern-sounding passage in an old-sounding Book. It is Tolkien's orc-register used as a moral mark.
- **Halyna** may say *thou* to each other at the deaths, never for ornament.

**The weak man.** Tolkien's weak cruelty is Saruman shrunk to "Sharkey", with Lotho and Wormtongue: small men propped up by another's power. Their spite is pointless and *hidden*, trees felled for nothing. ▒▒▒▒ fits this exactly:
- holds his post by his father's name;
- has never known battle;
- kicks a gift and screams for a killing;
- burns the evidence and writes the lie;
- hides his arm in sleeves for life.

Tell it plainly. Explain none of it. That is the whole portrait.

### 1.15 Songs that act

**What it is.** Tolkien's songs come out of the moment, someone sings them, and they *do* something.

**Tolkien.** Sam sings in the dark tower, and Frodo's faint voice answers. The song finds him. After the Pelennor, the song of the Mounds of Mundburg is given as what a maker of Rohan made later. That turns the battle into memory.

**For the Book.** Songs stay verbatim. The writers' work is placement:
- set each song after a silence;
- give it one task: to answer, call, mourn, or remember;
- keep *as they sing it still*, which is the Mundburg move.

V.7's gate-song is the best example in the Book. The first voice is missing, and the second comes in alone on the comma. Keep it.

### 1.16 Landscape that carries feeling

**What it is.** Weather and light carry meaning, and they turn when the story turns.

**Tolkien.** Mordor's fumes bring the Dawnless Day. The wind changes on the Pelennor. In Ithilien in spring, Sam stews rabbit among the herbs, a homely pause in a dangerous land.

**For the Book.** One weather per tale, turning at the halt, in one or two strokes:
- **IV.3:** a windless sea, *and the boat came anyway*. Later, fire leaps against the stillness, and the boat turns into a wind that is not there.
- **I.1:** dusk, snow on the hearth, and the wind taking the cloak.
- **V.7:** the drums all night, then the soft mortar under Seren's thumb. Time measured by a Stonewright's clock is the best line in v2.0's V.7.

### 1.17 The ending turn: eucatastrophe and the long defeat

**What it is.** *On Fairy-Stories* (1947) defines the eucatastrophe: the sudden joyous "turn". It does not deny sorrow. It gives joy "poignant as grief", felt as "a catch of the breath". Letter 89 speaks of joy that brings tears. Its twin is the long defeat, Galadriel's phrase and Tolkien's view of history in Letter 195. The theology is Tolkien's own. **The Book borrows the shape, with no Christian symbolism.**

**Tolkien.**
- At Cormallen, Sam wakes to find Gandalf alive and asks whether everything sad is going to come untrue. The host laughs and weeps together.
- Gondolin falls, and the child Eärendil escapes the ruin.
- Túrin's tale ends with no turn at all. That is the tragic pole.

**For the Book.** Each tale turns just before *We remember*.
- **I.1** turns to the hooded Captain: *It was like that.*
- **IV.3** turns on the stone. A boy picks it from the dust. Forty years later it goes into a chest, and a child of the guild carries that chest up the mountain. Hope comes out of the ruin in the smallest hands: Gondolin's turn, and Letter 131's motive that the world's wheels are turned by the "unknown and weak". Seren lands it and states no lesson.
- **The deaths of the bonded** are the long defeat with joy hidden in it.
  - Seren searches the mark for the stroke Della cut alone: *You cannot tell.* That is a eucatastrophe in miniature. Keep it, and cut the narration around it.
  - Public-domain model: Dasent's *Burnt Njal* (1861), ch. 128. Offered her life, Bergthora says she was given to Njal young and promised that they would share one fate. Njal says only that they will go to their bed, for he has long been eager for rest. That is understatement as death-song.
  - II.3 states the rule of the bonded once (*When the left hand dies, does not the right one die too?*). Della's death is that rule coming true, unrestated.
  - Wendhessa, under one cloak in the snow: *neither of them rose.* This is already saga-plain. Keep it that short.

## 2 · The three named tales at a glance

**I.1, The Torn Cloak.**
- Keep v1.3's sentences in old word order, with Kael's counting and oaths.
- Keep the coal as a bare plant (1.5).
- Add the tearing cloth as the cock-crow (1.11).
- The answer is the widening count.
- End on *It was like that.*

It needs older syntax, not more words.

**IV.3, The Warden's Door.**
- Return to v1.3's shape and length.
- Brenn only watched. His *we* is the post, said once (1.8).
- Three offers, refused in rising order. The kick is one sentence (1.9).
- The burning is ▒▒▒▒'s order to hide the deed, and no custom (1.10).
- The letter is the lone modern register (1.14).
- The annal ends on the stone carried up the mountain (1.17).

v2.0's additions are each well made: the runners' oath, the Council clerk, Tarnel's net. Under Jack's economy rule, keep at most one.

**The deaths of the bonded (II.3, V.7, Wendhessa).**
- State the rule once, in II.3.
- In V.7:
  - doom early: *higher it stood never* (1.7);
  - the house and the jest (1.6);
  - one halt, the mallet (1.4);
  - one image, the sail (1.12);
  - the soft mortar (1.16);
  - the turn: *You cannot tell* (1.17).
- Close with Njal's restraint: no one explains what happened.
