# R4 · What Makes a Story Build

*Craft research for the Book of the Riven Stone, aimed at Jack's note of 2026-10-02: "they don't build… so clinical… like you are writing history text book." Rules first, then a diagnosis of I.1, IV.3 and V.7 with line references.*

Line numbers: **v1.3** = `wf7/book/legends_v13.md`; **v2.0** = `wf10/book_v2.md`.

---

## 0 · The short answer

A history textbook does three things. It stands far off, it sums up, and it explains. A story comes close at the moment that matters, shows that moment as it happens, and leaves the meaning for the hearer to take up. Every source below says this in its own way.

v2.0 fails on all three. Its tales open with dated rubrics and a sentence announcing what the tale holds. They stop the action to explain customs, kin and procedure. Every big simile ends by explaining itself. And they stack ending after ending, one *and then* at a time. v1.3 is closer to a story, with short sentences and tellers who sound like people, but it explains too, most of all in V.7.

How would Tolkien tell these tales? He would start in a moment, not a date, and give the hearer one thing to love before it is lost. He would hold the instant before the deed, then let something small and uncaring turn it, as the cock crows at the Gate of Gondor. He would leave the deep past as glimpses, end on a picture, and let the song carry the rest.

## 2 · Fifteen rules

**1. Join beats by *because*, not *after*.** Aristotle asks for incidents that follow "by necessity or probability". It matters greatly, he says, whether one thing happens because of another or only after it (§§VIII, X). Parker and Stone: between beats put "therefore" or "but". If "and then" fits, the story is dull.
*For the Book:* the test is for *beats*, not for the word *and*. Ong shows that oral style is additive (*and… and…*), and that suits the Book's old parataxis. So clauses may be joined with *and*, but events must be joined by *therefore* and *but*. v2.0 gets this backwards: its sentences nest clauses, and its events are merely added.

**2. One action, one question.** Aristotle's unity of action asks for one action, complete. Olrik's folk-tale laws ask for one strand and two speakers to a scene. Gardner's basic plot: a character wants something, goes after it against opposition, and wins, loses or draws.
*For the Book:* name each tale's yes-or-no question before drafting. In I.1 it is *will this broken people stand?* In IV.3 it is *will anyone at that door do right?* In V.7 Part I it is *will the pair sent apart come back together?* Cut whatever does not press on it.

**3. Let the turn and the knowing fall together.** The best recognition comes with a reversal and "arises from the incidents themselves" (§§XI, XVI). Tolkien's "sudden joyous turn" is the same hinge swung toward joy. At the Gate it turns on something small and uncaring: "a cock crowed", "reckoning nothing of wizardry or war".
*For the Book:* the hinge is usually a small thing: the coal, the stone in the dust, the word *written*, the mortar still soft. It must come out of what the hearer has already seen, never out of Seren saying it.

**4. Show the crisis as a scene; sum up only what lies between.** Burroway: summary covers a long time briefly, and scene covers a short time at length. A crisis happens at a moment and cannot be summed up. Gardner's test is the reader's "vivid and continuous dream", which must be clear and unbroken. His scale of psychic distance runs from "It was winter of the year 1853" (far) to the snow under your collar (inside). Darwin warns that staying far off for long "can get very dull".
*For the Book:* the history-textbook voice is simply far distance plus summary, held through the crisis. Stand far off for the road and the years. At the deed, come in close: the hand, the step, the word said aloud.

**5. Try, fail, make it worse.** Sanderson: when a hero tries, the answer is "yes, but" (it worked, at a cost) or "no, and" (it failed, and now there is more trouble). Vonnegut: be a sadist, so the reader sees what your people are made of. Coats: we admire trying more than success.
*For the Book:* siege tales build when each defence costs something. Pellow sees the trick: yes, but the Captain can reach only one battery at a time.

**6. Plant before you pay.** Chekhov to Lazarev: do not put a loaded rifle on the stage if no one means to fire it. O'Connor's wooden leg is a wooden leg first, then gathers ("accumulates") meaning.
*For the Book:* v2.0 holds the Book's best plants: the unwritten half of the cloak (198 → 232), the runner's oath (1220 → 1276–1280, 1328), the bed of black wood (1218 → 1286), "Look close" (2190 → 2250), the soft mortar (2121 → 2166). Keep them all, but plant *before* the moment that needs it. v2.0 2115 reveals only at Della's grief that Enno "had always set it for her"; that habit belongs in the opening lines, shown.

**7. Give the one exact detail.** Chekhov to his brother: show a moonlit night through a bit of broken bottle glittering on a mill-dam. O'Connor: fiction reaches us through the senses, not through abstractions. Le Guin: crowd the prose, vivid and exact, every detail in focus.
*For the Book:* the best things in these tales are details. "Thirty-one there are, and four of them move" (v2.0 192). A mother's hand on a fevered neck (v1.3 752). A gull walking among the stones (v2.0 1230). The glass "whole" (v2.0 2218). The worst are abstractions, like "the trappings of a rich man's vanity" (v1.3 736).

**8. Make us love them before they die.** Griffin shows that the Iliad gives its minor dead little obituaries. Butler's Simoeisius was born by the river Simois, and "did not live to pay his parents for his rearing". Vonnegut: everyone should want something, even "a glass of water". Dasent's Bergthora gets one old promise before the fire: "we would both share the same fate."
*For the Book:* before a death, give one want, habit or promise, early and in action. Do not give a biography.

**9. Leave it unexplained.** Benjamin: half the art of storytelling is to keep a story "free from explanation". Herodotus never explains why Psammenitus stands dry-eyed as his children pass in chains, then weeps at an old servant. That is why the story still lives. Auerbach finds the same in Genesis 22: only the decisive points are shown, and the rest stays "fraught with background". Hemingway cut the real ending of "Out of Season" and found that the story grew stronger. Tolkien (Letter 247) puts the pull of his book in "glimpses of a large history", and says that going there destroys the magic.
*For the Book:* this is Jack's "muddies the story" put in theory. Tobe's custom (v2.0 1220, 1254–1258) is *explanation dressed as depth*. It answers the very question the hearer should be left holding: *how could they?* Once a custom explains the burning, it stops being ▒▒▒▒'s choice, and the horror leaks out. A glimpse opens a gap; invented lore closes it.

**10. Be colder at the grave.** Chekhov to Avilova: "try to be colder". Coldness gives grief a background against which it stands out; "your heroes weep and you sigh." In Dasent's saga, Thorgrim comes down off Gunnar's roof with a spear in him. Asked whether Gunnar is at home, he answers that he is sure "his bill is at home", and falls dead.
*For the Book:* at a death, write short and plain, with no simile and no comment. "It was still soft. / She was gone." (v2.0 2121–2123) is exactly right.

**11. End on a picture, not a moral.** Olrik: tales rise to clear *tableaux*, and the weight falls on the last place. Conrad says Marlow's meanings lie outside the tale, "as a glow brings out a haze".
*For the Book:* "On his back is the other half" (v2.0 232) and "We were a people of doors" (v1.3 778) are endings. "That night, for the first time, we saw that the enemy could hope" (v1.3 1293; v2.0 2285) is a moral. After the picture, only the hearth's fixed words may follow.

**12. Crowd, then leap.** Le Guin: draft full, then cut what pads, repeats or slows "till what's left is what counts". Every leap follows the line of the whole.
*For the Book:* this is Jack's "economy". Measured, v1.3 I.1 / IV.3 / V.7 run **1,217 / 1,042 / 1,046** words, and v2.0 runs **1,841 / 2,508 / 3,461**. Mean sentence length rose from about 12–16 words to 21–31, with a longest of 127. Semicolons rose from 0–6 per tale to 22–44. Aim at v1.3's length or less, keeping v2.0's plants.

**13. The style is the story, and real archaism is concise.** Le Guin: take away a fantasy's style and only "a synopsis of the plot" is left. Tolkien (Letter 171) rejects fake-medieval "tushery". Real archaic English, he holds, is more concise than modern idiom, and Théoden cannot use modern phrases because he does not think in them.
*For the Book:* Jack's two demands agree. The King James Bible, Malory and Dasent are hard through words, inflections and word order, while their clauses stay short. v2.0's run-ons are nested modern clauses: long, but still easy. Use short clauses in old grammar.

**14. Use the teller's tricks: pause, withholding, aside, a refrain that changes.** Tedlock: a spoken tale's meaning lives in its silences. Alter: biblical narrative repeats a key word (the *Leitwort*), and the *variation* carries the meaning. Olrik: patterns of three, with the weight on the last.
*For the Book:* write the pause as an image, like the cough and then "nothing in the ward but the wind" (v2.0 194). Use the aside rarely: "you children at the front" (v2.0 230). "We obeyed" works because it moves from "his men obeyed" to "we" (v1.3 754–756). Unchanging refrains turn into tics. These include "Hold the stone, Seren. I tell myself so every time." (v2.0 1294, 2095, 2184, 2262), "And the Captain put his hood back" (1326, 2178, 2256), and the verbatim pike line at each cairn. "We remember" is fixed by law, so let the *frame* vary it, as when the hearth is "slow to follow" after a killing by our own people (v2.0 1324).

**15. A frame heightens when the tale changes its listeners.** In Butler's *Odyssey* VIII, Ulysses hides his weeping under his mantle as the bard sings of Troy, and only Alcinous notices. In IX his name finally comes out. In XI he breaks off, and the hall sits "enthralled and speechless" until Arete speaks. Marlow becomes, in the dark, "no more to us than a voice". In the *Nights* the frame carries the stakes; Todorov's line for it is that narrative equals life. Chaucer's pilgrims "quite" each other. And Gandalf reads the broken Book of Mazarbul aloud as "drums in the deep" begin.
*For the Book:* Seren's frame should show a listener changed, an Amen hard to say, and the present pressing on the telling. On WOOD leaves the Mazarbul model answers Jack's note 4. With hands on the grain, {{Halyna}} give up broken phrases in two inks, and a line is often whole only across both. Then Seren makes the story. The hearer sees both stages.

## 3 · The textbook voice, named

1. **Dated rubrics.** v2.0 163, 1204, 2093, 2182, 2260 ("SEVENTY-FIVE YEARS BEFORE THE BANNER"). Compare the book subtitles: v1.3 156 ("How we came to stand") is a story, and v2.0 159 is an abstract.
2. **Thesis lines.** "Hear how the cloak was torn… and who answered it" (v2.0 173); likewise 2097, 2186 and 2264.
3. **Apparatus before the drama.** The 120-word rubric on seals and leave (v2.0 1206).
4. **Character by verdict.** v1.3 736–738; v2.0 1218; the Captain's résumé (v1.3 172–174).
5. **Similes that explain themselves.** The fisher's wife (v2.0 2117): "for she knows already which sail is lost". The lamplighter (2208): "for a taper carried fast goes out". The boots (2283): "for a man who asks for his boots has a road in his mind". The loaf (196) is a parable laid over the deed. Homer's similes widen the scene, like Butler's felled poplar "seasoning by the waterside". They never gloss it.
6. **Lore that explains a deed.** Tobe's custom (v2.0 1220, 1254–1262).
7. **Endings stacked with *and then*.** V.7 Part I (v2.0 2135–2178) has ten closing beats.
8. **The image glossed.** "The stranger was dead, so it was no man that screamed" (v2.0 1260). v1.3 760, "as though the wood itself was screaming", trusts the hearer.

## 4 · Diagnosis and rebuild

### I.1 · The Torn Cloak

**What builds (v1.3 158–229).** "I am a counter of things and not a singer" (164) gives the man and the method at once. Then the chain runs: the people break, "like ice going out on a river" (170); *therefore* the True Men look to the Captain; *but* he looks at the wall (177). The coal is planted at 179 and paid off with the writing. The peak belongs to the teller himself: the counter stops counting, "the only time I ever stopped" (196). The ending lives in the frame: "It was like that" (200).

**Where it reads like a textbook.**
- v1.3 172–174 and v2.0 181–187: the Captain's résumé sits between the breaking and the climb, so the dream halts at its highest stake for a lecture. v2.0 spreads it over four paragraphs and adds the route of the retreat (185).
- v2.0 163–165: a dated rubric, plus a retelling of Ebba's first "We remember", which Of This Book already gives (108).
- v2.0 169, 173, 175: an explanatory opening ("a soldier stands to give his count"), then the thesis line, then "the day of the banner" three times over (173, 175, 190).
- v2.0 196: the loaf simile at the very instant of the tearing. It puts Seren's voice in a counter's mouth, explains the halves before we see them, and breaks the dream.
- v2.0 212–222: twelve small portraits in the falling action, just when the hearer wants the answering.

**Rebuild.**
1. *Turn the résumé into action.* Make the list of what he was not into the Captain *looking*, three times, with the weight on the last. This keeps both the rhetoric and his reluctance: *He looked for a general, and the generals were slain. He looked for the lords, and the lords were scattered. He looked to the Stonewrights, and they sat in the grass with their chisels in their laps. Then the True Men looked to their Captain, and the Captain looked at the wall.* "He did not want to lead" moves *before* the column, as the plant.
2. *Keep v2.0's three best additions.* The thirty-one stones (192). The cough, then only wind (194). The unwritten half put back on his shoulders (198), paid off at 232. Cut the loaf.
3. *Make the roll a roll.* Each True Man gets a name and a haven. Give a short epithet only to the men whose habit pays off later (Pellow's glass, Hesk's lamp). Then: "Twelve. I counted us twice, and it did not take long."
4. *Fix the rubric to match Jack's note 3.* "He would not let me change a word of it, and I have not" (v1.3 160; v2.0 165) goes against the new canon. One stroke turns it into character: *He would not let me change a word of it. I changed a good many, and read it him again, and he said that was how he had told it.*
5. *Ending order:* the counter who stopped; the crimson, in one clause or cut; "you children at the front"; "On his back is the other half"; "It was like that"; the song, verbatim.

### IV.3 · The Warden's Door

**What builds (v1.3 726–782).** This is the strongest of the three, because it is a confession. Brenn's hindsight ("I know it now", 746) gives tragic irony: we know the end, as Aristotle's audience knew Oedipus's, so the dread lies in *how*. The plants are exact. The stone is set down like bread before a guest (748), which pays off at the law of the door (778). The hand is on the neck "the way my mother did when I had a fever" (752). The burning is the warden's order, "so there would be nothing to show" (760), which is correct canon. Then the letter (764), the master stroke. *The clinical voice belongs to ▒▒▒▒.* "Unknown intruder. Hostile intent. Neutralized." is the textbook register used as the tool of the crime. Nothing else in the Book should sound like it.

**Where it reads like a textbook, or breaks canon.**
- v1.3 734–738, v2.0 1216–1218: the warden is given by verdicts. Jack reads him as *weak*, and weakness is best shown by one act before the boat lands. That act then causes his terror at the cut and the cover-up. For example: *He would not go down to the shingle; the grey affrighted him, and he had his letters carried down to the boats.*
- v1.3 754: the right amount for a man who only watched, but there is no image from a watcher's eyes. v2.0 has one and buries it: between their hands the stranger "went down on his knees again upon the step" (1242). That is the guest-rite going on through the violence, and Aristotle's pity in one line. Make it *the* image of the killing.
- v2.0 1246–1248: "I struck him… Twice." Cut, under Jack's note. The broken arms are gore that explains itself. Be colder. Put the watcher's line, already in v2.0 1262, where the blows stood: "would not look, and looked". Those four words are his whole sin.
- v2.0 1220, 1252–1262: Tobe, the custom, the net simile that sets it up, and Tobe washing the poles. Cut all of it, and go back to v1.3's single clause of motive.
- v2.0 1204–1214 and 1294–1310: the dated rubric, the archive procedure, the "Hold the stone" tic, and a second tragedy (Wendhessa and Tarnel freezing) tucked into the annal. v1.3's "after the passes she carried it alone" (776) already holds that loss, and leaving it out makes it stronger. Keep at most one picture: frost "on both its handles" (1308).
- *Canon flag:* "the half of a holy thing" (v1.3 774; v2.0 1330) breaks the rule that the cross-sign is not holy. Use "the half of something, cut off in the middle" (v2.0 1298) or "the half of a sign".
- *Judgment flag:* "I laughed. I have not laughed at anything since" (v1.3 750) is among the Book's best lines, but laughing is a small act, and Jack says Brenn should only observe. Decide it openly. Keep it as a boy's echo of the men, or give the watcher's version (*The men laughed. I did not laugh, nor did I bid them stop*).

**Rebuild: move the climax to Brenn's own deed.** A watcher's confession has no blow to climax on. v2.0 found the true climax and then smothered it. Brenn is the runner and swore "What I saw, I carry. I carry nothing else" (1220). He carries gift and lie in one pack (1274). The old clerk asks "Is it so?", and Brenn answers "It is written" (1278–1280). It is the only thing he *does*, and it is a word, not a blow. That is a true reversal: his oath becomes his damnation. It also fits Jack's canon: he did not stop them, and he carried it. The sequence:
weakness (one act) → the boat with no wind → three words → bread before a guest → the kick, and the laugh decision → hand on the neck, then the cut → "Seize him" / "We seized him" → he kneels again → *We obeyed* → the burning, by order, to leave nothing → the letter → the pack → "Is it so?" / "It is written." → long sleeves; the bed of black wood → God.
Then a short annal (the name cut; the sign; the chest, "alone"), and the law of doors. The frame goes last and heightens: the hearth slow to follow Ebba, and the runners adding "Not as Brenn carried" (v2.0 1328). That is Chaucer's quiting and Scheherazade's stake at once, since the tale changes its listeners' oath. Keep the boat turning against the wind (1264–1266) only as the glimpse IV.5 needs. It is a wonder, not an explanation.

### V.7 · The Mirror in the Grain

**What builds.** The idea is the Book's best recognition. The enemy works as a bonded pair works (v1.3 1246). They learned deceit from us (1272–1276). They have learned to hope (1293). v2.0 gave these recognitions a human cost and planted well. A pair is sent apart (2107). The jest is split at the stair (2109). "A mile of wall is far" (2111). The mortar is "still soft" (2121) and later "drying" on Halvard's trowel (2166). "You cannot tell" (2133) echoes the two inks of Of This Book (122). Kael counts a dead pair as "One" (2147). In Part II, "One at a time is slow" (2210), the whole glass, and Ruan's "Look close" carry it.

**Where it reads like a textbook.**
- v1.3 1239–1293: without the deaths, all three parts are strategy debriefs. "They know us now. They know that we can break them" (1291) is the voice of a staff report.
- v2.0 2089–2101: a rubric, a dated header, the tic, a thesis line, then backstory and four pair-names before anything happens.
- v2.0 2115 explains a plant at the moment it pays off. v2.0 2117 sets a 70-word simile at the climax.
- v2.0 2125, 2135–2178: commentary, then ten closing beats. The recognition lies under fifty lines and never lands.
- *Jack's note 4:* {{Halyna}} speak only polished sentences on the shingle.

**Rebuild.** The hidden mirror is the key. The carved order says *"Let the east and the north go as one"*, and the Shore sent Enno east and Della north. They died in one breath, and so went "as one". v2.0 contains this but buries it. Aristotle's best recognition comes from the incidents, so Seren must *not* explain it. She only has to clear the way to the echo:
1. Open on the first stone of the house of one door. Enno sets the chisel and Della strikes, so the plant is shown. Then the drums stop.
2. "They sent a pair apart." Ormund gives Enno the east, Penna gives Della the north. The jest splits over the guns. "A mile of wall is far."
3. Colder. Enno falls offstage ("I did not see it. Hale did."). The mallet stops. She looks east. With no simile, she sets the chisel herself, cuts the last stroke, and lies down. "It was still soft. She was gone."
4. Only payoffs: the mark and "You cannot tell"; Stannard carrying Enno the mile; Orvenna finishing the jest; Kael's "One." Cut the prayer and the commentary, and keep the pike line short and varied.
5. The wood leaf as Mazarbul. Rhyna's hand goes on the plank, and Halvard goes pale on the wall. Then fragments in two inks: *…three roads… one hour…* / *…the east… and the north…* / *…as one.* Seren sets down the whole carving once, then "It is how we work a wall." / "The one waits for the other's stroke…" Halvard looks at the trowel, and the mortar is drying on it. End there, or on "I have written the name, and I will not write what I thought", the finest withholding in the Book. Choose one, and let nothing follow it but the hearth.
6. Parts II and III already run on *but* and *therefore*. We hid our guns, *therefore* the groves learned. Pellow looked close, *but* only one battery at a time, *therefore* he met them alone. Strip the lamplighter and boots similes to their pictures. End Part II on "Look close" and Part III on the lamp going out (v2.0 2293), not on the moral about hope.

## 5 · Checklist for the writers

1. Write the tale's one question at the top of the draft; cut what does not press on it.
2. Join beats with *therefore* or *but*; *and* joins clauses, never events.
3. No dated rubrics, no "Hear how…" lines.
4. Sum up the road; come close at the deed.
5. Plant every payoff earlier, as an act, and never explain it at the payoff.
6. One exact detail per beat, from the teller's trade.
7. Before a death, give a want, habit or promise. At the death, be cold: no simile, no comment.
8. No custom or lore that explains a deed. Glimpses may open gaps; nothing may close them.
9. Similes are rare and native to the teller, and never end in a "for…" clause.
10. Refrains must change or be cut. The frame varies "We remember".
11. End on the picture.
12. Make it Old English through grammar and diction, in short clauses: hard, never long.
13. Wood leaves: fragments in two inks under the hand, and Seren's story after.
14. A single tale at about 1,000–1,200 words or less.
15. Seren's rubric shows her craft and the teller's assent, never a vow that she changed nothing.
