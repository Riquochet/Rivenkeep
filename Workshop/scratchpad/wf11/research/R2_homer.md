## 0 · The diagnosis, in Auerbach's terms

In "Odysseus' Scar", the first chapter of *Mimesis*, Erich Auerbach describes almost exactly what Jack felt in v2.0. In his account, Homer lights everything evenly, and connectives spell out every relation between things. Nothing is "half-illuminated", and there is no glimpse of "unplumbed depths", only a "perpetual foreground". Against Homer he sets Genesis 22. There only the decisive points are lit, the rest does not exist, and feeling is only hinted at through silence. That story is "fraught with background".

v2.0 took Homer's syntax without Homer's eye: it **explains**. IV.3 grew from 1,039 words in v1.3 to 2,514. Its explanatory ", for …" clauses rose from 2 to 12, and its semicolons from 0 to 29. The headnotes give dates ("seventy-five years before the banner") and trace each object from hand to hand. That is how a history textbook reads.

The fix takes from both of Auerbach's poles: from Homer, the vivid foreground for **things**; from Genesis, the dark background for **hearts**. Then the tales build with the oral epic's engines: the end told first, a delay at the brink, three-step escalation, and a return to the start.

**Sources read.**

## 1 · Light the deed; leave the heart dark (Genesis 22)

**The source.** Auerbach notes what Genesis 22 leaves out:
- where God speaks from, and why He tempts Abraham;
- where Abraham is when he answers "Behold, here I am". Auerbach reads that phrase as a moral posture, not a place;
- the journey. Three days get a single sentence: on the third day Abraham lifted up his eyes and saw the place afar off;
- Isaac himself. He is only "thine only son … whom thou lovest", because nothing else about him serves.

In Homer, speech brings thought into the open. Here speech points at thoughts that stay unspoken. Abraham says "Here am I" to God, to his son and to the angel. Isaac asks where the lamb is, and Abraham answers. Twice the text says only "they went both of them together".

**The lesson.** The reader's dread fills the dark. Once a motive is stated, the reader stops wondering.

**Rules.** At a crisis, give no "for" clause of motive: give the deed, the thing in the hand and the words spoken aloud. A "for" that reports something seen may stay; one that explains a heart goes. Describe a person only by what the moment needs, and let one repeated phrase carry the feeling.

**Applied.**
- **IV.3.** Keep v1.3's "I do not know why I did it" word for word; it is the Genesis move. v2.0's "We knew the custom, and we were glad of it, for it let us stop thinking" goes twice over: the custom is gone (Jack's ruling), and the clause explained a heart. The burning's purpose must come from the **warden's own mouth, as an order** (§7). The weak man is shown by one order and one long sleeve, plus v1.3's three short sentences about him, and nothing more.
- **Brenn's "we".** v1.3's "His men obeyed." / "We obeyed." is Abraham's method: the verb repeats, the pronoun shifts, and nothing is said about it. The "we" means the post. Jack's correction needs one bare line, such as *"I struck no blow. Nor stayed I any."* (illustrative). Also change v1.3's "the warden had **us** lay the body" to "had his men lay it". The laugh can stay (§6), as the one thing a boy among men did; the orchestrator should confirm it does not read as taking part.

## 2 · One object that sounds at the crisis (Homer's foreground, used rightly)

**The source** (*Od.* 19). Odysseus turns from the firelight because he remembers the scar. The nurse takes the leg, knows the scar, and drops the foot. It falls into the bath, and the basin rings, tips over and spills. Her eyes fill and her voice stops. She catches his beard; he catches her throat. Penelope does not look, because Athena has turned her mind away. Her feeling is named only as "joy and grief". The basin does the rest.

**The lesson.** Homer's vividness does not come from explaining. It comes from the physical world reacting as the heart breaks.

**Rule.** Give each crisis one object that sounds or moves, never three, and plant it earlier in the tale.

**Applied.**
- **IV.3.** The kicked gift "went into the dust by the wall" is our basin. Keep v2.0's gull among the stones to one sentence, with "and the gull was gone" afterwards.
- **I.1.** The coal is planted at the dead hearth. v2.0's **other half of the cloak**, put back on his shoulders, is our scar: plant it once in action and reveal it once at the bench ("On his back is the other half.").
- **V.2.** "The broken grain met, as the halves of a split log meet", then "I have not asked them what it means". That is Homer's object and Genesis's silence together, and it is already right.
- **IV.6.** Rhyna's hands in the rain-butt: never explain them.

## 3 · Recognition: brief, costly, by the right token (Argos; the bed; Aristotle)

**The source.**
- **Argos** (*Od.* 17). The dog lies on uncleared dung, full of fleas. At his master's voice he drops his ears and wags his tail, but he cannot reach him. Odysseus hides a tear behind an idle question, and the dog dies the moment he has seen him. It takes a few dozen lines.
- **Aristotle** (*Poetics* 16) ranks recognition by tokens, such as scars, lowest. Recognition by memory ranks higher: Odysseus weeping at the bard's song. Best is the recognition that grows out of the events.
- **The bed** (*Od.* 23). One bedpost is a living, rooted olive.

**The lesson.** Recognition is short, it costs the one who recognises, and its token is tied to the deepest theme.

**Applied.**
- **IV.4.** "At the door he laid his hand on the post and greeted them, and they did not answer, for they were dead." This is Argos reversed: the kin who should know him are dead timber. Do not touch it.
- **The two beds.** v2.0's warden dies "in his bed of black wood". Odysseus's bed grows from a living tree; the warden's is sawn from a felled pillar. Keep the line, and never point out the contrast.
- **The Captain at the bench is Odysseus at the Phaeacian feast:** he hears his own deeds, hood up, and only one person notices (Alcinous there, Seren here). Use v2.0's "And the Captain put his hood back" once in the Book.
- **Epilogue.** "I know chisel-work": recognition by craft, Aristotle's best kind. Keep it that short.

## 4 · The bard at the hearth: the man who lived it praises the singer (Demodocus)

**The source** (*Od.* 8–11).
- **Odysseus praises the singer of his own war.** He sends the blind Demodocus a cut from his own portion. He honours him above all men, because the bard sings the Achaeans' sufferings as if he had been there. Then he asks for the wooden horse, knowing it will make him weep.
- **Odysseus becomes the bard.** When he stops mid-tale, the hall sits "enthralled and speechless", and the king begs him to go on, because the night is long.

Aristotle (*Poetics* 24) praises Homer for speaking as little as possible in his own person. Goethe and Schiller would have the reciter read from behind a curtain.

**The lesson.** (a) This is Jack's note 3 exactly: the man who lived it calls the singer's version the true one. (b) Inside the song, the singer is nearly invisible.

**Applied.**
- **I.1 headnote.** v1.3's "He would not let me change a word of it, and I have not" now contradicts Jack's note. Make it the Demodocus beat: *"He would have no word of his changed. I changed many; and when I read it him, he said it was the count he had meant to give, and let it stand."* (illustrative). One small gesture of thanks, like the cut of pork, may appear once in the Book.
- **Seren's art lives in the sentence, not in commentary.** Headnotes run to two sentences at most: who laid the tale, in which Tide, and which leaf faces it. No dates and no record of who carried what.
- **Each teller keeps one signature move,** and Seren gives him the one turn of phrase he could not have made. Kael counts, Hale runs, Voss asks, Brenn confesses, and {{Halyna}} split a line between them. Kael's "a sound like ice going out on a river" is Seren's line in Kael's mouth, and he let it stand.
- **The break.** Seren stopping at the night of the pass (v2.0's IV.3 annal) is Odysseus breaking off in Book 11. Use it once, and briefly.

## 5 · Begin in the middle; reach back once (*in medias res*)

**The source.** Horace says Homer does not begin the Trojan War from Leda's twin egg. He hurries the hearer into the middle of things *as if they were already known*. The *Odyssey* opens in the tenth year, with its hero absent for four books. We know him first by the hole he has left: the suitors in his hall, and a son who does not know him. The wanderings arrive late, as a tale told at a hearth.

**The lesson.** Trust the hearer to know the world, so that implication replaces explanation. Define the hero by what the others lack.

**Applied.**
- **The Captain** is that absent Odysseus: he never tells, is never named, and is seen only in other people's tellings. Keep that absolute.
- **Headnotes** never begin from the egg. Cut every "seventy-five years before the banner".
- **IV.5 (Voss).** Open on what is now his ending: the first hull of the siege burning and screaming, heard at forty on the shingle. Reach back to the grandmother's harbour-house and the rhyme. Return to the shingle in nearly the same words, which makes a ring (§9). With the custom gone, the ship-masters' dread rests only on the outpost men's sworn tale. That is stronger.
- **IV.3** already starts in the middle in v1.3 ("I was nineteen."). Do not put v2.0's runners'-oath preamble at the head. If the oath stays, it comes once, when Brenn carries the letter.

## 6 · How to build: the end first, then wait at the brink

**The source.**
- **Homer tells the end in advance.** Antinous, we learn early, will be the first to taste Odysseus's arrow (*Od.* 21).
- **Signs of doom are mocked** (*Od.* 20). The suitors laugh with jaws not their own, over meat that runs with blood, and they laugh at the seer who sees the walls drip.
- **The revenge is delayed across five books,** while the insults grow worse:
  1. the goatherd Melanthius kicks him on the hip "out of pure wantonness";
  2. Antinous hurls a footstool;
  3. Ctesippus throws an ox-hoof and calls it a guest-present.
- **The suitors' seating order.** Reece shows the suitors are passed in the same order three times: at the begging, the bow and the slaughter. The third pass runs in reverse.
- **Delay as a law of epic.** Goethe and Schiller make the delaying motif epic's law. Schiller says Homer's goal is already present at every step.

**The lesson.** Dread builds when the hearer knows the end and must wait at its edge. A tale turns clinical when there is no foreknowledge and no waiting, or when the waiting is filled with explanation.

**Rules.**
1. Put the end in the teller's mouth early, in one line.
2. At the brink, delay with a thing or a gesture, never an explanation.
3. Escalate in three steps; the third breaks.
4. Use at most one Homeric "had it not been…" per tale.

**Applied.**
- **IV.3** has all four in v1.3:
  - the end first: "I laughed. I have not laughed at anything since." It is also the suitors' laugh;
  - three steps: the kick, the hand on the neck, and the blade;
  - the counterfactual: "if it had stopped there…"

  Add only the gull, before the kick.
- **Book Four is the five-book delay.** IV.1 to IV.6 build toward the Guest's sentence. Each leaf ends on an image the next leaf takes up: the stone in the dust, then the boat burning, then the scream on the water.
- **IV.4 and V.4** each build to a single carved sentence. Nothing follows it except the closing ring.

## 7 · Guest-friendship and its breaking (*xenia*)

**The source.** Every stranger and suppliant is under Zeus. The Cyclops says his kind care nothing for Zeus, and he promises "Noman" a guest-gift: to be eaten last. A suitor warns that the beggar may be a god in disguise. Homer grades the breakers of the law: the monster (Polyphemus), the idle many (the suitors), and the petty man (Melanthius, who kicks because he can).

**The lesson.** The guest-gift is the poem's moral hinge. Homer's *weak* host is the goatherd, not the Cyclops.

**Applied.**
- **The warden ▒▒▒▒ is Melanthius;** keep him small. His "present" to the Guest is the kick and then the fire, like Ctesippus's ox-hoof. Invent nothing to frame the burning. The only frame is the law, said once at the end: "one who kneels at your door is under your roof. We were a people of doors." Keep it.
- **Let the order and the rhyme share words.** IV.5's rhyme stays word for word ("Burn it and shove it and give it the tide"). The warden's order in IV.3 can use the same words, spoken flat: *"Burn it, and shove it, and give it the tide; let naught be left to say otherwise."* (illustrative). The children's rhyme then preserves his cover-up, unremarked, with no new lore.
- **The letter** ("Unknown intruder. Hostile intent. Neutralized. Vessel burned and released.") is the Book's most Auerbachian line: an official foreground with the background cut away. Keep it word for word.
- **VI.1** ({{Halyna}} kneel, "the only step one people can take alone") restores the guest-law from the guest's side. The Epilogue's "We have not yet earned a guest" closes it.

## 8 · The simile and the epithet: rare, at the hinge, turned

**Similes.** The *Odyssey* saves its long similes for its hinges.
- **The victor weeps like the vanquished.** At the song of the horse, Odysseus weeps like a woman flung on her husband's body before his city and led off into slavery. Foley calls this a "reverse simile".
- **The wife and the shipwreck.** Penelope's joy at the reunion is a shipwrecked sailor's joy at reaching land.
- **Pity in the slaughter.** The dead suitors lie like netted fish gasping on the beach.

**Epithets.** Parry showed that Homer's epithets were tools for fitting the metre. On the page an epithet's power lies in **the turn**: the *Iliad* ends by calling Hector "tamer of horses" at his funeral.

**Rules.** At most one extended simile per tale, at its hinge, drawn from the teller's world; prefer a reverse simile that crosses the wall. Give the full epithet at the first naming and at the moment it bites.

**Applied.**
- **IV.3.** The Guest's hand "the way my mother did when I had a fever" is the tale's one simile. It is a reverse simile: the enemy compared to a mother. Add no other.
- **I.1.** Kael's "ice going out on a river" competes with v2.0's 70-word loaf simile. Keep the ice; reduce the loaf to a clause, or cut it.
- **Epithet turns.** "Voss who asked" answers at last (I.5). "Kael the Counter" stops counting. "Seren Two-Inks" can turn in the Epilogue: *"I write this in one ink."* (illustrative).
- **v2.0's True Men tags** are a Homeric catalogue, where the hearer rests, so they do not belong at a climax. Keep the bare count in I.1 and move each tag to that man's Homecoming line in III.1.

## 9 · Ring composition: out, back, and home in the same words

**The source.**
- **The scar passage is a ring.** The nurse knows the scar. Then come the boar hunt, Autolycus naming the child, the boar again, and her hands on the scar. The **centre is the name**.
- **Minchin and Douglas.** Minchin shows Homer framing embedded stories this way. Douglas defines a ring as a theme brought back to its beginning, with the meaning at the turn.
- **De Jong** reads the digression as Eurycleia's memory, flooding in at the touch. Auerbach denies this, but we can use both (§11).

**Rule.** Each tale has a centre that holds the one thing that matters, and it opens and closes in matching words.

**Applied.**
- **Wood leaves.** "This is held in the grain" … "and the grain holds it still" is already a ring. Put the hard image at its centre:
  - IV.4: "it was the one his foot found";
  - V.4: "But war is for the short-minded and the angry young";
  - IV.6: "Even charred, even dying, the wood knows the way home."
- **The Book ring.** The Title in coal faces the Last Carver's Title, broken off mid-line (I.1 and the Epilogue). This is already in place.
- **VI.1 is Reece's reversed pass.**

  | | The steps |
  |---|---|
  | The warden's door | gift, kneeling, kick, blade, a burned boat pushed out |
  | VI.1, run back | a boat built, a gift laid in it, a kneeling, the boat pushed out, the boat turning into the wind |

  Keep the shared formula identical everywhere ("when it was well out it turned").

## 10 · Lament, silence, and the cost of vengeance

**The source.**
- **Three laments** (*Il.* 24). Each woman mourns Hector for something particular. Andromache grieves that he died without a last word to her; Helen, that he never spoke to her harshly. Keening answers each lament, and one closing line ends the poem.
- **The mother's ghost** (*Od.* 11). She slips three times through Odysseus's arms.
- **Ajax's silence** (*Od.* 11). Ajax walks off without a word. Longinus calls that silence grander than any speech.
- **No boasting over the dead** (*Od.* 22). Odysseus forbids the nurse to whoop over the dead, because it is unholy.
- **Peace by forgetting** (*Od.* 24). Zeus decrees a forgetting of the slaughter so the feud can end.

**The lesson.** Lament is particular, silence is louder than speech, and vengeance is never clean.

**Applied.**
- **The two closings.** "*And no one answered*" is Ajax's silence, and "We remember" is the answering keen. Both stay word for word every time, because their power is that they never change. Vary only the one line of frame around them, and rarely.
- **The broken three.** The Guest's rite has three steps, and the third, his own cut, was never made. Keep "He died there with the rite half made."
- **The Homecomings (III.1)** follow Odysseus's rule: no boasting over the fallen. "We left it standing. It had come home." is the model.
- **The Book inverts the Odyssey's ending.** Homer ends the feud by forgetting. Rivenkeep's wrong *was* forgetting, so its peace must come by remembering both sides. That is why the Epilogue ends "it must be sent kneeling". The writers should feel this; the Book never says it.

## 11 · The wood leaves as the nurse's hands

Jack's note 4 (on a wood leaf, {{Halyna}} speak in phrases, and Seren makes the memory a story) is the scar scene's shape. The hand touches; the one who touches can only stammer; the bard tells the whole past; we return to the hand.

**The form for every wood leaf:**
1. **The hand.** Seren's headnote: whose hand was on the wood, and where the other of them was.
2. **The fragments.** {{Halyna}} in two inks, broken, as the knowing came. v1.3's IV.2 "first known shape" ("…iron… each fell, sky came through… it did not…") is the model. Every wood leaf gets a brief one, in the plural (*"we two have it… the door… the cold of the door…"*, illustrative).
3. **Seren's telling.** "This is held in the grain." The story is whole, in the wood's voice, and this is where her craft plays.
4. **The return.** "and the grain holds it still", then "*And no one answered.*"

**The two facing leaves are Auerbach's two styles.** The wood sees every deed and no heart: "Fear leaves no mark on wood". Brenn's confession has the heart but cannot see the Guest's meaning. Each leaf alone is fraught with background; the reader works in the gutter between them. Never let one leaf explain the other, and keep IV.4 to what the wood could know. It never learns the warden's reason.

## 12 · Old English without the run-on: the Genesis sentence

The KJV builds Abraham's morning from short deeds joined by *and*: he rose up early, saddled his ass, clave the wood, and went. It is hard for a new reader because of **diction and word order** ("clave", "afar off", "thine only son", "Then on the third day Abraham lifted up his eyes"), not because of its clauses. v2.0's run-on is the opposite: clauses of explanation ("for…", "which…", "so that…") stacked with semicolons.

**For the Book:**
- **Make it hard through these:**
  - inversion ("Then came the stranger out of the grey.")
  - object first ("The gift he kicked aside.")
  - archaic words (*ere, naught, spake, clave, nigh, bade, whereon*)
  - the subjunctive ("had it stayed there")
  - *thou/ye* in direct address, consistent for each teller.
- **Chain with *and* only for deeds,** never for reasons.
- **Keep most sentences to 4–16 words,** with one long sentence per tale at a ritual moment such as the three oils or the climb.
- **Plain Words** retells in short modern sentences and leaves dark what the Book leaves dark. It is easier, not more explained.

## 13 · A beat plan for IV.3

1. **Frame.** {{Halyna}}: these words are Brenn's.
2. **The post.** Nineteen, twelve men and the warden, black wood everywhere, the warden in three sentences.
3. **The stranger.** No wind, and the boat came anyway. Three words. The gift set down like bread.
4. **The delay.** No man moved. A gull among the stones.
5. **The kick.** The dust by the wall. "I laughed. I have not laughed at anything since."
6. **The neck.** His mother's hand in fever. "If it had stopped there…"
7. **The blade.** "Seize him." "Kill him."
8. **The confession.** "His men obeyed. / We obeyed." One bare line: no blow struck, none stayed.
9. **The order.** It comes in the rhyme's words. The wood screams. Brenn gives no reason.
10. **The letter,** with the oath here if it is kept.
11. **After.** The sleeves. The bed of black wood. "I do not know why I did it." "No one left to ask but God."
12. **Seren's annal.** The name cut from the rolls; the chest carried by the youngest; the pass in one sentence; her stop. The law of doors.
13. **Close.** *We remember.* The Captain's hood goes back, once.

At v1.3's density this lands near 1,100–1,300 words, half of v2.0, with every element Jack has liked.
