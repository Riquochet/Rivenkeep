# R3 · Economy and the Archaic Register

*A research brief and rulebook for Seren's hand: how the sagas, the King James Bible and Malory say much in few words; what Le Guin and Tolkien teach about high style; what readers find hard in old English and what they learn; and three dials, HEAVY, MEDIUM and NATIVE. Jack's notes of 2026-10-02 govern all of it.*

---

## 0 · The short answer

1. **Hard through grammar and words, never through length.** The King James Bible has sounded old to every reader for four centuries, yet under 1 per cent of its roughly 12,000 distinct words are archaic (BYU Religious Studies Center). Its age lives in the grammar of every line: *thou*, *-eth*, *spake*, *knew not*. A grammar is a closed system that repeats in every sentence, so it is learned within pages; a rare word comes once and is never learned. **The recipe for "if you read long enough, you start to understand": old grammar on every line, and a small stock of old words that keeps coming back.**
2. **Economy is leaving out.** The sagas, Genesis, Samuel and Malory tell deeds, not verdicts, put the weight in one short line of speech, and leave the reason unsaid.
3. **Building is rising repetition.** Bedivere goes to the water three times; two runners come to David. Repeat a frame and raise the cost each time. Never build by piling on description.
4. **v2.0's failure is measurable** (§1.7): six times v1.3's semicolons, and tales 60 to 140 per cent longer.
5. **The canon already holds the pronoun system.** Orrowen addresses elders and bonded pairs in the plural (*va*), and the Captain addresses a True Man in the singular (*tho*), as a comrade (Orrowen Spec v2, §3.9). Early Modern *ye*/*thou* translates it exactly: the archaic register is the most faithful English for the tongue the Book is written in.

## 1 · What the masters do

### 1.1 The sagas: deeds without comment

*Read: Njal's Saga, tr. Dasent (1861); Grettir's Saga, tr. Morris and Magnússon (1869); Heimskringla, tr. Laing (1844). All public domain.*

- **The death-line goes sideways.** Thorgrim, run through by Gunnar's bill at a window-slit, walks back to his band; asked whether Gunnar is at home, he says they may find that out, but the bill is at home, and falls dead. Atli, speared on his own threshold: *"Broad spears are about now."* Thormod draws an arrow-barb from his chest, sees shreds of heart on it, says the king has fed them well, and leans back dead. No saga says anyone was brave.
- **Terse speech carries the whole weight.** Gunnar's bowstring is cut, and he asks Hallgerd for two locks of her hair. *"Does aught lie on it?" she says. "My life lies on it," he said.* She remembers a slap. A whole marriage is in four speeches; the narrator adds nothing and hands judgment to others ("all men said that he never once winced").
- **Feeling shows on the body**, as a face red or pale, a look, tears (Kirsten Wolf, *Traditio*, 2016). **Verse is where the inside speaks:** the prose keeps a script of reticence, and the skaldic verses are its one room for open feeling (Sif Rikhardsdottir, *Emotion in Old Norse Literature*, 2017). The Book has this shape already: the prose holds back, and the songs speak.
- **One picture at the hinge.** Sentenced to exile, Gunnar is thrown from his horse, looks back, and sees his fields never so fair, white to harvest. He turns home, and the turning kills him. It is the saga's one lyric image, and the plot turns on it.
- **Close hard.** "And with this ends what we have to say about Thormod."
- **Number done right.** At Njal's burning the boy Thord would rather "die with thee and Njal than … live after you": *thee* for his grandmother, *you* for the pair. That is the grammar for {{Halyna}}.

### 1.2 The King James Bible: *and*, the concrete, the unsaid

- **Parataxis** (clauses laid side by side with *and*, no *because*). Counted on the Gutenberg text for this brief: **74 per cent of the verses of Genesis begin with "And"** (1,118 of 1,508); in 2 Samuel, 57 per cent. One word in ten in Genesis is *and*, and the mean word is four letters. Robert Alter (*The Art of Biblical Narrative*, 1981; *Pen of Iron*, 2010) argues that parataxis is how the Hebrew writers saw the world, and that its art is its refusal to spell out causes.
- **The unsaid carries the weight.** Erich Auerbach (*Mimesis*, 1946, ch. 1) sets Homer, where all is lit and explained, against Genesis 22, where the three days' walk, Abraham's thought and Isaac's fear stay in shadow, and the shadow is the weight. "So they went both of them together" comes twice; the second time, after Isaac asks where the lamb is, it means something else.
- **Economy.** *Jesus wept* (John 11:35). *Thou art the man* (2 Sam. 12:7).
- **The runners: the best model for Hale and Brenn** (2 Sam. 18:19–33). A watchman sees a man running alone: *If he be alone, there is tidings in his mouth.* The king asks the first runner whether the young man Absalom is safe, and he evades. The second answers sideways: may the king's enemies be as that young man is. Then a lament that is nothing but repetition: *O my son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom!*
- **It was already old in 1611,** keeping English sixty or seventy years older; *thou* was gone from southern speech by about 1650 (BYU RSC; Wikipedia, "Thou"). A register, not street speech, and readers learned it anyway: exactly the effect Jack wants.

### 1.3 Malory: plain old narration that moves

P. J. C. Field (*Romance and Chronicle*, 1971) finds Malory's prose mostly plain declaratives joined by *and*, *but* and *then*, little subordinated, in the simple past.

- **Building by three.** Twice Bedivere hides Excalibur and lies, the second lie lovelier (*"I saw nothing but the waters wap and waves wan"*) and so worse. Arthur's anger climbs. The third time, the arm comes out of the lake.
- **The lament by ladder.** Ector over Lancelot repeats one frame eight times (*"thou were the courteoust knight that ever bare shield…"*). Then only: "Then there was weeping and dolour out of measure."
- **Diction worth taking:** *lightly* (quickly), *lief* (dear), *eft* (again), *ween* (think), *ware* (aware), *but if* (unless), *him thought* (it seemed to him).

### 1.4 Le Guin: Elfland, Poughkeepsie, crowding and leaping

From the full text of "From Elfland to Poughkeepsie" (1973):

- **Poughkeepsie is Jack's diagnosis.** It is journalistic prose applied to the remote and the elemental: a fake plainness, flat rather than simple, vague rather than clear, its rocks and wind not really there. Jack's words were "clinical" and "a history text book".
- **Style is the book.** Remove it and a plot synopsis is left. In fantasy nothing else is there, so every word counts.
- **Archaism is a high wire.** It is a perfect distancer if done perfectly, and one slip spoils it. Her catalogue of failures: *-est* on every verb (*I goest*); every *was* turned to *were*; *thee* as a subject; hypercorrect case; and fancy words (*eldritch*, *mayhap*, *perchance*, and the worst, *ichor*).
- **Mixed registers jerk the reader.** Heroes who drop into slang for a joke and climb back for earnest moments fall apart in her mind.
- **Plain language is the noblest and the hardest.** The sagas and the *Mabinogion* are as plainspoken as can be. Tolkien's English is plain, concrete and flexible, rising to the stately without strain. Dunsany's imitators manage only names, vague splendours and sentences that begin with *And*: the *and* must carry a deed.
- **Crowding and leaping** (*Steering the Craft*, ch. 10). Pack each sentence with exact sense, and leap over the rest. What is left out matters more than what is kept; only the relevant belongs.
- *Terms.* "Forsoothery" is later critics' slang, not Le Guin's. The older word is **tushery**, Stevenson's (to W. E. Henley, 1883), for romances stuffed with *tush* and *marry*.

### 1.5 Tolkien's defence

- **Letter 171** (a draft to Hugh Brogan, 1955, never sent). Answering the charge of tushery, Tolkien defines it as bogus "medieval" colour laid on with expletives, and says real archaic English is *"far more terse than modern."* He rewrites Théoden's speech in modern idiom to show the result is longer and false. A man who thinks as Théoden thinks cannot talk that way, and making him do so splits word from meaning, which is a kind of insincerity.
- **"On Translating Beowulf"** (1940). The poem's diction was already poetic, archaic and artificial when it was made, so a translator must avoid both the colloquial and the obscure. *That is Seren's position: a literary register, older than the tellers' speech, and chosen.*
- **Appendix F.** He hinted at familiar forms with *thou* in only one or two places, warning that modern ears hear *thou* as formal, the reverse of its old nearness.
- **How would Tolkien tell IV.3?** Tersely. He would let the men who think in the old way speak in it, put the dreadful thing in one plain sentence, and not explain it.

### 1.6 What readers struggle with, and what they learn

- **False friends are worse than dead words.** Crystal (*Think on My Words*, 2008) puts Shakespeare's truly difficult words at about 1,000, 5 to 10 per cent of his vocabulary, and singles out false friends (*naughty*, *merely*). An obsolete word announces itself; a false friend misleads in silence.
- **The density of rare words, not syntax, is the main obstacle.** Shakespeare has about one rare word in eleven, against one in seventeen in a modern rendering, and inverted order proves manageable (Full Measure Press). The Folger editors (Mowat and Werstine) teach a handful of patterns: verb before subject, object first, words separated or left out. Patterns can be learned.
- **Context teaches words that recur.** Readers of *A Clockwork Orange*, given no warning of a test, recognised about 76 per cent of 90 *nadsat* words afterwards (Saragi, Nation and Meister, *System*, 1978). Words met fewer than eight times were not kept three months later, and full learning takes 20 to 30 meetings (Waring and Takaki, 2003). Burgess sets each new word where its slot and neighbours narrow its meaning, and glosses almost nothing; the American edition's glossary was added without him (Vincent and Clarke, 2017).

**Hard but learnable:** frequent forms in one consistent system; new words pinned by context, recurring. **Merely irritating:** rare showy words, inconsistency, false friends, hypercorrection, registers switching in one mouth, archaism as decoration.

## 2 · Twelve laws of economy (in every dial)

1. **Old through grammar and words, never through length.** Full stops. Sentence mean 10–15 words for a teller, 12–18 in Seren's narration, none over about 30; at most one semicolon a paragraph. No tale longer than its v1.3 self; most shorter.
2. **Every *and* carries a deed.** "He laid his hand on the stone, and it held, and he climbed." An *and* that adds only description is padding.
3. **Deeds, not verdicts.** No *terrible*, *cruel*, *heartbreaking*, *brave* in narration. Feeling shows on the body (the face whitened; the hand would not open); judgment goes to others ("they who saw it said…").
4. **The weight goes in one short line of speech, said sideways.** A death-line is under ten words and never explains itself.
5. **Understatement once a tale, at the hottest point.** Bracher's classic study (*PMLA*, 1937) shows it at the heart of Old English poetry; *Beowulf*'s "that was a good king" is the model.
6. **Build by three.** Repeat a frame, change one thing, raise the cost; the third time breaks.
7. **Leave the reason out.** Never invent a custom to explain a deed; it muddies it, as Jack said of the burning boat. A weak man's cruelty is shown by what he does. *Illustration, not canon:* not "In his cowardice the warden had the boat destroyed", but "▒▒▒▒ bade them burn the boat."
8. **One picture per movement.** Seren's painted image: concrete, sensory, short, at the hinge, like Gunnar's look back at his fields. Never a run of similes.
9. **The songs say what the prose withholds.** They stay verbatim.
10. **Close hard.** The last line is a flat fact; then the hearth: *We remember.*
11. **One register for the Book; a voice for each teller.** Voices come from rhythm, concern and idiom, never from switching register. Kael counts and swears. Hale runs, slipping into the present at the moment of action, as Dasent's Gunnar "sees" and "thrusts". Voss asks. Brenn's *we* is the whole post. No teller drops into modern speech for a joke.
12. **Every archaic form right.** One wrong *-est* spoils a page; check §3's tables.

**Fixed matter.** Jack's fixed lines (*In memory of our home, our families, our God, our freedoms, our peace.* · *The mortar cries out from the ground. Rise, Stonewrights! Lay stone upon stone. Stand every wall to the very end.* · *Rise! Rise! Rise!* · *We remember our home too.* · *We remember.*) and every song stay **verbatim in every dial**, never archaized. **Plain Words** uses no archaism (§3.4).

**Pair-names.** {{Halyna}} take plural verbs (HEAVY: "{{Halyna}} are come", "{{Halyna}} say", never "saith"); a pair is addressed *ye*/*you*/*your* or "ye two"; they speak as "we two"; one of them alone may be *thou*. No singular *they*: for an unknown person, *whoso*, *he that*, *one*.

**No Christian marks.** *God* (Mardh) only. Ban *LORD*, *Christ*, *amen*, *saint*, *church*, *gospel*, *christen*, *rood*, and every tushery oath, which are Christian oaths anyway (*zounds*, "God's wounds"; *marry*, "by Mary").

## 3 · The three dials

### 3.1 HEAVY: Early Modern grammar throughout, in narration and speech

**What it is.** The manner of the KJV and Malory. *Thou*/*ye* in all speech and address. *-est*/*-eth* in every present-tense verb. Old strong pasts, *be*-perfects, negation without *do*, questions by inversion. Vocabulary capped at §3.2's list plus the HEAVY additions below.

**Table H1 · Pronouns**

| | Subject | Object | Possessive before a consonant | Before a vowel or *h*; standing alone | Self |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 sg | I | me | my | mine (*mine eyes*; *it is mine*) | myself |
| 2 sg: one near hearer (kin, friend, comrade, child); God; rite; oath; a foe in scorn | **thou** | **thee** | **thy** | **thine** (*thine hand*; *it is thine*) | thyself |
| 3 sg | he · she · it | him · her · it | his · her · his/its | his · hers | himself · herself · itself |
| 1 pl | we | us | our | ours | ourselves |
| 2 pl: many; **any pair**; one held in honour (an elder, the Captain) | **ye** | **you** | your | yours | yourselves |
| 3 pl | they | them | their | theirs | themselves |

*This is the canon's own system (Orrowen §3.9): elders and bonded pairs get **ye**; the Captain gives a True Man **thou**. A switch from you to thou mid-speech marks a rush of love or scorn, as between Shakespeare's Gertrude and Hamlet: rare, and always for a reason.*

**Syntax**

- **The *be*-perfect for verbs of motion and change:** *he is come*, *they are gone*, *the night was fallen*.
- **Negation without *do*:** *I know not*, *he looked not up*, *fear not*. Use *do* only for emphasis.
- **Questions by inversion:** *Knowest thou the way? Came he alone? Whither goest thou?*
- **The subjunctive after *if*, *though*, *lest*, *till*, and in wishes:** *if it be so*, *though he were dead*, *lest the wall fall*, *till the day break*, *God keep thee*. Do not use it anywhere else.
- **Inversion after an adverb placed first:** *Then came he. Now is the hour come. Thus spake Kael.*
- **The imperative may take its pronoun:** *Go thou. Stand ye fast.* The reflexive *sat him down* is also allowed.
- **Also allowed:** *which* for persons; *whoso* for whoever; *his* for *its*, but only where it cannot be misread.

**Word-list.** All of §3.2's list, plus: *wist* (knew); *wot* / *wottest* / *wotteth* (know); *quoth* (said); *hight* (was named); *me thinketh* (2 Sam. 18:27); *what time* (when); *peradventure* (it may be); *holden*, *gotten*, *stricken* as participles. Use these sparingly: two or three of them in a tale.

**HEAVY failures**

1. **Endings on the wrong person:** *I goest*, *they goeth*, *{{Halyna}} saith*, *he willeth*.
2. **Case confused:** *thee* as subject, *thou* as object, *ye* as object ("I bid ye").
3. **Thou and you mixed** to one hearer without a reason; ***-s* and *-eth* mixed** in one mouth.
4. **Hypercorrection** (*to he who*) and **the subjunctive everywhere** (every *was* become *were*).
5. **Scripture pastiche:** *it came to pass*, *and lo*, *verily*, *begat*, *saith the Lord*.
6. **Length dressed as archaism:** semicolon chains and Homeric similes (the v2.0 failure); **Yoda order** outside verse; *ye olde*.

**HEAVY demonstration** (about 120 words)

> The sun was gone down behind the ridge, and the wall stood black against the last light. A man came to it alone. He looked not up. He laid his hand upon the lowest stone, and it held, and he clomb.
>
> Half the face was fallen. Where it was fallen he went by the stones that stood out of the mortar. Twice a stone turned under his foot, and twice he found another.
>
> "Come down," one cried from the ward. "The light faileth, and thou canst not see thine hand."
>
> "I need not see it," said he. "I know where it is."
>
> At the top the wind found him, and there he kneeled. "Thou that holdest the high places, hold me yet a little."

### 3.2 MEDIUM: archaic words and inversion throughout; *thou* only where it is close or sacred

**What it is.** Modern grammar by default, with archaic words and inverted order throughout. *Thou* only in intimate address, oath, prayer and rite, and then fully conjugated; *-eth* only in set phrases. Harder than v1.3, lighter than the KJV, nearest Tolkien's high speech.

**Table M1 · The grammar**

| Feature | MEDIUM rule | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Ordinary address, to one or many | *you*, *your* | "Ere long you will not see your own hand." |
| Intimate address, prayer, oath, rite, scorn | *thou*/*thee*/*thy*/*thine*, with the **full** forms of Tables H2 and H3 | "Thou that holdest the high places…" |
| A pair or a company, in rite or oath | *ye* (subject) · *you* (object) | "Stand ye at the stone, ye two." |
| Third-person *-eth* | **Set phrases only:** a proverb, the words of a rite, *hath*/*doth*/*saith* when an elder is quoted; never in narration | *A wall that leaneth is a wall that falleth* (illustration, not canon) |
| Strong pasts | modern forms, plus a short list still felt as old: *bade*, *slew*/*slain*, *smote*, *wrought*, *sprang*, *clove*, *hewn*, *rent*, *durst* | "He bade them stand." |
| Negation | *verb + not* may be used freely; *do* is also allowed | "He looked not up." |
| Questions | inversion allowed | "Came he alone?" |
| Inversion | at most one sentence in three or four: an adjective or adverb first, or a verb before its subject | "Black it stood." "Broken was the face." |
| *Be*-perfect | with verbs of motion and change | "The hour is come." |
| Subjunctive | in conditions and wishes only | "were I", "if it be", "lest it fall" |

**Word-list.** These are the core words, to be used freely. They are meant to recur until the reader owns them.

| Time | Place | Logic and linking | Quantity and degree | Verbs and nouns |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **ere** (before) | **nigh** (near; nearly) | **wherefore** (why: only in questions) | **naught** (nothing) | **bide**, **abide** (wait, stay, endure) |
| **ere long** (soon) | **yonder** | **therefore** | **aught** (anything) | **tarry** (linger) |
| **anon** (at once) | **hither**, **thither**, **whither** | **lest** | **save** (except) | **bade**, **bid** (ordered) |
| **betimes** (early) | **hence**, **thence**, **whence** | **albeit** | **sore** (greatly: *sore afraid*) | **ween**, **deem** (think, judge) |
| **the morrow** | **betwixt**, **amid** | **howbeit** (however) | **exceeding** (*exceeding wet*) | **wax** (grow: *the night waxed cold*) |
| **even**, **eventide** | **afar** | **withal** (besides; with it) | **full** (*full weary*) | **smite**, **slay**, **hew**, **rend**, **cleave** |
| **oft** | **unto**, **upon** | **for** (because) | **yea**, **nay** | **kin**, **kinsman**, **bane**, **boon**, **doom** (judgment), **fell** (fierce) |
| **wont** (custom; used to) | **thereof**, **therein**, **whereon** | **whoso** (whoever) | **fain**, **lief** (gladly) | **methinks**, **methought** |

*Seasoning words, no more than two in a tale:* *it booted not* (it was no use); *rede* (counsel); *lightly* (quickly, from Malory); *eft* (again); *ware* (aware); *but if* (unless); *him thought* (it seemed to him).

**False friends.** Use none of these in any dial unless the sentence itself fixes the sense. Each reads as modern English and misleads without a sound: *want* (lack), *still* (always), *presently* (at once), *prevent* (go before), *let* (hinder), *suffer* (allow), *meat* (food), *quick* (alive), *sad* (grave), *silly* (innocent), *nice* (foolish), *fond* (foolish), *naughty* (wicked), *conversation* (way of life), *careful* (anxious), *awful* (full of awe), *weird* (fate).

**MEDIUM failures**

1. **Thou for distance or formality** (the modern misreading); here it means nearness, God or rite.
2. **Thou unconjugated:** *thou are*, *thou will*, *thou holds*. **-eth in narration:** "the wind bloweth".
3. **Inversion on every line**, or **old words stacked** ("ere yonder nigh the morrow"): spread them so they recur.
4. **Poughkeepsie leaking in** (*okay*, *really*, *a lot*, *focus*, *process*) and **Tolkien's insincerity**: modern thought in old dress (*trauma*, *closure*).

**MEDIUM demonstration** (about 120 words)

> The sun was down behind the ridge ere he came to the wall, and black it stood against the last light. Alone he came. He looked not up, but laid his hand on the lowest stone, and it held.
>
> Broken was the face for half its height. Where the stones had fallen he climbed by those that stood out of the mortar. Twice a stone turned under his foot. Twice he found another.
>
> "Come down," a man called from the ward. "Ere long you will not see your own hand."
>
> "I need not see it," he said. "I know where it is."
>
> At the top the wind found him, and there he knelt. "Thou that holdest the high places, hold me a little longer."

### 3.3 NATIVE: MEDIUM plus a small stock of Orrowen words left untranslated

**What it is.** MEDIUM grammar plus at most a dozen Orrowen words left untranslated and learned from context, as *nadsat* is. The stock can also be laid over HEAVY.

**The rule for choosing.** Seren leaves a word untranslated only where **its Orrowen sense is wider than any one English word**. Every word comes from the canon lexicon (Orrowen Spec v2, §5); never invent words, or lore with them.

**Table N1 · The candidate stock**

| Word | Sense (from the lexicon) | Why English cannot hold it |
|---|---|---|
| ***trenn*** | a course of stones; a line of writing; a tale laid in one night | course, line and tale in one: a tale laid is a course in the wall |
| ***lodh*** | mortar; the mortar line; the bond (*Lodh Helv*, the Aetherbond) | mortar and bond in one; teach the mortar first |
| ***keth*** | hold, keep; know (of the wood) | to know the wood is to hold it |
| ***myst*** | sea-fog; the grey | colour, fog and the enemy's country in one |
| ***ston*** | "It stands": the word that closes an oath | a whole vow in one syllable |
| ***hoskvoll*** | a pair-name, a "lintel-name" | explains the {{Name}} drawn under a lintel |
| ***sullast*** | the unbonded third of a hearth | Seren's own state |
| ***haskard*** | a runner | Hale's and Brenn's office: a runner carries what is seen, never commands (I.3) |
| ***arra*** | a guest | the Guest of IV.4 |
| ***tumar*** | yes (literally "we remember") | spoken yes **only**; the hearth's *We remember.* stays in English, always |

|---|---|

| Pace | At most one new word a tale; never two in one sentence |
| Glosses | None in the Book; Plain Words and the Book of Knowings may gloss |
| Form | Always italic, as the Book sets *Tumar*; no English *-s* (use the lexicon's *-ath*/*-eth*, or the singular) |
| Wood leaves | Only in {{Halyna}}'s own voice (*keth*, *lodh*), never as the wood's word: the wood knows only the Mystaeri side |
| Concrete first | *lodh* as mortar in early tales; *the lodh between {{Halyna}}* later |

**NATIVE failures**

1. **Too many words, or too few meetings** (under eight, never learned).
2. **Glossing every use**, or a first use the sentence cannot decode.
3. **Words or lore invented outside the lexicon**; Orrowen put in place of a fixed line.
4. **English inflection on Orrowen:** *trenns*.

**NATIVE demonstration** (about 120 words)

> The sun was down behind the ridge ere he came to the wall, and off the sea the *myst* was rising, grey on grey. He laid his hand on the lowest stone, and it held.
>
> Broken was the face. Where a whole *trenn* had fallen he climbed by the *trenn* above it, feeling for stones whose *lodh* was sound. Twice the *lodh* crumbled under his foot. Twice he found another hold.
>
> "Come down," a man called from the ward. "In the *myst* you will not see your own hand."
>
> "I need not see it," he said. "I know where it is."
>
> At the top he set his palm flat on the capstone. "*Ston*," he said, as a man ends an oath. The wall stood.

*(Four stock words in 120 is denser than the Book should be, to show the method; in the Book, about one in 150 words.)*

---

### 3.4 Plain Words, beside it

Plain Words is modern English a twelve-year-old can read: short words, contractions, normal order, no archaism. It is a true **retelling**: it may say what the Book only implies and reorder sentences, and it keeps every event, fixed line and song. The MEDIUM passage, retold:

> The sun had set behind the ridge by the time he reached the wall. It stood black against the last of the light. He came alone. He didn't look up. He put his hand on the bottom stone, and it held.
>
> Half the wall had fallen away. Where the stones were gone, he climbed on the ones that still stuck out. Twice a stone rolled under his foot. Twice he found another.
>
> "Come down!" a man shouted from the yard. "Soon you won't be able to see your own hand."
>
> "I don't need to see it," he said. "I know where it is."
>
> At the top the wind hit him, and he knelt. "You who hold the high places," he prayed, "hold me a little longer."

## 4 · Banned in every dial

| Kind | Words |
|---|---|
| Tushery and fake oaths | forsooth, prithee, egad, gadzooks, zounds, marry, 'sblood, by my troth, by'r lady, tush, pish, ye olde |
| Le Guin's fancy words | mayhap, perchance, eldritch, tenebrous, smaragd, ichor (and use *it may be* in place of *haply*) |
| Scripture formulas | it came to pass, and lo, verily, begat, unto this day, thus saith the Lord |
| Christian marks | Christ, Jesu, LORD, amen, saint, church, gospel, christen, rood, Holy Ghost |
| Game-flavoured or misleading | wight, weird (as fate), aye (it is ambiguous; use *yea* or *ever*) |
| The false friends of §3.2 | unless the sentence itself fixes the old sense |

---

## 5 · Recommendation

Set the Book at **HEAVY**, vocabulary held to §3.2's list plus the HEAVY additions: the KJV's own recipe of old grammar on every line and few old words. It gives the widest gap from Plain Words, it is the honest English for Orrowen's *tho* and *va*, and a first-time reader stumbles on it and, a few pages in, begins to read. Lay the **NATIVE** stock over it once calibration reads clean; if a sample reads as Bible pastiche, step down to **MEDIUM**. In every dial the twelve laws of §2 hold, and no tale grows to make up the difference.
