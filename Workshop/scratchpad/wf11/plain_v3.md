## OF THIS BOOK

*By Seren Two-Inks, who writes this Book and is the third member of the household of {{Halyna}}.*

The nights are short. The hall sleeps, the Long Hearth is down to coals, and on the wall the banner lifts in a wind I can't hear.

The Book is half full, and one of its tellers, who held a wet finger to every wind, already lies under the east cairns. Tonight I turned back to the first page. I left it blank, because you don't cut a lintel, the stone over a door, before the posts are up.

The Keep's first builders laid their tales at this hearth, and when they left, the tales went cold. Now we sit where they sat, warming idle hands.

The stone in the hollow beside me was the inner gate's top stone. It lay face down in the gateway, its vow in the dirt, until {{Halyna}} turned it at dusk on the day we raised the banner. It takes two to turn it, and the vow is still sharp.

::: native legend_stonwryt_hal line
The Stonwryt, the first builders' vow, on the underside of the tale-stone, in their old writing, the Hal
*This wall will stand. We two laid it, and we two answer for it, as long as stone is stone. It stands.* It is signed with one pair-name under one lintel: {{Aldwena}}, who laid that top stone.
:::

So I write each bonded pair under one stroke, like a lintel on two posts, one name for two people. Rhyna of the Inner Gate and Halvard Stone-Warden are {{Halyna}}. {{Idrenna}}, the oldest, taught them, and nod by the door when a tale is true. Our wall-masters, {{Orvenna}}, never shout. If one of {{Enrella}}, the youngest, starts a joke, the other finishes it.

We say a tale is *laid*, not told. A told tale is gone by morning, but a laid one is part of the wall.

Every tale gets the same two words in answer: *We remember.* On the first night of tales, nobody knew what to say, until old Ebba of Fenholm said them into the quiet. Nobody asked her, or asked us to follow, but the hearth took them up. She's still first most nights, a breath early, and we follow like the tide behind its first wave.

Forgetting just came over our fathers, who never chose it. But we choose to remember, the way a mason chooses which stones to keep.

Some tales are the wood's, mostly from wreck-wood. Records forget, and can be made to lie. The wood can't lie, but it can't see fear. One tale came from a dark stone in the chest at my feet. {{Wendhessa}} sealed that chest and carried it out when Eldhythe fell. No pair would break that seal until the wood showed {{Halyna}} what the silent stones beside it were, and then {{Idrenna}} said yes.

The wood isn't read. It's known whole, like a memory, so {{Halyna}} pass it on in pieces, one picking up where the other stops. I write the pieces in two inks, and they won't let me mark which ink is whose. You can't tell. From the pieces I build the tale, then write down the hearth's silence after it.

{{Halyna}} want the wood's page on the right, facing ours.

<!-- HALYNA -->

"A tale from just one side of a wall,"

"is only half a tale."

<!-- HALYNA END -->

Where the wood's page can't be told yet, I've left only the grain, the shape of what we still don't understand.

I'm not Bonded, though {{Halyna}} made me their third. I hold the lamp while they know the wood. I laid my hand on the grain once, and let go. My hand is for writing, not knowing. Every writer before me was half of a pair. I write alone.

The Captain hears everything, says nothing, and keeps his hood up.

I haven't patched broken carvings with guesses. I've blotted out names the guild cut away, and where the wood speaks, I've kept its own words for things.

I set each tale into our language like a mason laying a row, each joint over solid stone. I say each word aloud three times first, to hear if it holds. I read each page back to its teller. The tale is his and the words are mine, and no one has asked for his words back. Most nights, like tonight, the coals are ash before my last word is out.

I love our language.

I carried two things up the mountain, our language and a chest. Only the chest was heavy.

For now this Book is ink. {{Halyna}} want it carved into this hall's granite, row on row, when peace comes. Until then it stays in the chest with the silent stones.

## BOOK ONE · THE BOOK OF THE WALL

*How we made our stand.*

### I.1 · The Torn Cloak

*Kael the Counter laid this on the first night the hearth asked for a story, and he told me not to change a word. I changed plenty, and when I read it back to him, he swore it was exactly what he'd said.*

Give me the stone. No, I'll stand. I've sat long enough this winter.

I swear on the stone over the western stair, where the old wardens carved their names. I'm no singer. I count heads, stones and days, and what I didn't count, I won't tell.

The column came in at dusk, and I heard it before I saw it: many feet, and not one voice. From the gate I counted old men, women with bundles, children asleep on their feet, and soldiers from six broken companies who no longer knew their officers. The Captain came last, because he'd guarded the rear the whole way. Two thousand and a few hundred in all.

We'd walked a month from burnt-out Glasspire to reach a castle, and found rubble. The gate hung off one hinge and the yard was knee-deep in grass. The great hall from the songs had no roof, and snow lay on the hearth where you're sitting.

Some shook, some cried, and some fell down and wouldn't get up. Then the shouting started. Some wanted to beg the grey sails for peace, though nobody alive understood a word they said. Others wanted to cross the high passes into the snow, or hide in the cellars, as if the sea would forget us.

I'd heard soldiers break on the long retreat, but never a whole people. It sounds like river ice cracking up in spring.

A bitter wind came down off the split ridge. People wanted a leader, but the generals were dead, the lords had scattered, and even the Stonewrights, our builders, sat in the grass holding their chisels.

Nobody looked at the Captain. He only led one small company, and that was us. All the way here he'd waited for the real leaders to take over, but they were dead. Don't get me wrong, there was never a greater man. He just didn't want to lead, and I swear to that.

<!-- PROLOGUE EXCERPT BEGIN -->
The True Men looked at the Captain, and the Captain was looking at the wall.

He had a dead coal from this hearth in his hand, and nobody knew why. He climbed alone, using the stones that stuck out, and we let him, since he hadn't asked us to follow. I've counted them since: thirty-one stones, four of them loose.

On top he took off his cloak. It had been red once, before the road faded it to the colour of brick, and it was all he had against that wind. The yard went quiet. A child coughed, his mother shushed him, and then there was only the wind.

He tore the cloak in two, down the back.

Old cloth makes only a small sound when it rips, but I heard it over the wind.

He spread one half on the wall-top, knelt on it, and wrote with the coal, slowly, like a man not used to writing. He tied it to a pike from the rubble and put the blank half back on his shoulders.

I was next up, so I read it first. His hand hadn't shaken once, but my voice shook as I read it out to the yard. I won't pretend it didn't.

I counted who answered, and in what order. A count doesn't count until it has names. The True Men answered first. The Captain had found us one by one, in forts and on roads, and held us with nothing but trust. We kept our word, built what we promised, and fought only for a just cause. That's all a True Man is: not much, but not nothing.

Stannard of the ridge, Corlen of the harbour, Tamm of the three spans, Marl the fen-born. Aske of the canopy, Garvel of the smithies, Harl of the burning shore, Ulden of the galleries. Hesk of the mere, Lanner of the crags, Pellow of the spire. And me, Kael the Counter, the oath-keeper, from no haven. The Captain found me on a road. Twelve of us. I counted twice, and it didn't take long.

Next the six companies answered, a troop for each of us, and they trust us the way we trust him. Then the rest of the column, who'd carried their children through the passes on their backs. One voice, then a dozen, then hundreds, more than I could count. So I stopped counting, the only time I ever have.

He never gave an order. He only wrote down what we still had, and it was enough. Our pennants are red, here and on every keep we raise, the colour of that cloak.

Look at the end of the bench, you children in front. That's him, with his hood up.

The other half is on his back.

He'll tell you it wasn't like that. It was like that.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

### I.2 · The Cry of the Bonded

*This is my own tale, the first I ever laid, and the hearth had heard only one before it. On the day I'm telling about, I was twelve years old and a child of the guild.*

I was scared to speak tonight, and {{Halyna}} put my hands on the stone.

<!-- HALYNA -->

"Hold the stone."

"Hold it, and it will hold you."

<!-- HALYNA END -->

So I'm holding it. At dusk on the day of the banner, the soldiers cheered while hundreds of us Stonewrights sat silent in the grass with our chisels.

I had no trade yet, so I carried the water. Over the mountains I'd walked and slept beside a guild boy who knotted nets as he went. I came into the yard alone. Soon after me, Enno and Della came in holding hands. They were fifteen, the only children's pair to get up the mountain whole, and they sat side by side and held on.

In twenty months we'd watched ten havens, our cities, die. We left each one with our chisels packed and never lifted a hand, because our faith said *Build, don't destroy. Protect, don't attack.* Mortar and chisels were all we had, and they weren't enough. Twelve generations of faith still hung on us like a wet cloak, and every elder had the same hard question. If walls built for peace must be held with blood, whose blood, whose hands?

Back then I only knew {{Halyna}} as two elders sitting by themselves under the broken inner gate. They didn't talk to anyone, because they didn't need to. Her family built that gate and his cut its stone, and they were husband and wife, one since birth.

Then the Captain came down off the wall. He wasn't tall, and he had coal-black fingers and half a cloak. I'd watched his banner go up without knowing what it meant for us. He looked from the elders to the broken gate, then asked one question, the only speech of his anyone here can repeat.

"Your walls are falling. Will you build, or will you watch and die?"

Nobody answered, and all I remember is the wind in the grass.

Then {{Halyna}} stood up.

They looked up at the torn cloak and bowed to it together. Their two chisels rose as one into the last light, and she began the cry, and he finished it.

<!-- HALYNA -->

"The mortar cries out from the ground. Rise, Stonewrights! Lay stone upon stone. Stand every wall to the very end."

"Rise! Rise! Rise!"

<!-- HALYNA END -->

It was one cry from two throats, and hers came first only because a cry has to start somewhere. Some tell it as call and answer, with silence in between.

There was no silence.

The guild rose like an arch when its wooden frame is knocked away, every stone leaning on the next. I stood with them, though I don't remember doing it. The soldiers took up our cry, and then the column did. Old stone doesn't shake, but I felt it shake under my feet.

That night, by this hearth's first fire in years, {{Idrenna}} made a deal with the Captain while I brought in the water. They spoke slowly, as old people do, and he didn't rush them.

The Stonewrights wouldn't carry weapons, and he didn't ask them to, but they'd build between volleys and mend walls under fire. The guild still puts it this way: *with the speed and precision that only Aetherbonded pairs could achieve*. {{Idrenna}} gave it to him in halves.

"Not the sword, but the stone."

"Not the killing, but the keeping."

The Captain bowed, and the guild has said it ever since.

Before the fire died, {{Orvenna}} cut the siege day into three, like a quarryman splitting stone along its crack.

"Every gun you haul is a row of stone we don't lay," they told him.

"So spend each gun the way a careful man spends silver."

*Then the masons sang, as they still do:*

> *Lay the stone while the sea is low.*  
> *Haul the guns while the guns are cold.*  
> *Hold the wall when the grey sails come,*  
> *and the wall will hold.*

If you see a Stonewright on the wall in a holding, look again. You'll see two.

Later, by hissing torches, we laid the war's first row across the broken inner gate, each stone over a joint below. {{Orvenna}} wanted the clearest voices for the havens' old gate-song, so Enno and Della stood up. They shared the lead, one singing up to the comma and one the rest.

*Then they sang it, with the guild joining in:*

> *Stone on stone, and the stone stays true.*  
> *Hand over hand, and the hand holds on.*  
> *Two at the gate when the grey sails gather.*  
> *Two at the gate, and the gate stands strong.*

I sang up to the comma. Then I sang the rest.

Enno and Della fell asleep against the new row as the torches burned down, still holding hands as they had on the mountain. I slept near the water buckets.

By morning the mortar had set.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

### I.3 · The Captain's Long Breath

*Hale the runner told this at seventeen, in his first weeks on the wall. He told it standing up, as fast as he runs, and I've written it down slower.*

Give me the stone, then. I'm no storyteller. I run.

Every haven had a runners' stone, and in our first week we swore the old oath on ours, a fallen block near the inner gate. "What I saw, I carry. I carry nothing else." Wick, our youngest and fastest, swore last, and he loves nothing more than carrying word to the Captain.

On the morning of the first attack, I was at Kael's guns, still our only guns. The Captain told Kael where to watch, what to load and when to hold. Then he went to the far end of the wall, and Kael put a hand on my shoulder.

"Only his own voice beside me can change that, lad, and nobody can stand in two places."

*Then the troops practised the three calls, as they still do:*

> *Move your spot, and watch new water.*  
> *Change your shot for the sea, the shore, the sky.*  
> *Hold, or fire, when the Captain is by.*

On the east wall, Jory of Eldhythe whistled a tune the old ship-masters hate. Merrick, the oldest of them, was coiling rope below and told him to stop.

"It's only a rhyme," Jory laughed.

Merrick didn't laugh.

The sails of my first holding came before mid-morning, too fast to count. In the laying, hours drag because stone is heavy. They drag worse in the hauling, but in a holding an hour goes by in a heartbeat.

Late in that holding, when the other ships were finished, Kael's lookout spotted a hull through one of Pellow's spyglasses. It had waited in the grey, as no ship had before, and now it came in late and alone.

The smoke hid it from the Captain, so Kael gave me three words: "Hull hangs back."

I ran it.

I run. Head down under the smoke, past the masons and the ropes, not speaking, not stopping, up into clear air above the gate.

The Captain is standing still.

What I remember first is everything on the wall moving except him. He stood facing the sea with one hand on the stone. He never turned round, but he heard every word.

He set off for Kael's guns without hurrying, with me at his shoulder, and the hour started to stretch. It didn't stop, only slowed, like a stone cart on a long hill, until a boy behind could count the spokes. I can't say it better.

Water poured off the late hull's bow. I counted the ripples on its grey sail the way you count rings in a sawn log, and each took a minute, I think. The smoke didn't move, the gulls hung, and he kept walking.

I had time to get scared, then time to feel ashamed of it, then time to stop.

He stopped beside Kael, who was quietly counting, and gave the call in his own voice.

"Hold."

The guns held while the masons below kept laying stone in pairs and never looked up. The late hull kept coming, until its timbers shone wet.

"Loose."

The hour let go of us slowly, like a held breath let out, and the guns fired. The hull broke apart right where the Captain wanted it, where it couldn't hurt anyone.

I don't know what the Captain was thinking. I know what he did.

That evening, walking the tide-line as runners do after a holding, I found marks cut into a plank in the shallows. I carried them to {{Halyna}} past the fires: "Marks on the wood." I ran it.

Coming back that night, I followed Hesk's lamp on the north wall, his mother's lamp, and knew the north was awake.

Along the wall the men say, "For the Captain, an hour lasts as long as a day." I think I started it, though Voss says it's older. They say the Rite gave it to him, and I did too, before I knew better. Kael fixed it, and I carry his words and nothing else.

"It's not the world that slows down, lad. It's the Captain who won't hurry. The moment he chose was the moment that came."

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

### I.4 · The Rite of the Cornerstone

*Told by Seren Two-Inks, the third in {{Halyna}}'s household. In the Fourteen Nights I was a child of the guild, and nobody trusted me with anything but water.*

Hold the stone, Seren. I tell myself that every time.

A moving light woke me three nights after the cry. Rhyna was up, and at that moment Halvard took the lamp. I didn't call out but watched it sink to a spark down a well.

Rhyna's mother used to sing {{Aldwena}}'s family rhyme over their cradle, but nobody asked what it meant.

> *Under the hall, and under the floor,*  
> *under the stairs that go down no more,*  
> *down where the rope let the first stone go,*  
> *the first two keep what the first two know.*

Rhyna knew where to look. She let out the rope as Halvard went down the shaft, and neither spoke.

They told me a black stone chest stood at the bottom, under dust a finger thick. Halvard wiped the lid with his sleeve.

It was the mark of {{Aldwena}}.

The chest held the Rite on slates, slates about building for war, the founders' record and a staff. Halvard shook the three stone flasks, but nothing moved.

By the fire {{Halyna}} read out what the slates meant, a line, a breath, a line. I put down my bucket and forgot it.

"which sets a keeper over a great work, to build and keep it till it stands."

Then the staff's rule.

<!-- HALYNA -->

"Each staff is made for one work and never leaves it,"

The slates below made them pause longer: walls sloped so shots bounce off, gates slanted against rams, stonework laid to fall outward if broken. The first builders had known war, but the slates don't say what they fought.

The Rite needed three oils nobody alive had made. For the cedar, Stannard led {{Halyna}} through snow to the grove that gave the first staff. They wouldn't cut a live tree, so he rapped the fallen trunks with a knuckle and listened. "I know which ones will hold."

They boiled down the heartwood here, and on wet nights the stones still smell of it.

We children scraped the pale crust off long-forgotten stones with old chisels. Masons call it *bloom*, because it flowers where nobody comes. It was my first job that wasn't water, and it rubbed my fingers raw.

The mountain had no myrrh. {{Idrenna}} had carried a flask of it all the way for the guild's dead, who couldn't be buried on the pass. Rhyna warmed it into the cedar for the Captain, and the hall went still, like a street when a coffin passes.

I knew who it had been for.

All night {{Idrenna}}'s empty flask sat on the hearthstone, and nobody touched it. The Captain came in late from the wall, mortar to his wrists, looked at it and left.

The fourteenth day brought the first thin sun since the banner. The new capstone, cut by Stannard and smoothed by {{Orvenna}}, lay in the yard with {{Idrenna}} beside it, flasks on their knees. The Captain stood where the slate put him, hand on the stone, facing the people.

He didn't like it. He stood there.

Over the gate the torn cloak rose once and fell, and I heard snow-melt in the old gutters.

Then {{Idrenna}} got up, each leaning on the other, and four old hands emptied the flasks onto his bowed head. All winter their hands had shaken, but not on the flasks. One named the oils, and the other said what they meant:

"Cedar, for endurance. Granite-bloom, for strength. Myrrh, for the burden of command."

The oil soaked his collar, and he let it. At the front we children showed each other our raw hands and smiled, our first smiles since the passes.

{{Idrenna}} offered the guild's staff, polished wood capped with fine polished stone. Every leader of a great Stonewright work has carried one since the founding, and this was the first. The Captain looked at it a long while before reaching out.

Four hands laid it in two.

With it he was named Commander of Rivenkeep, and stood still in the shouting.

That night, by my lamp, {{Halyna}} carved their mark and vow under the new stone, one holding the chisel, the other striking. Their mark isn't mine to draw. Next to the vow they put words that aren't a vow, copied stroke for stroke from the cloak.

The writing was the Captain's, big and slow, and neither of them had corrected one stroke.

The True Men set the stone in the gate, mark down. The Captain propped the staff in the gatehouse corner, because the wall and the war were his work. He went back to the wall in his half-cloak. Nobody's moved the staff since. If you doubt me, go and see the dust on it.

With the stone up and the tools clean, {{Halyna}} sent me down alone with the lamp to put the slates back. I went down where I'd seen their light go, and lifted the empty flasks.

There was one more slate.

Its letters were big and careful like a child's, and mine still are. I brought it up in my shirt, cold on my skin. By the fire {{Halyna}} read me what the youngest apprentice of the First Days wrote.

### I.5 · The Naming of the Rivenmen

*Told by Voss who asked, of Eldhythe, True Man of the harbour battery. He grew up in his grandmother's harbour-house on the dock.*

Give me the stone. I've owed this hearth my answer for years.

Late that first winter I put a friend under stones on the east face, where a spade won't dig. As boys we'd turned the skipping rope for the little ones on the dock, and I laid his last stone myself.

His name was Jory.

That night, cold, I asked this fire a hard question: "Why do we fight for ice and stone?"

Stannard knocked on the hearthstone once and said, "It's not the ice I'm fighting for." Stannard's answer was good, but it was his, and mine took the whole war to carve. My grandmother said a slow answer is like a slow tide, washing across the same stones.

A week after the Rite, the whole Keep gathered in the yard below the torn cloak. I was in the slush with the companies, and Jory, for once in his life, wasn't whistling. The Captain was among us, without the staff.

Kael the Counter climbed the rubble by the gate and said one thing, once.

"The Commander fought."

The whole guild finished it together, from {{Idrenna}} to the smallest water-carrier:

"The Stonewrights built."

That was the whole speech, and it was enough. Soldiers and masons, we looked at each other. We weren't two peoples sharing a ruin, but one, the way mortar makes two rows of stone one wall.

That day we gave the land the Keep's name. Each haven held by the grey sails is Rivenkeep, right to the sea, and that's a vow. In that yard we became the Rivenmen, and we call our fathers Shorelanders and ourselves just *we*.

All day we climbed to the stone below the pike. Each touched it and swore, like men tying up their boats at evening. Jory went before me and came back down whistling, and by nightfall the Keep was held.

We swore to take back every stone. Not one stone that wasn't ours. But every one that was.

The old ones disliked the new name but kept quiet, and still mumble "the Shorelands" into their shawls. This winter, after the ten great hulls settled into the havens, I asked Ebba of Fenholm why she kept the old name. She held her hands out to the fire without looking up.

"It's a name to come home to, when the last wall stands, and the last Mystarch lies broken."

Then she added one word, to the fire, not me.

"Perhaps."

I didn't understand it then, but I think I do now.

Four nights ago she stayed late at that bench-end by the coals with her shawl to her chin, and we left her sleeping. The fire burned low in the night, and nobody fed it.

In the morning she didn't wake.

At dusk we buried her beside the soldiers on the east face, and {{Idrenna}} prayed over her in two halves. Her grandson Marl set the last stone, saying "the Shorelands" over it, and nobody corrected him. The Captain tied a strip off his half-cloak to a pike.

This hearth's answer, *We remember*, came from her, and most nights she started it a moment before the rest. Now it starts with a gap one old voice wide, and nobody's tried to fill it.

So here's mine, and you can weigh it. Jory, who I asked it for, never heard me ask.

On the east wall I once said, "We do not fight because we are right. We fight because our children are behind these walls."

It's true, but not all of it: I have no child of my own, and still I said *our*.

From the east tower you can see the keeps we built in this war, each with a red pennant. But a keep isn't for staying in forever. It's for going out from.

We don't fight for ice and stone. Every stone of this land is Rivenkeep, and Rivenkeep is home, and we swore on the banner to bring it home.

Old Ebba stays on the east face, and Marl has her stone from Fenholm in his pack.

Tomorrow we go down to the havens.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

## BOOK TWO · THE BOOK OF THE BUILDERS

*Who we were, and who they were.*

Hold the stone, Seren.

Their mark and vow are on the bottom of this stone. It takes two to turn it, but late at night, alone at the fire, I've reached under and traced the mark with one finger.

To the masters it's a Stonwryt, *a vow sealed in living rock, earned by toil and skill*, and gold can't buy one. Still, the havens' master pairs stamped theirs on coins, and to people the coins meant what the marks did: *trust, sealed in stone*. I have no mark, and I'll never cut one.

Under the first warden's name his sons carved theirs, one after another, and under the last the stone is smooth. Halvard Stone-Warden comes from that family. Rhyna of the Inner Gate comes from {{Aldwena}}'s. The two families were one long before those two shared a cradle.

Then the people moved to the warm harbours below, and the guild followed. Before leaving, the founders put the slates and the Keep's first staff into the black chest, like masons packing their tools at dusk. By the sea they built Eldhythe, the Eldest, though it's only the eldest of the havens. Nobody wrote down who left last, and after them the wind blew in under an unguarded gate.

It never fell. It was only left behind.

People forgot it until only the songs remembered it. No pair climbed up to *point* its walls, pressing new mortar into the joints. I love that word. So the rain got in, and then the frost and the ice.

Some winter, nobody knows which, the capstone slid off its two posts.

Nobody heard it fall.

But the whole coast heard the havens fall. After Glasspire, we climbed to the castle the songs promised would hold, and found only a shadow.

Forgetting did what no enemy ever could.

At dusk on the day we raised the banner, Halvard and Rhyna led the column through the inner gate. I followed with the water-skin, and I didn't know their names. Halvard swept the snow off the fallen capstone with his sleeve. A pale bloom covered its top, like frost on a gatepost.

They knelt, put their four hands under it, and turned it over like a boat the Tidesmeet fishermen gave up for lost.

It was theirs.

{{Aldwena}}'s mark and vow were there, still sharp, beside dents from the wardens' chisels. Rhyna touched her ancestors' mark. Halvard put his thumb in a dent his fathers' chisels made. The stone was whole. In the yard people kept coming in, their feet silent on the snow.

The stone kept its vow. We were the ones who didn't keep ours.

Before it got dark, they called the guild to its feet. After the Rite a new stone went up, and the True Men set the old one in the hollow of this hearth wall.

Whoever holds it holds the first builders' vow and their children's shame. Tonight it's under my two hands.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

## BOOK THREE · THE BOOK OF THE HAVENS

*Ten havens, our cities by the sea, and the ten men who speak for them: what we had, and how we walked away from it.*

### III.1 · Of Their Raising

*The True Men told this, each for his own haven, standing to answer Kael the Counter as they had on the day of the banner. {{Halyna}} had found the records in the chest I carried, and I read out the parts nobody remembered.*

Give me the stone. No, I'll stand for a roll call.

At dawn I could still see the coast from the east tower, and no hearth on it smoked. Hold on to the havens' names, because they belong to us. I won't speak for another man's city. As oath-keeper I call each haven, and its own man swears what he'll do when it comes home.

*First I read the records. Our fathers were builders, not sailors, and feared the open sea. They built where the mountainsides meet the water, and laid roads like veins over them. Each record begins:* Today we laid the first row and cut a staff for the work. *The clerks' writing is small and careful, as if they hated wasting paper. Stonewrights built every wall. Ten havens, ten staffs.*

**Eldhythe, the Eldest Haven.**

Corlen of the harbour. For fifty years the boats rode the evening tide past my dock, slowly, to the coast's warmest harbour-houses. When our first boat comes home, I'll steer it in.

*Then Corlen, eldest of the Twelve, sat and held up a wet finger in this windless hall, as he does before every fight. When young men laughed from the back, he told them what he tells eager gunners: "Wait. The sea hasn't finished."*

**Tidesmeet, where the river meets the tide.**

I'm Tamm of the three spans, and my city stood on both banks, joined by three great bridges.

*He set three stones in order by the fire, as he does by his blanket each night.* One span is only a plank, but three make a city. I'll set them out on the riverbank where the spans stood.

**Fenholm, the town that was always sinking.**

I'm Marl the fen-born, and Fenholm stood on posts in the marsh, sinking a hand's width a lifetime. Each generation put a new row on top, and any fen-man who can't laugh at that belongs on a hill. My grandmother, old Ebba, laid the top row of our house. I carry one of its stones, and I'd carry her too if she'd let me. That's her nodding by the fire, and I'm to set her stone on the old row.

**Holtward, the keep under the canopy.**

Aske of the canopy. Holtward sat so deep in the trees you could pass under it without seeing a wall. We cut no branch, and swept the leaves off the wall-walk by hand each autumn.

*Aske touched each plank of wreck-wood before it burned.* These were trees once, and while I live, no axe comes into Holtward.

**Carnhold, the stronghold of the cairns.**

Garvel of the smithies. Carnhold stood among the cairns, the first quarrymen's graves, and you could tell each forge by its ring. I listen to every gun like a mother hearing a cough, and a gun that rings true shoots true. But at a Carnhold anvil I'll make something that isn't a gun and hear it ring.

**Emberhythe, the forge on the burning shore.**

Harl of the burning shore. Emberhythe lay where the mountain smokes into the sea, on ground so warm our children went barefoot in the snow. I'll take my shore back and burn the grey, and the sea too, if it would burn.

**Sandreach, the citadel carved in the canyon wall.**

I'm Ulden of the galleries, and though Sandreach had no harbour, it was still our haven, because it sheltered us. It was dug, not built, gallery under gallery in the red rock. Until Sandreach fell, I slept each night under the whole mountain, my ear on the floor, hearing it hold. I'll sleep under that roof again.

**Rimewatch, the watch on the frozen mere.**

I'm Hesk of the mere, and our great hall stood on the frozen lake all winter, with the town feasting warm inside. At dusk my mother's candle lit the lamps in each window, and fishermen out on the lake steered home by them. Tonight one burns on the north wall, and I'll light it on the ice again.

**Highreach, the tower of the floating stones.**

Lanner of the crags. Our cliffs were so high every row went up by crane, hanging on the wind like hawks over a field. I smell a wind a day away, and our boys whistle one up before they can walk. I'll climb the highest again and whistle for a wind.

**And Glasspire, the last and proudest.**

Pellow of the spire. Seven generations built Glasspire, and it flashed in the sun like a blade of ice. The great glass on top sent sunlight a day's sail out to sea. My father ground it. I carried pieces here in straw and ground them into far-glasses. When Glasspire comes home, I'll be the first to see it.

*Then Pellow, youngest of the Twelve, held his glass to the fire, as he does with everything, even his supper. "Look close," he told the children in front.*

Ten cities, ten harbours, ten peoples who called themselves one nation. Under the banner, twelve of us answered, and tonight I hold ten oaths. Stannard of the ridge swore nothing, because the ridge wasn't a haven. He rapped once on the hearthstone, and he trusts no stone he hasn't knocked on. I have no haven.

The Captain found me on a road.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

### III.2 · Of Their Fall

*The True Men told this one too, each for the fall of his own city, on a night of hard frost while the guns cooled and the mortar was still wet. Kael the Counter called them, and I spoke for the guild.*

Give me the stone. No, I'll stand for this one. You learned their names in autumn, so hold them as they fall, and don't look away. We didn't, when we told it.

In the last spring of peace, twenty months before the banner, swallows nested in the gate arches, and nobody ever broke a nest. The havens had no warships, and their walls were built for feast days and weather. At night their wide gates stood open.

Then, without warning, the dark sails came out of the grey. No messenger came that anyone understood, only a knocking from the grey like endless thunder. Our soldiers call that the drums. We watched those ships for a captain, a crew, one man on one rope, but the decks were empty. I swear to that.

So we called it the ghost fleet, and its senders the Mystaeri, after the mist. A man has to name his death. Those sails were simpler than today's, and each came alone, and one was enough. No haven sent for help, since none had any to give. *Then each man took the stone in turn, Corlen first.*

**Eldhythe.**

On that spring's first morning, the dark sails rode the rising tide into the harbour. All day the harbour burned, first the boats at the dock and then the harbour-houses, where the coast's ship-masters had sat by the fire.

*Then he waited, as he does at his guns, and the hall waited with him.*

That evening the wind spread smoke over the city, so the city never saw its harbour go. It held three months, and on its last evening the tide crept over the ash, as always. The sea hadn't finished. *Corlen passed the stone to Tamm and never held it again.*

**Tidesmeet.**

It took three days, and from the high road we saw and heard the spans fall into the river. The upper went, then the lower, and the middle lasted longest, and we waited, and it went. After dark I climbed down for a stone from each.

**Fenholm.**

It sank in one night, as if the marsh had been waiting. A town's just new stone on old, we'd say, laughing, but nobody laughed that night. My grandmother carried her stone out on her back, and wouldn't give it to me until the marsh covered the last row.

**Holtward.**

It burned with its trees, and burning leaves came down on the wall-walk we'd swept. I touched the last tree standing, and its bark was warm, like a living hand.

**Carnhold.**

When its forges burned, I heard the smiths' hammers stop one after another, until the last anvil rang on alone.

Then it stopped.

**Emberhythe.**

It held five months on its burning shore, and the column spent the winter there, warm on bare ground. As it fell, a mason carried out a burnt plank in his pack. I told him to burn it.

**Sandreach.**

We lost it gallery by gallery. Each night, with my ear on the floor, I'd hear the one above me go before any runner came. At last we got out at the canyon's foot, in the dark. Every night since, I've slept on open ground with my ear to it.

**Rimewatch.**

On the first ice, fire caught the great hall on the lake, its lamps still lit. The fire went window by window, like my mother's candle at dusk, and it burned the way it used to shine. I went into the smoke and took her lamp from its window, still lit.

I headed for land over new ice, and it talked under me, far off, then near. Once the wind nearly put the flame out, and I cupped my hand round it till it rose. The roof fell in behind me, and ahead the only light was in my hand.

The ice held.

I brought the lamp out lit. Since then I've lit it on our north wall every dusk, and the night-watch steer by it.

Someone has to keep a light.

**Highreach.**

The wind comes before the sails. I smelled the grey on it a whole day ahead, and that day was good for nothing but leaving. We carried the children down the cliff stairs in the dark, the cranes burning on the crags behind us.

**Glasspire.**

At first snow it sank into its own cellars overnight, spire and glass together, and the dark hole is still open. Some who escaped say a shadow bigger than any ship stood in that night's fog. I stood on the last stair, with the glass falling round me in pieces, and the snow too.

I looked close. I saw nothing.

*Then Pellow sat down without holding up his glass, and gave me his place at the stone.*

On each haven's last day, when masons' hands could do nothing more, the guild left by the land gate with only their chisels. The masters went last, with their trowels cleaned as usual and wrapped in sacks for the road.

The Stonewrights did not fight. They simply walked, and I was one of them.

They left each haven's staff against its falling wall. It's what a woman does on her final night in her mother's house: she covers the fire with ash, like any other night. A staff is cut for its work and stays with it. Not one of the ten staffs came up the mountain.

*Then Kael took the stone back for the Road.*

After Glasspire fell, the column began to climb the mountain. It had grown haven by haven, and none was left behind it. We spent a month in the passes, and on the night of the pass, when the cold was worst, many children died. Some old people died with them. They'd given the children their cloaks.

By morning it was still snowing, and whoever could get up, got up and walked. I counted us at the bottom of the passes and again at the top. I won't tell you the difference.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

## BOOK FOUR · THE BOOK OF THE WOUND

*The wrong that was done, told from both sides of the grey.*

### IV.1 · Of the Felling of the Black Pillars

Hold the stone, Seren.

After a year of reading, one night in the thaw, we reached the last rolls in the chest. The oldest one's rotten cord snapped in Rhyna's fingers, and {{Halyna}} wouldn't read me a line until I'd written this down.

"We two didn't live it,"

"but our fathers' fathers did. They wrote it all down and never read it."

The oldest roll calls the grey a dark haze at the sea's edge. After that, the night watch keeps asking what it is and who breathes it, and nobody answers. When the answer came, it wasn't in our writing.

The first dark sails were already on the sea.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

### IV.5 · The Burning Boat

*Voss who asked told this as his grandmother Elspet told it to him at her fire on the dock at Eldhythe. The ship-masters wouldn't tell it, the children wouldn't stop singing its rhyme, and the shore laughed at it, but the sea didn't.*

In this war's first fight I heard one of their ships burn and scream. The old sailors on the wall looked away, Merrick first, and Jory was whistling. A rhyme turned in me like a skipping rope, one I'd skipped to at seven and understood at forty.

As a boy I asked my grandmother why the ship-masters wouldn't hear it. She answered with a story, and you can judge it.

At twelve she looked after the fire at her mother's harbour inn. The ship-masters came in wet, the weather still in their beards. Three flat grey stones hung above the fire, with letters like bird tracks nobody could read. Only children asked, and the masters knocked their pipes out on the lowest.

A burning boat came screaming at them out of the grey, into the wind and against the current, which no boat can do. Three came home.

There were two boats.

Everyone laughed at those three, and by day so did she, pouring for them below the lowest stone. At night she didn't.

### V.5 · The Tides, as the Wall Counted Them

> *The wood of forty summers holds a word, and waits for its brother.*  
> *The wood of a shore-man's life holds a sentence, with a wall remembered in it.*

### VI.2 · The Thrones That Walk the Sea

*At each fallen Throne, one of {{Halyna}} went among the timbers and the other stood outside with the soldiers, who wouldn't go near, and the one outside knew it too. A Throne's heart comes in a breath, but its name comes last and hardest.*

<!-- READING -->

*— Water. Houses on us.*  
*— Feet on us. Rotting in the marsh.*  
*— Walking roots. Stone answers.*  
*— Ash. Dark below the rock.*  
*— White. White, so long.*  
*— Sky, raised. Glass —*  
*— smashed. Names, under it all. Thae — [ ]*

This is held in the grain.

We came late. The young wood burned your havens, not us: green, hasty wood, one sign per ship. We were the oldest Naelsaen, the last pillars left on the edge, holding the sky between our crowns. We'd grown nothing since the iron, waiting for our sky to return.

<!-- RING -->

The shore still wasn't silent when the last long-lived carvers hurried to us. They carved judgements into us slowly, because a judgement isn't a word, a sentence or tomorrow's plan. They pulled us up and grew us into a throne for each haven, and set us walking the sea, leading the last fleet.

When we left, the breath we'd anchored left too, and the sky above the last groves tore open, white and hard.

The long-lived's children lay down in that light and couldn't breathe.

Reaching you cost us the last of our own sky.

Each of us sat in a fallen haven, where that haven had wronged the wood most. Our dead lay there, our family's timber in walls, beams and bridges, and we sat with them like mourners at a grave. A judgement is heavy, and the grain cried out under ours. Our names were our own, so keep them.

We are **Thaesaen**, the tide-heart, seated under the harbour in our family's timber, which held its houses up. *Give them the middle, take the sides, close the hand.* We shut it on nothing but sea.

We are **Leavaren**, bearer of new branches, seated beneath the three spans, our family, laid flat under your feet. *Strike one head, and two will answer.* When cut, we grew back, as our family would have without the iron.

We are **Vaelress**, the grain that rots, once the only wood that never rotted. Our family was driven into the marsh beneath a town. *Rot the wall, wait for the repairs to fail.* So we were turned into rot.

We are **Ralensaen**, the root-heart, and our family roofed the homes beneath the canopy. We walked, not sailed, as a pillar might walk home if it were able. *Go under what you can't go through.* We went under root by root and stood among living trees that didn't know us.

We are **Rhenvael**, the stone-grain: *Every gun that speaks, the mountain answers.* We answered from the cairns with the mountain's rock. The iron spoke to us first, through many summers at the breath's edge.

We are **Esthaer**, the burning, seated in the ashes of our family, who burned slowly, unlike a burning ship. *Burn as you go, let them chase the fire.* We turned our burning into a trick and you followed. Once, the wood had no tricks.

We are **Senneir**, the deep-beneath, and in the dark below the canyon our family held up rock over the shore-men's heads. *Show them the army, come up from under the stone.* We came up by paths our dead had kept clear for your people.

We are **Eirlenth**, the long winter. Our family roofed a big hall on the ice and held many people's warmth through the long nights. *Wait in the white, strike the stone that can't be fixed.*

We waited a long time in the white, and no one roofed us.

At last a little fire burned above us, where the hall once stood.

We are **Naelthar**, blood of the sky. Our family, lighter than iron, became the cranes that hoisted the floating stones and your towers into our sky. *Come the way the young wood came.* We dropped from that sky all at once, with what was left of it filling our sails.

We are **Neivaere**, the silvered, the mirror. Your shore once flung the sun at us, and the breath burned off wherever it struck. No throne was at sea the night the spire fell, and the grain holds nothing of it. Much later we sat among shattered glass in the spire's cellars, one shard in a thousand. *Show them themselves.*

We showed you yourselves, and you were hard to look at, and so were we.

<!-- RING -->

Those were the ten, the breath's last pillars. All have fallen, and your havens are yours again. We once carried the sky between our crowns while the long-lived's children slept beneath it. Now the sky above the groves has worn thin, and the grain still holds it.

*And no one answered.*

### VI.3 · The Knowing of the Green Wood

*I, Seren Two-Inks, held the lamp, and I told this on the night the boat went out, in the sixth autumn. {{Halyna}} stood at the stone with me, each with a hand beside mine, and we held it a long time before anyone spoke.*

<!-- TOKEN {LAST_THRONE_AT_HAVEN} -->

Hold the stone, Seren.

All day the tenth Throne, {LAST_THRONE_AT_HAVEN}, lay where it fell, a split black hill of timber taller than a harbour-house. Even dead, it scared the soldiers off. {{Halyna}} had known nine hearts since spring, all dead wood. Toward evening the Captain came down himself, stood a long time, hood up, and looked at {{Halyna}}.

At dusk I followed them in with the lamp, under dripping timbers. The heart lay in the oldest wood, hard, grey and still, with a sliver no bigger than a hand in a crack beside it.

It was green.

I lowered the lamp, not trusting the light. For six winters we'd searched wood and sea for orders, names and sails, but never for anything alive. And this had lived in a dead heart, like a seedling in a cracked wall.

Since the first winter {{Halyna}} had needed only one hand, but now they both touched it together. A drop hit the lamp flame and hissed.

Their faces changed as one. It wasn't an order or a wound. Rhyna spoke as if someone had put the word in her mouth and left.

"Home."

Halvard said she was right, but the word didn't come alone, and it didn't stop where we stop. They said it in one breath.

*We remember our home too.*

Rhyna cried, and Halvard didn't, because she was crying for them both. I held the lamp until she'd finished.

They gave me one more thing, tucked in the answer like a seed in a fruit. The wood's people call themselves *Naelear*, those of the breath, and *Mystaeri* is only the shore's word. I said their name three times before writing it, and it held.

Since the iron came, the black pillars hadn't grown a leaf, waiting for the sky to come back.

So it was a seed.

Rhyna carried it up to the Keep on moss, Halvard holding her elbow. A fisher-girl from the east keep, who lit the north lamp, ran up to ask where it was going.

"Home."

"And we two must find it a boat."

The guild has no record of a Stonewright boat. The sea was something we walled out, never a road. But the havens' boatbuilders had carried their tools up the mountain too. Their oldest was Tarnard, Tarnel's father, and next day on the beach he laid the keel himself.

Harl of the burning shore shouldered the first plank out of the vault. He'd said *Burn it* about every plank in the war, but he laid this one at Tarnard's feet without a word. Tarnard put his palm on it and said, "Wood that knew the sea knows the way home."

Those planks had all spoken to us once, and now they'd speak for us. For the front post, Tarnard chose the two halves I'd set raw edge against raw edge, the *shore* plank and the silent one. {{Orvenna}} locked them like stone, and Ormund struck the last key once while Penna held it.

The broken grain met, and held.

That evening {{Halyna}} cut their mark over the join, one holding the chisel while the other struck, as my lamp burned a finger's width lower. No mark of ours had ever gone into wood.

The wood took it.

Rhyna pressed her palms to the cut, and Halvard covered them with his. Tarnard let me tie the bow with the knot Tarnel taught me on the Road, a knot for every mile. Tarnard stared at it a long time, then quietly repeated his son's saying. "A net is just knots holding hands."

It rode a rough sea off the point for a day without leaking. It was small, and it was sound.

At the evening tide the whole hearth came down, the old leaning on the young. {{Halyna}} put the green sliver on its moss in the bow, against the front post. Next to it went a Stonwryt coin of gold and black stone. It was trust sealed in stone, our dearest gift. They added their two chisels, which caught the last light.

"because we two have cut our last mark."

In silence, {{Halyna}} knelt at the bow on the wet stones. The whole hearth knelt behind them, and the soldiers put their pikes down. Kael the Counter, who won't sit to tell a tale, knelt too. Last in line, his hood still up, the Captain knelt.

That's him at the end of the bench, his knees still dark from the wet beach.

{{Halyna}} spoke from the front.

"One people can go this far alone: the gift and the kneeling."

A wave ran up and soaked the keel. Then Harl stood, lifted the bow in both arms and walked it into the tide, and the fisher-children shoved it off. The girl who'd asked where it would go was in front, waist-deep in the cold water.

Far out on the outgoing tide, the boat turned. It went into the wind and against the current, into the grey.

The old ship-masters lined the beach, Merrick first, and for six winters they'd looked away from the sea whenever a ship burned.

They did not look away.

*And no one answered.*

When the three of us let go, the hearth stayed silent a long time. The fire burned low, and nobody fed it. I think we were waiting for one old voice. Ebba of Fenholm is buried under the east cairns.

### The Stone out of the Grey

*I wrote this on the page facing Kael's tale of the torn cloak. It stood empty until I drew the grain on it, with one line:* Not told yet. This is still too deep for {{Halyna}} to hold. *Nothing {{Halyna}} knew ever filled it.*

Hold the stone, Seren.

I write alone, in one ink, with nobody left who knows the wood. Their two inks sit dry on my table.

{{Halyna}} are gone.

That seventh winter they died the same night, as the Bonded do. Rhyna went first, asleep, and Halvard before the lamp went out, because he knew. They share one grave and one name under the inner gate's new top stone. The Title, their vow and their mark are cut on its underside, hidden until the wall falls.

*{{Orvenna}} sang at the grave, Ormund up to the comma and Penna the rest:*

> *Stone on stone, and the stone stays true.*  
> *Hand over hand, and the hand holds on.*  
> *Two at the gate when the grey sails gather.*  
> *Two at the gate, and the gate stands strong.*

{{Idrenna}}, old even when {{Halyna}} were children, have outlived them. {{Halyna}} carved Halvard's name on the western stair, under his fathers', before they sent their chisels into the grey. Below it the stone is blank, because his family is gone.

That night the Captain tied a hand's width of his half-cloak to a pike above the gate, the last of it. All winter he wore a plain grey cloak.

I didn't follow {{Halyna}}. They once said that alone I could go where they couldn't, and carry the Book on. Now the Covenant makes sense: when God takes one, he leaves the other a task.

This is mine.

I was scared to speak at my first tale, so {{Halyna}} put my hands on the stone. One said, "Hold the stone," and the other, "Hold it, and it will hold you." Now I say both halves alone.

One rainy morning in the seventh spring, an empty grey boat came in from the grey and beached itself.

The old ship-masters hung back, but the children went down together, and the first pressed both palms to the bow. A child once answered the green wood when we couldn't.

It was the same child.

Then Merrick came slowly down, the man who looked away from every burning ship.

Merrick put his hand on its side.

I went last, and the children parted like water before a boat. I knew its front post: the first heart's two halves, locked together, with {{Halyna}}'s mark over the join.

It had gone home, and it had come home. A stone lay where {{Halyna}} had set their chisels and the green sliver.

It was wood turned to stone, like the Guest's gift. The Rivenmen call it Myststone and say it remembers everything. Stone does not translate, but this was never only stone. Its carver learned the chisel late but well, young by their count, I think, and a man by ours.

The rain had stopped, the sea lay slate grey, and the tide drew out with a long sound.

I knew that chisel's bite.

I'd held the lamp for it twice, under the top stone and across this post. It was cut in our letters, so one person could read it alone, and I read it to the children without touching it.

*In the memory of our home, our families...*

The cutting stops there, its last stroke still sharp. He never saw our cloak, since he never sailed, but he learned our letters. I call him the Last Carver, because I think he was the only one left.

It has a *the* before *memory*, which our Title never had. I read it like that, and no child laughed. I wonder if it stops there because his hand stopped. Nobody came with it, and maybe that's wise. We haven't earned a guest yet.

That spring the staff vanished from the gatehouse, and nobody saw the Captain take it. It left a clean line in seven winters of dust. If you doubt me, go and see the dust. The work is done. I know *the stairs that go down no more*.

The young wood cut only the root, and each Tide after added to it, so the oldest carvings are the young ones grown old. By the late Tides I could spot a root from the wall at a glance, but nothing more. The wood saves the rest for the Bonded, to be known, not read.

**The Last Note · Three Stones over the Hearth**

Hold the stone, Seren.

On this winter's first night, a child at the front asked what the three grey stones above the hearth say. In the havens every child, me included, was told they say nothing. I didn't say that. I said I didn't know.

Every night since, in the empty hall, I've held the lamp to the lowest stone. I wanted its sounds, not its meaning, because stone does not translate. The Mystaeri spelled our words by sound, in among their letters, and Brenn had given me three of our words.

Last night, alone by a low fire, I watched my lamp burn a finger's width lower. Then I found all three together, the sounds close but the symbols wrong.

*Stop… Sky… Dying…*

They were cut at the foot of the black pillars and sold off with the timber. At Eldhythe they spent three generations above a harbour-house fire. Under them, people laughed at the three axemen who came home on a spar, and ship-masters knocked their pipes out on the lowest. And nobody read them.

Nobody knocks a pipe on them here. I think I've read them. I say I think.
