# HOW SEREN TELLS A TALE

*The craft guide for the Book of the Riven Stone. Every writer of the new Book, and of its Plain Words, works to this page. Jack's notes of 2026-10-02 and the orchestrator's reading notes (`wf11/jack_2026-10-02.md`) override it wherever they speak. The research it distils is in `wf11/research/`: R1 Tolkien, R2 Homer and Auerbach, R3 archaism and economy, R4 how a story builds, R5 the diagnosis of v1.3 and v2.0, and R6a/R6b the element inventories. What a tale must hold is R6's business. How it is told is this page's.*

---

## 0 · The whole of it, on one page

Jack's verdict on v2.0: run-on sentences, too long, clinical, "like you are writing a history text book". The tales do not build. v1.3 had the right sentences and the right voices, but it too explained, summed up, and gave its endings away in the headnote. The new Book is:

1. **v1.3's sentences.** Short and long mixed, full stops, the crisis told shortest, the great line alone on its paragraph.
2. **Made hard by old English.** Word order, grammar, and a small stock of old words that keeps coming back. Never by length.
3. **Told as stories.** A question, a rising, one scene told close, a turn, a cost, a closing picture.
4. **In Seren's hand.** Playful on the road, plain at the grave. Every teller lifted, and still himself.
5. **At a master's length.** About 29,000 words for the tales proper, against v2.0's 52,000.

**How would Tolkien tell it?** Every master the research read says the same few things:

- Come close at the deed, and sum up the road (Tolkien's telescope; Burroway).
- Light the deed, and leave the heart dark (Genesis 22, read by Auerbach).
- Build by three. The third time breaks (Malory's Bedivere; Olrik).
- Plant a thing and pay it without a word (Chekhov; Homer's scar).
- Hold the high style back for the one moment that needs it (Tolkien at the Pelennor, read by Turner).
- Be colder at the grave (Chekhov; the sagas).
- End on a picture (Olrik; Conrad).
- Real old English is terse (Tolkien, Letter 171: "far more terse" than modern).

And Jack's last word: this is fiction, and the most fun to write. Seren loves the tongue. If a sentence would not have given her pleasure to set down, it is not finished.

**The dial is not set here.** How much old grammar the Book carries (R3's HEAVY, MEDIUM or NATIVE) is being tested elsewhere. Every rule below holds at any setting. The *after* lines use only what every setting shares: old word order, negation without *do*, *is come* and *was gone* for verbs of motion and change, a few stock words (*ere*, *naught*, *bade*, *yet*), and *thou* only in prayer or close speech. When the dial is set, convert them.

**The locks.** These are unchanged, and listed here so that no writer has to look them up:

- {{Halyna}} discover, Seren writes, other hands act.
- *We remember* is the hearth's Amen.
- A wood leaf holds only what the wood could know, which is the Mystaeri side.
- The warden's name is always ▒▒▒▒. He is a weak man, and the burning was his order, to hide what was done. There is no custom of burning anywhere in the Book.
- In IV.3 Brenn only watched. His *we* is the post. He did not stop them. He was a boy among the men.
- A pair-name is written {{Name}}, drawn under a lintel. It takes plural verbs, and a pair speaks as *we two*. Never use a singular *they* for one person.
- The cross-sign is not a holy sign, and there is no Christian mark anywhere.
- The two peoples' tongues share an ancestor that is never revealed. The parting of the peoples stays untold.
- The Captain is never named. No one is aboard the grey fleet.
- Every song stays word for word. So do Jack's lines:
  - *In memory of our home, our families, our God, our freedoms, our peace.*
  - *The mortar cries out from the ground. Rise, Stonewrights! Lay stone upon stone. Stand every wall to the very end.*
  - *Rise! Rise! Rise!*
  - *We remember our home too.*
  - *We remember.*
  - *But war is for the short-minded and the angry young.*
  - *When the left hand dies, does not the right one die too?*
  - The Book Five line, *a doom set in motion by the dead, crewed by the living, against a people who had never known they were the foe*.
- Everything in the Book is original. Public-domain texts are models, never sources of lines.

## 1 · The rules

Twenty-two rules, each with its source and one example from our own tales. The *before* is v2.0 or v1.3. The *after* shows the way it should go. The after-lines are illustrations, not canon, and the beats stay R6's.

**1. Make it old through grammar and words, never through length.**
*Source:* Tolkien, Letter 171: real archaic English is terser than modern. The King James Bible sounds old in every line through its grammar, yet fewer than one word in a hundred in it is archaic (R3 §0). R5 found that v2.0's archaism was 90% *upon* and *ere*, laid over modern grammar.
*Before* (v2.0, I.2): *It was dusk on the day of the banner, and the last of the light lay thin as ash upon the snow, and the guild sat in the old ward where it had gone to grass…* (the sentence runs 78 words)
*After:* *Thin as ash lay the last light on the snow. In the old ward, that was gone to grass, sat the guild.*
*Test:* a sentence of the Book may be harder than its Plain Words twin, but never longer.

**2. One breath to a sentence. *And* joins deeds, never reasons.**
*Source:*
- R5: v1.3's sentences average 15 words, and v2.0's 21. One v2.0 sentence in eight runs past 45 words, and v2.0 has 2.7 times v1.3's semicolons.
- Ong: oral style adds deed to deed.
- Parker and Stone: between beats goes *therefore* or *but*, never *and then*.

*Before* (v2.0, I.1): *At the top, where the wall yet stood whole, the Captain took off his cloak, a plain soldier's cloak, crimson once and gone to the colour of old brick; and on that mountain, in that wind, it was all he had against the cold.*
*After:* *At the top he took off his cloak. Crimson it was once, ere the road wore it to brick. In that wind it was all he had.*

**3. Go shorter at the crisis, and give the great line its own paragraph.**
*Source:* R5 §1: v1.3's IV.3 drops to 9.5 words a sentence at the killing, while v2.0's Green Wood climbs to 30 at the knowing. Tolkien holds his grand style back for the turn of the Pelennor (R1 1.12). Tedlock: in a spoken tale, the pause carries the meaning.
*Before* (v2.0, I.1): *I had heard men break on the long retreat; but never till that dusk had I heard a whole people break.*
*After:* *Men had I heard break ere then. Never had I heard a people break. It is a sound like ice going out on a river.*

**4. Open on a moment: an hour, a place, one sense, and the seed of something wrong.**
*Source:* Tolkien opens the Ride of the Rohirrim on Merry in the dark, hearing drums he cannot place. Horace: Homer hurries the hearer into the middle of things. Gardner: distance kills the dream.
*Before* (v2.0, IV.3): *Forty years ago I served at the outpost at the end of the land, where the rock runs out into the sea toward the grey; and of all the posts upon that coast it was the last, and the farthest from any haven, and the poorest.*
*After:* *Nineteen I was. Twelve were we at that post, and the warden.* Soon after it comes v1.3's model sentence for the whole Book: *There was no wind, and the boat came anyway.*

**5. Raise one question by the hundredth word, and let every beat press on it.**
*Source:* Aristotle, *Poetics*: one action, and a whole one. Gardner: a want, pursued against opposition. Olrik: one strand.
*Before* (v2.0, I.1): four paragraphs on what the Captain was and was not. They stand between the breaking and the climb, at the very height of the question *will this people stand?*
*After:* the lecture becomes looking (R4).
> *He looked for a general, and the generals were slain. He looked for the lords, and the lords were scattered. He looked to the Stonewrights, and they sat in the grass with their chisels in their laps. Then the True Men looked to their Captain. The Captain looked at the wall.*

**6. Tell the deed as a scene, and the road as summary.**
*Source:* Burroway: summary covers years briefly, and scene covers a moment at length. Tolkien's telescope gives Beren's wanderings a few lines and his meeting with Lúthien whole paragraphs. R1 1.4: one halt to a tale.
*Before* (v2.0, V.7): *{{Enrella}} they were, the young pair, the two of them sealed in our first summer under the old hands of {{Idrenna}}, and prentices to {{Orvenna}} the wall-masters; and they had come up the mountain road side by side…*
*After:* *Since their sealing they had built at it, the house of one door. One course high it stood, and higher it stood never.* Then the morning, told close.

**7. Plant early, as a deed. Pay it without a word of gloss.**
*Source:* Chekhov to Lazarev: no loaded rifle on the stage unless someone means to fire it. O'Connor: the wooden leg is a leg first. Homer's scar. Tolkien's coal, taken from the dead hearth and never explained.
*Before* (v2.0, V.7, at the very moment it pays): *That morning, for the first time in her life, she both set the chisel and struck it, as Enno had always set it for her.*
*After:* at the opening, *Enno set the chisel. Della struck.* At the north breach, *She set the chisel herself, and struck.*

**8. Build by three, and let the third break.**
*Source:*
- Olrik: the law of three, with the weight on the last.
- Malory: Bedivere goes to the water three times, and each lie is lovelier and worse.
- Reece: Homer passes the suitors three times.
- Tolkien: Gollum's near-caress on the stair, broken by one rough word (R1 1.9).

*Before* (v2.0, IV.3): beats laid side by side, none of them raised: the gull, the poles, the heels, the net, Tobe's saying, Tobe washing the poles.
*After:* the Guest offers three things, and each is refused harder.
> *His words we heard not. His gift ▒▒▒▒ kicked into the dust. His hand on the warden's neck the men tore away, and slew him.*

In the tale each refusal is its own short scene. This is only the spine.

**9. At the crisis, let one object sound.**
*Source:* *Odyssey* 19: the nurse knows the scar and lets the foot fall, and the basin rings and spills (Auerbach; R2 §2). Chekhov: a moonlit night is shown by one glint of broken bottle-glass.
*Before* (v2.0, V.7 Part II): Pellow's death is told among the hunters, a 50-word lamplighter simile, and the Captain's pace along the wall.
*After:* v2.0's own line, given room: *Pellow lay dead at the parapet. His glass lay by his hand, and it was whole.*

**10. Light the deed and leave the heart dark. Never let a custom explain a deed.**
*Source:*
- Auerbach on Genesis 22: only the decisive points are lit, and the rest is "fraught with background".
- Benjamin: half the art of the storyteller is to keep the story free from explanation.
- Tolkien, Letter 144: even a mythical age needs some enigmas.
- Jack: the custom "muddies the story", and makes it "less impactful".

*Before* (v2.0, IV.3): *We knew the custom, and we were glad of it, for it let us stop thinking.*
*After:* the order is the warden's, and a weak man's. It is said in words that the children's rhyme will one day keep without knowing it: *"Burn it," ▒▒▒▒ said, "and shove it off, and give it the tide. Leave naught."* Of the stone, Brenn says only: *Why I did it, I know not.*

**11. Give deeds, not verdicts. The body shows the heart.**
*Source:* no saga says that a man was brave. Feeling shows on the face and the hands (Wolf 2016; R3 §1.1). Tolkien shows his weak men, Sharkey and Wormtongue, and never judges them (R1 1.14).
*Before* (v1.3, IV.3): *It was the trappings of a rich man's vanity, and he was a rich man's son.*
*After:* *Black wood had he set everywhere: in the doorposts, in his table, in the frame of his bed.* Later, with no comment: *▒▒▒▒ wore long sleeves all the rest of his life, and I know why.*

**12. Make us love them before they die. At the death, be cold.**
*Source:*
- Tolkien: Théoden's promise to Merry of ordinary days is broken by his death (R1 1.6).
- Griffin: the *Iliad* gives even its minor dead a small obituary.
- Chekhov to Avilova: "try to be colder".
- Dasent's Bergthora: one old promise, kept before the fire.

*Before* (v2.0, V.7): a 70-word simile of a fisher's wife stands between the stopped mallet and Della's look east.
*After:* *The mallet stayed in the air. She looked east.* Then: *She set the chisel herself, and cut the last stroke, and lay down by the mark.* No simile, and no comment.

**13. Understate once a tale, at the hottest point, and say it sideways.**
*Source:*
- Dasent's Njal: a man speared at Gunnar's window says only that Gunnar's bill is at home, and falls dead (R3 §1.1).
- Bracher, on understatement in Old English poetry.
- *Beowulf*: "that was a good king".

*Before* (v2.0, V.5 Count IV, and the same beat again in V.7): *I counted them as one. I will not say more.*
*After:* once in the whole Book, at the pike over the north stone: *Kael stood by the pike and counted them. "One," he said.*

**14. End on a picture, never on a moral.**
*Source:* Olrik: a tale rises to a tableau, with the weight on the last place. Conrad: the meaning lies outside the tale, as a glow brings out a haze. R4 rule 11.
*Before* (v1.3, V.7 Part III): *That night, for the first time, we saw that the enemy could hope.*
*After:* *The lamp went out on the shingle, and I filled it not a third time.*

**15. Give one short picture to a movement, from the teller's own world, with no scaffold.**
*Source:* Tolkien's images are mostly a few words of plain comparison, set at the hinge (Turner; R1 1.12). Homer saves his long similes for the hinges (R2 §8). R5 counts twenty v2.0 similes of 60 to 76 words each, in which the reader holds a side picture before the main clause arrives.
*Before* (v2.0, I.1): *As a man at a poor table, in the dead of the year, holds the last loaf of his house in his two hands, and breaks it…: so the Captain held his cloak. He tore it down the back.*
*After:* the small homely sound becomes the turn (R1 1.11): *He tore it down the back. A small sound it made, the sound of old cloth. Over all the crying I heard it.*

**16. A teller is a person within three sentences.**
*Source:* Tolkien gives each people and each person an idiom (Shippey). Kullmann and Siepmann count eight speech-styles in the Fellowship alone. At the Council of Elrond each speaker reports in his own register.
*Before* (v2.0, I.1): *Fourteen nights I have sat at this fire, and a soldier stands to give his count.* This explains the man.
*After:* *Give me the stone. No, I will stand. Enough have I sat this winter.* This is the man.

**17. The words are Seren's; the tale is the teller's.**
*Source:* *Odyssey* 8: Odysseus honours the singer of his own war above all men, because he sang it as if he had been there (R2 §4). Aristotle: the poet should speak as little as possible in his own person.
*Before* (v2.0, Of This Book): *The words are the teller's, and I change not one of them; the measure is mine.*
*After:* *The tale is the teller's; the words are mine. No teller yet has asked for his own back.*

**18. Keep one register in every mouth. The Book never contracts, and it has one exception: the letter.**
*Source:* Le Guin, "From Elfland to Poughkeepsie": heroes who drop into slang for a joke fall apart. Tolkien, Letter 171: Théoden does not think in modern idiom, so he cannot speak it. Tolkien gives his orcs a clerk's speech, and it marks them (R1 1.14).
*Before* (v2.0, I.5): *It is not the ice I'm fighting for.* (And Jory's *"It's only a rhyme."*)
*After:* *It is not the ice I fight for.* (And *"It is only a rhyme."*)
The only modern-sounding words in the Book stay as they are, and they are the warden's: *Unknown intruder. Hostile intent. Neutralized. Vessel burned and released.*

**19. Speak little, in halves, and let the halves stand bare.**
*Source:*
- In the high style, people say least at the worst moment (Beren before Thingol; R1 1.14).
- 2 Samuel 12: *Thou art the man.*
- Njal's saga: a whole marriage in four speeches (R3 §1.1).
- R5: the tag "one said, and the other finished" comes 0.44 times in every 1,000 words of v2.0.

*Before* (v2.0, VI.3): *"Home," one of them said, and the other finished, "to be planted in its own ground; and we two must find it a boat."*
*After:* two lines, in two inks, with no tag.
> *"Home."*
>
> *"And we two must find it a boat."*

**20. Get the grammar of a pair right every time.**
*Source:*
- The canon: a pair-name is one name for two, drawn under a lintel, with plural verbs and *we two* (R3 §2).
- Dasent's Njal: *thee* for one person, *you* for the pair.
- R5: v2.0 has "the two of them" 40 times and "he and she" 12 times.

*Before* (v2.0, I.2): *Then {{Halyna}} stood up, he and she together.*
*After:* *Then {{Halyna}} stood up.* The lintel says two, and the verb says two. Nothing more is needed.

**21. Use a few old words, often. Never a false friend, a fake oath, or a holy mark.**
*Source:*
- Saragi, Nation and Meister: readers of *A Clockwork Orange* learned most of its strange words from context, without warning, but they lost the words they met fewer than eight times (R3 §1.6).
- Crystal: a false friend misleads in silence.
- Stevenson: "tushery".
- The canon: God, and no Christian mark.

*Before* (v2.0, V.7 and the Epilogue, the prayer of {{Idrenna}}): *"Lord, thou madest these two one, ere either of them drew breath."*
*After:* *"God, thou madest these two one ere either drew breath."*
Do not write *upon* where *on* will do. v2.0 used *upon* 261 times, and it taught the reader nothing.

**22. A refrain must change, or go.**
*Source:* Alter: a key word repeats, and its variation carries the meaning. The *Iliad* ends by calling Hector "tamer of horses" at his burial, and the epithet turns. R4 rule 14.
*Before* (v2.0, fourteen times, never changing): *And the Captain put his hood back.*
*After:* the refrain that grows is the strip of cloak.
- Told whole once: *It was the first strip.*
- After that, a clause.
- Then paid: *Tonight it does not reach his belt.*
- And at the last: *It was the last of his half.*

## 2 · The build

### 2.1 The shape of a tale

1. **The call.** The teller's formula, in one line: *Give me the stone.* · *Hold the stone, Seren.* · *This is held in the grain.*
2. **The first sentence of story.** It gives an hour, a place, one sense, and the seed of something wrong. It never gives a date.
3. **The question, by the hundredth word.** It is a yes-or-no question that a child at the front could ask:
   - Will this people stand?
   - Will anyone at that door do right?
   - Will the two who were sent apart come back together?
4. **The stakes.** One thing to love, shown in action before it is in danger: a habit, a want, or a promise. Examples are the house of one door, the lamp on the north wall, and *Look close.*
5. **The doom, if the teller knows it.** One line, inside the scene: *I laughed. I have not laughed at anything since.*
6. **The rising.** Try, fail, and make it worse. Each step is "yes, but" or "no, and" (Sanderson). There are three steps, and the third breaks.
7. **The halt.** One scene, told close and slow, at the deed. The sentences get shorter there. One object sounds, and nothing in the scene is explained.
8. **The turn.** It comes out of what the hearer has already seen, and is often something small and careless of the war. Tolkien's is the cock that crows at the Gate. Ours are the sound of old cloth, the mortar still soft, and the word *written*. Seren never says what it means.
9. **The cost,** paid in a line.
10. **The closing picture.** After it come the teller's close and the hearth's answer, and nothing else.

**The test of the joints.** Between any two beats, write *therefore* or *but*. If only *and then* fits, cut the beat or rebuild it (Aristotle; Parker and Stone).

**Proportion.** Take a tale of 1,000 words as the example:
- about 250 words to reach the question and the stakes;
- about 300 for the rising;
- about 300 for the halt;
- about 100 for the turn and the cost;
- fewer than 50 for the close.

The halt is the longest stretch of the tale. The turn is the shortest. The last third has the shortest sentences.

### 2.2 How a death is planted and paid

1. **Plant.** Give one habit, want or promise, shown in action and early, ideally a leaf or more before. Not a biography.
2. **Glimpse.** One line of doom, if the teller knows it.
3. **The death.** Cold, short, with no simile and no comment. If the teller did not see it, he says so: *I did not see it. Hale did.*
4. **The token.** The thing that outlasts the dead: the glass whole, the lamp burning on the ice, the knot, the frost on both handles.
5. **The kindness remembered.** At the cairn or at the hearth, give one line of the good, not only the loss (memory theme 4). A life holds more than its ending.
6. **The strip.** Told whole once. After that, a clause, or nothing. It is paid in V.5 Count VI and in the Epilogue.
7. **Never:** tell one death in full twice, explain it, or name it brave.

**The ledger.** Each death is told whole in one place only. Elsewhere it gets a line.

### 2.3 The build of every tale

These are the craftsman's reading. R6 governs the content; the beats named here are R6's beats, placed. The budget is the ceiling for the tale proper.

**Of This Book** · Seren · 850 words
- *Shape:* Seren alone at the coals, with the Book half full. Then the stone, and the pun on *laid*. Then Ebba's first *We remember*, and the vow: we remember because we choose to, as a mason chooses which stone to keep (memory theme 1, said once, in her own words). Then the wood, known and not read, the two inks, and *You cannot tell.* Then her hand: she changes the words, and no teller has yet asked for his own back. Then the Captain at the bench. Then the Book's rules, once: the blots, the Thrones by their own names, and both tellings kept where they disagree.
- *Close on:* *Up the mountain I carried two things, our tongue and a chest; and only the chest was heavy.* Then *This Book is ink.*

**The Invocation** · 200 words
- *Shape:* the hall's call to listen. Keep the Title and *Hold the stone. Lay the tale. When it is laid, the hearth will answer.* Let the handing-on be felt (memory theme 3), and add nothing to it.

**I.1 · The Torn Cloak** · Kael · 1,100 words
- *Question:* will this broken people stand?
- *Halt:* the climb, the coal, the tearing.
- *Turn:* the small sound of old cloth, then the widening count, until the counter stops counting.
- *Close on:* *On his back is the other half.* · *He will tell you it was not like that. It was like that.* Then the Lay.
- *Note:* keep the thirty-one stones and the cough, then only the wind. The True Men's roll stays bare, and their tags move to III.1.

**I.2 · The Cry of the Bonded** · Seren · 1,000 words
- *Question:* will a guild sworn never to bear a weapon rise?
- *Halt:* the Captain's question, then the wind in the grass, then {{Halyna}} rising, then the cry.
- *Turn:* *There was no silence.* Then the terms, in two halves; the hours, in one line of {{Orvenna}}'s; and the first course laid.
- *Close on:* Enno and Della asleep against the new course with their hands joined. *By morning the mortar had set.*
- *Note:* no glossary at the cry. The pair-names were taught in the foreword.

**I.3 · The Captain's Long Breath** · Hale · 850 words
- *Question:* will the boy's word reach the Captain in time, and what will he do with it?
- *Halt:* the Captain standing still, and the hour stretching: a cart on a long hill, the ripples counted.
- *Turn:* *"Hold."* … *"Loose."*
- *Close on:* Kael's mend: *The moment he chose was the moment that came.*
- *Note:* the plants go in as single lines: the runners' oath, Wick, Jory's whistle and Merrick, Hesk's lamp, *Marks on the wood.*

**I.4 · The Rite of the Cornerstone** · Seren · 1,200 words
- *Question:* can the Rite be made when the flasks are dry?
- *Rising:* the three oils are the build by three:
  - the cedar, from a tree they will not fell, found by Stannard's knuckle;
  - the bloom, scraped by the children's raw hands;
  - the myrrh, carried for the road's dead.
- *Turn:* *I knew for whom it had been carried.*
- *Halt:* the Rite in the thin sun. The hands of {{Idrenna}}, which shook that winter at everything else, do not shake on the flasks.
- *After:* the Title cut under the capstone. *Neither of them had mended one stroke.* Then the child's leaf at the bottom of the chest.
- *Close on:* *The people called him Commander. In his own heart he was still only the Captain.*
- *Note:* the finding of the chest is summary, in two short paragraphs. The cradle-rhyme stays word for word.

## 3 · The frame, made light

### 3.1 The dateline

It names when the tale was laid, or known, in a few words, in plain italic:
- *The first winter.*
- *The third winter, in the Tide of Remembering.*
- *The fourth year after the homecomings.*

It is eight words at most. It is never in capitals. It never names the date of the events, and never says "years before the banner".

### 3.2 The headnote

One or two sentences, and fifty words at most. It tells who laid the tale. It may add one human touch. On a wood leaf, it tells whose hand was on the wood, and where the other of them was.

It never holds:
- the ending, or the contents;
- a chain of custody, the archive's procedure, or a seal;
- a rule of the Book (those go in the foreword, once);
- a gloss;
- the date of the events.

Models:

> **I.1.** *Laid by Kael the Counter, on the first night this hearth asked for a tale. He forbade me to change a word of it; I changed a good many, and when I read it him he swore it was word for word as he had told it.*
>
> **IV.3.** *The words of Brenn of the outpost, as a pair of the guild at Eldhythe took them from his mouth in his old age. {{Halyna}} read them at the hearth, and the annal after them is mine.*
>
> **IV.4.** *Known from the gift itself, which Rhyna held an hour at the hearth; Halvard, across the ward at the mortar, never touched it, and began the telling. The last of it is about me, and I have let it stand as the wood gave it.*
>
> **IV.6.** *Known from the heart of a Standard-Bearer, which Halvard took up in the vault. Rhyna was on the wall; the soldiers beside her say she plunged both her hands into the rain-butt, and would not say why.*

The v1.3 headnote of IV.3 gave the end away: *the man who picked up the stone*. The v2.0 headnote ran 175 words on seals and leave. Both are gone.

### 3.3 The fixed words of the hearth

These are the hearth's liturgy, and they never change:

- **The Invocation**, with the Title.
- **The teller's close:** *This I lay as it was laid for me.* {{Halyna}} close with *This we lay as it was laid for us.*
- **The Amen:** *And the hearth said: We remember.* It is the same every time. Its power is that it never varies.
- **The wood's ring:** *This is held in the grain.* … *and the grain holds it still.* Then *And no one answered.* This holds until VI.3, where a child answers.

The frame line around the Amen may change only a handful of times in the Book, and each time for a reason:
- after IV.3 the hearth is slow to follow;
- after I.5, and once more later, it waits a breath for Ebba;
- in VI.3 a child answers the wood.

Seren names the vow once, in the foreword. After that the Book never explains it.

### 3.4 The teller's asides

The aside is the tale's punctuation at its high moments. It points at someone in the hall, in the present tense:

- *He is sitting there now, at the end of the bench, with his hood up.*
- *Merrick is sitting by the door tonight, and he has not gone out.*
- *Look to the end of the bench, you children at the front.*

Rules for the aside:
- Two at most in a tale, and only at the turn or the close.
- Never to explain.
- Never to say where a tale came from. For that a teller says *I did not see it. Hale did.*
- The present presses on the past, as when Odysseus hides his tears at the singer's song and only the king notices. The aside should change the listener, or show that he is changed.

### 3.5 What leaves the tales

- **"Hear how…" openings that tell the outcome** (v2.0 has 22): all of them go. A call to listen that names no outcome may stay, once in a leaf, at the head of a catalogue: *Hear now the names of the havens, and hold them, for they are ours.*
- **Editorial rules** go into the foreword, said once: the blots in place of the warden's name, the Thrones written by their own names, the two inks, and both tellings kept where they disagree.
- **The annal after a story** is as short as a saying. IV.3's annal runs 150 words at most.
- ***Added in Seren's hand*** stays only where the note is itself a turn, and three times at most in the whole Book:
  - Keep: V.5 Count I, *It was not the last.*; VI.1, the child who lights the north lamp.
  - Cut: IV.1's two glosses; IV.4's note decoding the arms; III.2's records.
  - Cut IV.3's *It was the half of a holy thing.* It also breaks the lock: the cross-sign is not holy.
- **The Arguments of the six Books** are one line each and spoil nothing. v1.3's tags are the model (*How we came to stand.* · *The wrong, told from both sides.*). Jack's line in Book Five stays word for word.

### 3.6 Refrains: which stay, which turn, which go

- **The liturgy** (§3.3) stays, unchanged.
- **A teller's opening formula** is said once a leaf, not once a part. Kael says *Give me the stone. No, I will stand.* once at the head of V.5.
- ***Hold the stone, Seren.*** may open her leaves. Its second half, *I tell myself so every time*, is said once. It is turned in the Epilogue: *There is no one now to finish it, and I say both halves alone.*
- ***And the Captain put his hood back.*** Tell the habit once, in the foreword. Write it into a tale twice more at most, where it bites:
  - after IV.3, the first Amen over a killing by our own, when he bares his head for the Guest;
  - in the Epilogue, for {{Halyna}}.

  The strips of cloak carry the other deaths.
- **The strip of cloak** is told whole at Jory, then given a clause, then paid in V.5 Count VI and the Epilogue.
- ***I did not understand it then. I think I do now.*** belongs to Voss, once, at Ebba's *Perhaps* (I.5).
- **Seren's three sayings of a word** are shown whole once (V.1, *shore*), then echoed at most twice (VI.3, *Naelear*; the Knowings).
- ***Look close.*** stays. It changes hands, from Pellow to Ruan, so it is a refrain that builds.
- ***I swear to that.*** comes as often as Kael would say it, which is once or twice a leaf.

With headnotes of fifty words or fewer, the Book comes to about 31,000 words. A wood leaf's reading counts toward its tale's budget.

**How to cut.** Jack likes every element. So the words come out of the joining, never out of the beats (R6b §0).

1. **Cut the joints.** Every *", and…; and…"* chain becomes full stops, or loses its weakest clause.
2. **Cut the scaffold and keep the picture.** An *As when … so* simile becomes a clause.
3. **Cut the tag.** Drop *he and she* and *the two of them*. An epithet appears at the first naming and where it bites, and nowhere else.
4. **Cut the retelling.** Each beat is told whole once in the Book (the ledger in §2.2).
5. **Cut the explanation and the cross-reference.** The facing leaf does that work, silently.
6. **Cut the time-stamp and the "Hear how".**
7. **Squeeze the road.** Years go in a sentence.
8. **Keep every liked element, as a line if it cannot be a paragraph.** v2.0's best lines are mostly single sentences already (R5 §2): *▒▒▒▒ kicked it aside.* · *On his back is the other half.* · *Tarnel did not wake.* · *The wood took it.*

> *Then, in the last of the light, the Captain of the Torn Cloak came down from the wall. I had seen the cloak go up, and heard the soldiers shout, and knew not what it was to us. Into the middle of the courtyard he walked, a man not tall, with the coal-black yet on his fingers and the half of a cloak upon his shoulders, and looked at the elders, and past them at the broken gate, and stood; and he asked one question, and it is the only speech of his that anyone at this hearth can repeat:*

After (70 words). Every element is kept: the cloak seen by a child who did not understand it, the coal, the half cloak, the elders, the gate, the one question.

- the clause that stages a walk to the middle of a yard we are already in.

### 4.2 Play

**Seren plays on the road and goes plain at the grave.** Her turns of phrase belong in the openings, the catalogues, the frame, the turns of thought between deeds, and the closing sayings. They do not belong at the kick, the blow, the death, or the first instant of a knowing. There the plainest word wins. *▒▒▒▒ kicked it aside.* is better than any cleverer verb.

**Words that work twice.** The Book's own stock. Set each one up once, then let it bite.

| Word | Its two senses | Where it bites |
|---|---|---|

| *tell*, *teller* | to recount; to count | Kael: *A teller am I, and no singer* (R5 §9) |
| *course* | a row of stone; a night's tale | the founders' leaf (*one course each night*); the house *one course high* |
| *hold*, *holding* | to keep in the hand; the third hour of the siege day; a fortress | *Hold it, and it will hold you*; *the grain holds it still* |
| *keep* | to keep a vow, a light, a count; the Keep itself | *The stone kept its vow. It was we who did not keep ours.* |
| *bond* | the bond of mortar; the Aetherbond | *as two courses become one wall when the mortar takes* |
| *mark* | a pair's Stonwryt; marks on the wood; the wound on an arm | *Marks on the wood.*; the mark on the warden's arm |
| *sentence* | words joined; a doom | IV.4, *We were the sentence.*; V.1, *a sign without a sentence is only a mark* |

**Her turns.**
- **The mirror.** *The stone kept its vow. It was we who did not keep ours.* · *I carried the lie in a day and a night. I carried the truth forty years, and gave it to no one.*
- **The litany that ends on the wrong thing.** The count of the black wood ends: *He slept in it, and was proud.*
- **Not much, and not little.** *I say it so you will know it is not much. Nor is it little.*
- **The withheld thought.** *I have written the name, and I will not write what I thought.* · *I have not asked them what it means, and they have not told me.*
- **The epithet turned.** Kael the Counter stops counting. Voss who asked answers. Seren Two-Inks writes at last in one ink.
- **The refusal at the hinge.** *We know not why. Fear leaves no mark on wood.*
- **The saying at the close.** *We were a people of doors.*

**Painted pictures.** One to a movement. Each is fifteen words or fewer, concrete, and from the teller's own trade. These, already in our tales, are the measure:
- *It is a sound like ice going out on a river.*
- *Thirty-one there are, and four of them move.*
- *The ship-masters knocked out their pipes on the lowest one.*
- *the way my mother did when I had a fever* (IV.3's one simile: the enemy likened to a mother)
- *A fledgling could have slept in that hand.*
- *as the halves of a split log meet*
- *like teeth from a jaw*
- *His glass lay by his hand, and it was whole.*

**Her habit.** She says a word three times before she trusts it to the ink. It is shown whole once (V.1) and echoed twice at most (§3.6). In the foreword she may say *I love our tongue.* She never lectures on a word. v2.0's II.1 stops to explain *point*. The better way is to use the word so that it works: *No mason came to point its mortar, and the rain found every joint.*

### 4.4 The tellers agree that her version is best

Show this lightly, with wit, in three places. Never say it in so many words.

1. **I.1, the headnote** (§3.2). Kael forbade her to change a word. She changed a good many, and he swore it was word for word as he had told it.
2. **V.5, the headnote, six winters on.** *This time he brought me the counts himself, and bade me mend them as I mended the first. I have mended them less.*
3. **II.3, already in the text.** {{Idrenna}}, the eldest pair, sit by the door and do not nod. Then they nod.

The foreword states the rule once: the tale is the teller's, the words are Seren's, and no teller yet has asked for his own back. Nowhere else.

## 5 · The wood-leaf form

This settles the open question in R6b §0.5. {{Halyna}} give the pieces in their own two voices. Seren then tells the memory whole, in the wood's own *we*. That keeps the ring and the wood's *you* to the Shore. Her hand shows in the shaping, and the story holds only what the wood could know.

### 5.1 The order of a wood leaf

1. **Dateline and headnote.** Whose hand was on the wood, and where the other of them was. One sentence.
2. **The reading.** {{Halyna}}, as the wood gives the memory up:
   - four to eight lines, about 80 words at most, and less than a tenth of the tale;
   - the two voices alternate in two inks, and neither is marked; a thought may break across both;
   - nouns and verbs more than sentences; the present tense, because they are inside it; corrections (*no —*); *we two*;
   - only what the wood gives, plus their own effort to tell (*no*, *wait*, *cold*, *we two have it*): no Shore knowledge and no names;
   - the reading should break off at or near the tale's halt, so that the story fulfils what the reading could not finish;
   - set it in italic under `<!-- READING -->`, with the inks alternating line by line;
   - where the wood was broken, or the telling failed, mark the gap and never mend it.
3. **The story.** *This is held in the grain.* Then the memory, whole, in the wood's *we*:
   - it builds as a stone leaf builds: a question, a rising, the halt, the turn, the close;
   - it has one hard image at its centre;
   - it never decodes and never explains a motive;
   - the rite is told as it happens, never in advance.
4. **The return.** *…and the grain holds it still.* Then *And no one answered.*

### 5.2 What the wood can know

The wood can know:
- touch, weight, cold, fire, blood, iron, the sound that passes through it (laughter, blows), and a long dark;
- its kin in sawn timber, and that they are dead;
- words spoken over it, and what came to it through the blood of the long-lived, or through the roots.

The wood cannot know:
- the names of Shore people;
- a motive on the Shore (*Fear leaves no mark on wood*);
- a Shore word for a thing. It says *the mute thing*, *shore-men*, *the iron*, *the breath*;
- the date, or the Shore's name for a place, unless {{Halyna}} had to give one in the telling, and then sparingly.

Shore facts go in the headnote, or nowhere. The facing leaf is the reader's to set beside it. The gap between the two leaves belongs to the reader (R2 §11), and one leaf never explains the other.

## 6 · The never-list

**1. Run-on chains.**
- Clauses stacked with *, and…; and…; for…*. Example: v2.0 I.2's first sentence, 78 words.
- The *As when … so* scaffold. Example: the loaf in I.1, and nineteen like it.
- A simile that explains itself with a *for*. Example: *for she knows already which sail is lost* (V.7).

**2. The annal or history-book voice.**
- Datelines in capitals, and *seventy-five years before the banner*.
- *In the sixth autumn, when the first frosts lay white of a morning upon the walls we had raised again, the tenth Throne fell…* (VI.3).
- Names arriving like entries in a register, as in *Tarnard at their head, the eldest boatwright, of Tidesmeet, where the river meets the tide*.
- A record after the story's end. Example: v2.0's IV.3 annal of 585 words.

**3. Explanation interrupting.**
- A glossary at the climax. v1.3 I.2 spends 188 words on pair-names and lineages between *Then Halyna stood up* and the cry.
- A rite told before it is done (IV.4 in v1.3 and v2.0).
- Cross-references at the moment of wonder. Example: *I turned back the leaves of this Book…* (VI.3).
- Rulebook passages (I.2's laying, hauling and holding).

**4. Invented custom or lore that explains a deed.**
- Tobe's *"What the grey sends, the fire sends back, or it comes again"*, and every echo of it: IV.5's "old words of the coast", V.1's "old way of the coast", the Epilogue's "the custom".
- Any new *why*: a custom, a lineage, an editor's reason.

**5. Summary where a scene belongs.**
- v1.3 IV.1 reports the clerk's line (*there is a line in a clerk's small hand… that the stumps had stopped*). v2.0 stages it: the lamp bent twice, and the line itself, *All along the edge, the stumps put up nothing.* Stage it.
- A tale with no halt. A tale with twelve halts is the same fault.

**6. Abstraction where a thing would do.**
- *holy pleas reduced to ornament*. Write instead: *the ship-masters knocked out their pipes on the lowest one*.
- *the trappings of a rich man's vanity*. Write the black wood in the bed-frame.
- Verdicts in the narration: *cruel*, *brave*, *terrible*, *heartbreaking*.

**7. Modern idiom.**
- Contractions anywhere in the Book (*It's only a rhyme*; *I'm fighting*).
- Poughkeepsie words (Le Guin): *okay*, *really*, *focus*, *process*, *trauma*, *closure*.
- Modern thought in old dress (Tolkien's "insincerity").
- The one modern register is the warden's letter.

**8. Parody archaism.**
- *upon* and *ere* as costume.
- *-est* or *-eth* on the wrong person. *{{Halyna}} saith* is wrong.
- *thee* as a subject; *thou* for distance.
- Yoda order: three inversions in a row with no weight behind them.
- Tushery: *forsooth*, *prithee*, *mayhap*, *perchance*, *methinks* piled up.
- Scripture formulas: *it came to pass*, *and lo*, *verily*.
- False friends whose sentence does not fix the old sense.

**Also never:**
- **Holy marks.** *Lord*, *amen*, *saint*, *church*. Never *holy* for the cross-sign.
- **Lock breaches.** A singular *they*. *He and she* after the foreword. Naming the warden. Brenn's hands on the Guest, the body or the fire. The shared ancestor of the two tongues revealed. The parting of the peoples told.
- **Changed fixed matter.** Any change to a song or to Jack's lines.
- **Stacked endings.** v2.0's V.7 Part I closes ten times.
- **Refrains left unchanged** (§3.6).

## 7 · Plain Words, beside it

Plain Words is a real retelling in easy modern English (R5 §8; R3 §3.4). It is not the Book re-punctuated.

- **Retell from the beats, not the sentences.** Start in the scene, and put each fact where a modern reader needs it. Test: no more than about 20% of its four-word runs appear in the Book.
- **Easy, not choppy.**
  - Sentences of 11 to 14 words on average, under 30% of them below 10 words, and almost none over 30.
  - Everyday words, normal word order, and *because*, *so* and *but* instead of *for*.
  - Contractions, about 10 to 20 in every 1,000 words.
  - A Flesch-Kincaid grade of 4 or under.
- **Gloss once, in passing.** For example, *a course of stone, one layer of the wall*. Never a glossary paragraph.
- **One short comparison to a tale at most.** Never "Picture a…".
- **No apparatus.** The headnote is one line: who is telling, and when.
- **The same length as the Book, or shorter.** It keeps every event.
- **The voices survive in modern form.** Kael counts. Brenn confesses in short, guilty sentences. {{Halyna}}'s fragments stay fragments.
- **It leaves dark what the Book leaves dark.** It is easier, not more explained.
- **The fixed lines stay word for word.** The songs follow the existing Plain practice, with the Book's songs untouched.

## 8 · Before you hand in a draft

**The numbers for the Book** (R5 §9; R3 §2):
- Sentences average 12 to 16 words: a teller 10 to 15, Seren 12 to 18.
- About 9% of sentences run over 30 words, and 1% or fewer over 45.
- At most 0.1 semicolons and 0.7 *and*s to a sentence.
- The crisis third of the tale has the shortest sentences.
- No contractions.
- At most one extended picture to a tale.
- The budget holds.

**The questions:**
1. Can I say the tale's question in one line, and is it raised by the hundredth word?
2. Is there one halt, told close, at the deed?
3. Does each beat join the one before it by *therefore* or *but*?
4. Is every payoff planted earlier, as a deed, and paid without a gloss?
5. Is there anywhere a *for* of motive at the crisis, a custom, or an explanation? Cut it.
6. Does the tale end on a picture, with nothing after it but the fixed words?
7. Is every element R6 lists still here, as a line if not as a paragraph?
8. Does the teller sound like himself within three sentences, and like himself at his best?
9. Is the old English right (R3's tables), learned by meeting it again, and never costume?
10. Do the locks hold?
    - Brenn only watched.
    - ▒▒▒▒ gave the order.
    - Pair-names take plural verbs.
    - No singular *they*.
    - No holy cross-sign.
    - The songs and Jack's lines are word for word.
    - There is no custom of burning.
    - The Captain is unnamed.
11. Read it aloud to yourself, once. Would you lean in at the hearth? Would Seren have enjoyed writing it? If not, it is not done.
