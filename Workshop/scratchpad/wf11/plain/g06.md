## BOOK FOUR · THE BOOK OF THE WOUND

*The wrong that was done, told from both sides of the grey.*

### IV.1 · Of the Felling of the Black Pillars

*Seren Two-Inks laid this one from the Harvest rolls, the guild's old records of the felling. {{Halyna}} read them to me at night, and I copied so fast that my letters leaned like reeds in the wind.*

Hold the stone, Seren.

After a year of reading, one night in the thaw, we reached the last rolls in the chest. The oldest one's rotten cord snapped in Rhyna's fingers, and {{Halyna}} wouldn't read me a line until I'd written this down.

"We two didn't live it,"

The oldest roll calls the grey a dark haze at the sea's edge. After that, the night watch keeps asking what it is and who breathes it, and nobody answers. When the answer came, it wasn't in our writing.

In the havens' proudest days, ships sailed farther than ever. Where the grey began, they found rows of straight black pillars. The ship-masters called them Mystholders, because they looked like posts holding up a roof of grey. The wood was harder than oak, lighter than iron, and never rotted.

The Trade Council kept the axes going for two generations, and the rolls count every load. Listen and remember, because it's ours.

Black wood went into wall props, bridges, Tidesmeet's three spans and the posts under Fenholm. It made Holtward's roof beams, Highreach's cranes, Sandreach's galleries and Rimewatch's hall on the ice. Emberhythe's forges burned it slowly, and Carnhold hammered out the axes that went back for more.

Then it went into things that didn't need it: a lord's dance floor in Eldhythe, and the harbour houses' trading tables and dice boards.

*[A stain covers three lines here.]*

Last of all, a Tidesmeet merchant had a whole pillar carved into one bed. He slept in it, and he was proud.

Halvard turned the page, then turned it back.

Stranger still, every stump sent up straight black shoots, and the axemen came back for those, because the guild bought them as scaffold poles. They cut, and it grew back. They cut, and it grew back. They cut.

Rhyna's finger stopped under a count of poles, at a tiny line some clerk had squeezed in. Twice I brought the lamp closer before she could make it out.

*All along the edge, the stumps put up nothing.*

Nobody wrote why, and nobody asked. The roll doesn't say who wrote it, or I'd have kept his name.

The grey thinned every season, and the Council called it luck. I tried the word on my tongue, and it crumbled like old mortar.

The Stonewrights did warn the Council, in our own writing, on a page no thumb ever smudged:

*"These trees grow in strange soil. The carvings we find beside them bear a tongue we cannot read. Where wisdom is absent, let restraint be our guide."*

But by then trade came first, and our advice was grandmothers' fireside talk. It was read out once in Eldhythe's Council hall, then shelved.

And there really were carvings. By the pillars the axemen found standing stones carved in curling letters, as lovely as frost on glass. Nobody could read them. Some hung in harbour houses for show, over men drinking and doing sums. The guild locked the rest in its archives, with its own warning.

I carried a few up the mountain, in this chest by my knee. {{Halyna}} have touched them with four hands and got nothing. Stone does not translate.

In the rolls for one late summer, Halvard found a letter from the farthest post. A clerk had copied it between the pole and oar counts, just as neatly. Halvard read it out the way he'd read the oars.

*Unknown intruder. Hostile intent. Neutralized. Vessel burned and released.*

Under it a name is cut out, ▒▒▒▒, and Rhyna touched the blot and took her finger away. On the next page two timber boats are crossed off a cargo list, marked *lost at the edge* in the same handwriting.

I copied it like that clerk, in among the poles and oars.

I didn't know what I was holding.

Wisdom didn't stop the Harvest, but people changing their minds did. The near pillars ran short, and timber from the northern hills was cheaper. After the burning-boat year no crew would go where they could see the grey, and the rolls don't say why. So the axes stopped, and nobody decided it.

Then the havens forgot. The stones stayed on the walls, and children who asked what they said got no answer. Some of you here tonight were those children.

Late, very late, the pride wore off and the temples were mended. People came up our worn steps for advice again, as glad as men mending a sea wall in the calm. Nobody asked about the stones, and we said nothing. We turned the key ourselves, and that's our share of it.

But humility came too late.

The first dark sails were already on the sea.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

### IV.3 · The Warden's Door

*When Brenn of the outpost was an old man, {{Wendhessa}} of the guild at Eldhythe wrote down his words as he spoke them. {{Halyna}} read them at this hearth, and I wrote what comes after.*

"We two have the stone,"

"but these words aren't ours. We read them word for word."

I was nineteen and the runner, one of twelve under the warden at the farthest post, where the rock runs into the grey. Old Tobe had fished there thirty years. At fifteen I'd sworn on the runners' stone, palm down, "What I saw, I carry. I carry nothing else."

▒▒▒▒'s doorposts, table and bed frame were all black wood. Barely older than his runner, he'd never known battle, or patience. His father's seat on the Trade Council got him the post. His temper got him sent to the edge, where it could only hurt us twelve.

One late-summer morning a small grey boat came out of the grey, though there was no wind. A tall, pale stranger in a cloth like mist beached it and came to the warden's door, but ▒▒▒▒ stayed inside. The cloth moved when he didn't, and I saw no weapon.

His language was as loud as storm wind in old trees, and it scared us, because we didn't know that's how they talk. Then he said three of our words, carefully, like a man setting down something breakable.

"Stop… Sky… Dying…"

They were the right words, and he meant them, I know now. But words without a sentence are only sounds, and that's all we heard.

He knelt on the doorstep and set down a dark stone as big as two fists, like bread for a guest. Nobody moved, and a gull landed behind him and walked about.

▒▒▒▒ kicked it aside.

It rolled into the dirt by the wall, and his men laughed. I stood with them, and I still hear it.

The stranger got up and gently held the warden's neck in his right hand, the way my mother held me through fevers. He brought their foreheads together silently, and ▒▒▒▒ let him. If it had ended there, I think our lives would all be different.

Then, with a small blade in his left hand, he touched the warden's right arm.

▒▒▒▒ had never bled in his life, and when he tore away, the little cut went deep and bled.

"Seize him," he screamed. They grabbed the stranger, and he didn't fight. The blade in his hand was turned toward his own arm. Held between them, he knelt again as he had with his gift, and I didn't understand.

"Kill him," ▒▒▒▒ screamed. They snatched up sticks, beach stones and boat poles, and one man took mine, the pole I pushed boats off with.

He never raised a hand, and any cry he made sounded like wind to me. I counted the blows, and I still do. When he went still, ▒▒▒▒ kept screaming, so some kept going. They broke both his arms, the gentle one and the one with the blade.

His men obeyed. We obeyed.

I say *we* because that day the post was all one thing, and I was in it. I never touched him. I did not stop them. I was a boy among the men.

Then it went quiet, and the gull was gone. They dragged him down the beach by his heels, his head hitting every stone, and threw him in his boat like a torn net.

▒▒▒▒ clutched his arm and didn't look at the boat. "Burn it," he said, "and shove it off, and give it the tide. Leave nothing behind."

They lit it, and it didn't burn like wood. Something in it screamed, and the stranger was dead, so it wasn't him. It was the wood itself.

They pushed it off with the same poles, and Tobe waded in knee-deep and washed them. The rest of us stood there, trying not to look, and looking.

Far out, the boat turned. It went into the wind and against the current, into the grey, still burning.

That day I watched ▒▒▒▒ tell the clerk what to write to the Council. I know it by heart:

*Unknown intruder. Hostile intent. Neutralized. Vessel burned and released.*

Not a word about the gift, or the kneeling.

Next morning, before anyone woke, I picked up the stone from the dirt, and I don't know why. ▒▒▒▒ handed me the letter at his door, so the lie and the gift shared a pack. I ran with it, saying the oath every mile.

In Eldhythe an old Council clerk with inky fingers read it and looked up. "Is it true?" I didn't say yes. I said, "It is written." I've weighed that clever answer for forty years.

I carried the lie for a day and a night. I've carried the truth forty years and given it to no one.

That autumn three axemen floated home on a mast, swearing a burning boat came out of the grey, and the shore laughed. I didn't, because I'd seen it turn, but I kept quiet. The Council had it in writing.

▒▒▒▒ wore long sleeves after that, even in hot Eldhythe summers, and I know why. He died last winter in his black wood bed, and I'm the last of the twelve.

For forty years I've carried the stone and never once held it. Now I give it up with these words, and ask God to forgive me, because only God is left to ask.

Brenn's words end there, and {{Halyna}} were silent at every blot. The warden's arm kept one mark, half of something, cut off in the middle. And the Council kept the letter.

Brenn died like a man putting down a load. The guild put his name on his grave, and cut the warden's name out.

{{Wendhessa}} sealed Brenn's words and never told a soul. At the Fall they carried the chest out, a handle each, until their arms gave out on the Road. The masons called it worthless and gave it to Tarnel and me, the youngest. Tarnel knotted his net one-handed, and I watched the knots.

{{Wendhessa}} lay down one night on the pass and never got up. Tarnel and I slept by the chest, a hand each on a handle, and by morning frost covered us both.

Tarnel did not wake.

I stopped there tonight, hands on the stone, and {{Halyna}} put theirs beside mine.

No people has a law older than this: whoever kneels at your door is under your roof. We were a people of doors.

We have laid this tale the way it was laid for us.
