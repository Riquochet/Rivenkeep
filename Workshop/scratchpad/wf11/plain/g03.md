### I.4 · The Rite of the Cornerstone

*Told by Seren Two-Inks, the third in {{Halyna}}'s household. In the Fourteen Nights I was a child of the guild, and nobody trusted me with anything but water.*

Hold the stone, Seren. I tell myself that every time.

A moving light woke me three nights after the cry. Rhyna was up, and at that moment Halvard took the lamp. I didn't call out but watched it sink to a spark down a well.

Rhyna's mother used to sing {{Aldwena}}'s family rhyme over their cradle, but nobody asked what it meant.

> *Under the hall, and under the floor,*  
> *under the stairs that go down no more,*  
> *down where the rope let the first stone go,*  
> *the first two keep what the first two know.*

Rhyna knew where to look. She let out the rope as Halvard went down the shaft, and neither spoke.

They told me a black stone chest stood at the bottom, under dust a finger thick. Halvard wiped the lid with his sleeve.

It was the mark of {{Aldwena}}.

::: native legend_stonwryt_aldwena chip
The mark of {{Aldwena}}, on the lid of the black chest: the same mark that's cut on the bottom of the tale-stone
:::

The chest held the Rite on slates, slates about building for war, the founders' record and a staff. Halvard shook the three stone flasks, but nothing moved.

By the fire {{Halyna}} read out what the slates meant, a line, a breath, a line. I put down my bucket and forgot it.

<!-- HALYNA -->

"This is the Rite of the Cornerstone,"

"which sets a keeper over a great work, to build and keep it till it stands."

<!-- HALYNA END -->

Then the staff's rule.

<!-- HALYNA -->

"Each staff is made for one work and never leaves it,"

"and once the work is done, it's laid down there."

<!-- HALYNA END -->

The slates below made them pause longer: walls sloped so shots bounce off, gates slanted against rams, stonework laid to fall outward if broken. The first builders had known war, but the slates don't say what they fought.

The Rite needed three oils nobody alive had made. For the cedar, Stannard led {{Halyna}} through snow to the grove that gave the first staff. They wouldn't cut a live tree, so he rapped the fallen trunks with a knuckle and listened. "I know which ones will hold."

They boiled down the heartwood here, and on wet nights the stones still smell of it.

We children scraped the pale crust off long-forgotten stones with old chisels. Masons call it *bloom*, because it flowers where nobody comes. It was my first job that wasn't water, and it rubbed my fingers raw.

The mountain had no myrrh. {{Idrenna}} had carried a flask of it all the way for the guild's dead, who couldn't be buried on the pass. Rhyna warmed it into the cedar for the Captain, and the hall went still, like a street when a coffin passes.

I knew who it had been for.

All night {{Idrenna}}'s empty flask sat on the hearthstone, and nobody touched it. The Captain came in late from the wall, mortar to his wrists, looked at it and left.

The fourteenth day brought the first thin sun since the banner. The new capstone, cut by Stannard and smoothed by {{Orvenna}}, lay in the yard with {{Idrenna}} beside it, flasks on their knees. The Captain stood where the slate put him, hand on the stone, facing the people.

He didn't like it. He stood there.

Over the gate the torn cloak rose once and fell, and I heard snow-melt in the old gutters.

Then {{Idrenna}} got up, each leaning on the other, and four old hands emptied the flasks onto his bowed head. All winter their hands had shaken, but not on the flasks. One named the oils, and the other said what they meant:

"Cedar, for endurance. Granite-bloom, for strength. Myrrh, for the burden of command."

The oil soaked his collar, and he let it. At the front we children showed each other our raw hands and smiled, our first smiles since the passes.

{{Idrenna}} offered the guild's staff, polished wood capped with fine polished stone. Every leader of a great Stonewright work has carried one since the founding, and this was the first. The Captain looked at it a long while before reaching out.

Four hands laid it in two.

With it he was named Commander of Rivenkeep, and stood still in the shouting.

That night, by my lamp, {{Halyna}} carved their mark and vow under the new stone, one holding the chisel, the other striking. Their mark isn't mine to draw. Next to the vow they put words that aren't a vow, copied stroke for stroke from the cloak.

::: native legend_title_capstone line
The Title in the course-hand, cut under the new capstone of the inner gate in the Captain's own strokes
*Ul dumol ol varn, ol theldeth, ol Mardh, ol lunnath, ol sollan.* In memory of our home, our families, our God, our freedoms, our peace.
:::

**IN MEMORY OF OUR HOME, OUR FAMILIES, OUR GOD, OUR FREEDOMS, OUR PEACE.**

The writing was the Captain's, big and slow, and neither of them had corrected one stroke.

The True Men set the stone in the gate, mark down. The Captain propped the staff in the gatehouse corner, because the wall and the war were his work. He went back to the wall in his half-cloak. Nobody's moved the staff since. If you doubt me, go and see the dust on it.

With the stone up and the tools clean, {{Halyna}} sent me down alone with the lamp to put the slates back. I went down where I'd seen their light go, and lifted the empty flasks.

There was one more slate.

Its letters were big and careful like a child's, and mine still are. I brought it up in my shirt, cold on my skin. By the fire {{Halyna}} read me what the youngest apprentice of the First Days wrote.

<!-- HALYNA -->

"With the stone up and the tools clean, we gather at the hearth, and one of us lays a tale."

"One row goes on the wall each night, so the wall and tale rise together."

<!-- HALYNA END -->

So this hearth came before the havens. We didn't begin it. We forgot it, and found it again in the bottom of a chest. The next night Kael, who wouldn't sit, stood and laid the first tale, about the Captain.

The people called him Commander. In his own heart he was still only the Captain.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

### I.5 · The Naming of the Rivenmen

*Told by Voss who asked, of Eldhythe, True Man of the harbour battery. He grew up in his grandmother's harbour-house on the dock.*

Give me the stone. I've owed this hearth my answer for years.

Late that first winter I put a friend under stones on the east face, where a spade won't dig. As boys we'd turned the skipping rope for the little ones on the dock, and I laid his last stone myself.

His name was Jory.

That night, cold, I asked this fire a hard question: "Why do we fight for ice and stone?"

Stannard knocked on the hearthstone once and said, "It's not the ice I'm fighting for." Stannard's answer was good, but it was his, and mine took the whole war to carve. My grandmother said a slow answer is like a slow tide, washing across the same stones.

A week after the Rite, the whole Keep gathered in the yard below the torn cloak. I was in the slush with the companies, and Jory, for once in his life, wasn't whistling. The Captain was among us, without the staff.

Kael the Counter climbed the rubble by the gate and said one thing, once.

"The Commander fought."

The whole guild finished it together, from {{Idrenna}} to the smallest water-carrier:

"The Stonewrights built."

That was the whole speech, and it was enough. Soldiers and masons, we looked at each other. We weren't two peoples sharing a ruin, but one, the way mortar makes two rows of stone one wall.

That day we gave the land the Keep's name. Each haven held by the grey sails is Rivenkeep, right to the sea, and that's a vow. In that yard we became the Rivenmen, and we call our fathers Shorelanders and ourselves just *we*.

All day we climbed to the stone below the pike. Each touched it and swore, like men tying up their boats at evening. Jory went before me and came back down whistling, and by nightfall the Keep was held.

We swore to take back every stone. Not one stone that wasn't ours. But every one that was.

The old ones disliked the new name but kept quiet, and still mumble "the Shorelands" into their shawls. This winter, after the ten great hulls settled into the havens, I asked Ebba of Fenholm why she kept the old name. She held her hands out to the fire without looking up.

"It's a name to come home to, when the last wall stands, and the last Mystarch lies broken."

Then she added one word, to the fire, not me.

"Perhaps."

I didn't understand it then, but I think I do now.

Four nights ago she stayed late at that bench-end by the coals with her shawl to her chin, and we left her sleeping. The fire burned low in the night, and nobody fed it.

In the morning she didn't wake.

At dusk we buried her beside the soldiers on the east face, and {{Idrenna}} prayed over her in two halves. Her grandson Marl set the last stone, saying "the Shorelands" over it, and nobody corrected him. The Captain tied a strip off his half-cloak to a pike.

This hearth's answer, *We remember*, came from her, and most nights she started it a moment before the rest. Now it starts with a gap one old voice wide, and nobody's tried to fill it.

So here's mine, and you can weigh it. Jory, who I asked it for, never heard me ask.

On the east wall I once said, "We do not fight because we are right. We fight because our children are behind these walls."

It's true, but not all of it: I have no child of my own, and still I said *our*.

From the east tower you can see the keeps we built in this war, each with a red pennant. But a keep isn't for staying in forever. It's for going out from.

We don't fight for ice and stone. Every stone of this land is Rivenkeep, and Rivenkeep is home, and we swore on the banner to bring it home.

Old Ebba stays on the east face, and Marl has her stone from Fenholm in his pack.

Tomorrow we go down to the havens.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*The hearth waited a moment for Ebba.*

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*
