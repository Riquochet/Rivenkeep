## BOOK THREE · THE BOOK OF THE HAVENS

*Ten havens, our cities by the sea, and the ten men who speak for them: what we had, and how we walked away from it.*

### III.1 · Of Their Raising

*The True Men told this, each for his own haven, standing to answer Kael the Counter as they had on the day of the banner. {{Halyna}} had found the records in the chest I carried, and I read out the parts nobody remembered.*

Give me the stone. No, I'll stand for a roll call.

At dawn I could still see the coast from the east tower, and no hearth on it smoked. Hold on to the havens' names, because they belong to us. I won't speak for another man's city. As oath-keeper I call each haven, and its own man swears what he'll do when it comes home.

*First I read the records. Our fathers were builders, not sailors, and feared the open sea. They built where the mountainsides meet the water, and laid roads like veins over them. Each record begins:* Today we laid the first row and cut a staff for the work. *The clerks' writing is small and careful, as if they hated wasting paper. Stonewrights built every wall. Ten havens, ten staffs.*

**Eldhythe, the Eldest Haven.**

Corlen of the harbour. For fifty years the boats rode the evening tide past my dock, slowly, to the coast's warmest harbour-houses. When our first boat comes home, I'll steer it in.

*Then Corlen, eldest of the Twelve, sat and held up a wet finger in this windless hall, as he does before every fight. When young men laughed from the back, he told them what he tells eager gunners: "Wait. The sea hasn't finished."*

**Tidesmeet, where the river meets the tide.**

I'm Tamm of the three spans, and my city stood on both banks, joined by three great bridges.

*He set three stones in order by the fire, as he does by his blanket each night.* One span is only a plank, but three make a city. I'll set them out on the riverbank where the spans stood.

**Fenholm, the town that was always sinking.**

I'm Marl the fen-born, and Fenholm stood on posts in the marsh, sinking a hand's width a lifetime. Each generation put a new row on top, and any fen-man who can't laugh at that belongs on a hill. My grandmother, old Ebba, laid the top row of our house. I carry one of its stones, and I'd carry her too if she'd let me. That's her nodding by the fire, and I'm to set her stone on the old row.

**Holtward, the keep under the canopy.**

Aske of the canopy. Holtward sat so deep in the trees you could pass under it without seeing a wall. We cut no branch, and swept the leaves off the wall-walk by hand each autumn.

*Aske touched each plank of wreck-wood before it burned.* These were trees once, and while I live, no axe comes into Holtward.

**Carnhold, the stronghold of the cairns.**

Garvel of the smithies. Carnhold stood among the cairns, the first quarrymen's graves, and you could tell each forge by its ring. I listen to every gun like a mother hearing a cough, and a gun that rings true shoots true. But at a Carnhold anvil I'll make something that isn't a gun and hear it ring.

**Emberhythe, the forge on the burning shore.**

Harl of the burning shore. Emberhythe lay where the mountain smokes into the sea, on ground so warm our children went barefoot in the snow. I'll take my shore back and burn the grey, and the sea too, if it would burn.

**Sandreach, the citadel carved in the canyon wall.**

I'm Ulden of the galleries, and though Sandreach had no harbour, it was still our haven, because it sheltered us. It was dug, not built, gallery under gallery in the red rock. Until Sandreach fell, I slept each night under the whole mountain, my ear on the floor, hearing it hold. I'll sleep under that roof again.

**Rimewatch, the watch on the frozen mere.**

I'm Hesk of the mere, and our great hall stood on the frozen lake all winter, with the town feasting warm inside. At dusk my mother's candle lit the lamps in each window, and fishermen out on the lake steered home by them. Tonight one burns on the north wall, and I'll light it on the ice again.

**Highreach, the tower of the floating stones.**

Lanner of the crags. Our cliffs were so high every row went up by crane, hanging on the wind like hawks over a field. I smell a wind a day away, and our boys whistle one up before they can walk. I'll climb the highest again and whistle for a wind.

**And Glasspire, the last and proudest.**

Pellow of the spire. Seven generations built Glasspire, and it flashed in the sun like a blade of ice. The great glass on top sent sunlight a day's sail out to sea. My father ground it. I carried pieces here in straw and ground them into far-glasses. When Glasspire comes home, I'll be the first to see it.

*Then Pellow, youngest of the Twelve, held his glass to the fire, as he does with everything, even his supper. "Look close," he told the children in front.*

Ten cities, ten harbours, ten peoples who called themselves one nation. Under the banner, twelve of us answered, and tonight I hold ten oaths. Stannard of the ridge swore nothing, because the ridge wasn't a haven. He rapped once on the hearthstone, and he trusts no stone he hasn't knocked on. I have no haven.

The Captain found me on a road.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*

### III.2 · Of Their Fall

*The True Men told this one too, each for the fall of his own city, on a night of hard frost while the guns cooled and the mortar was still wet. Kael the Counter called them, and I spoke for the guild.*

Give me the stone. No, I'll stand for this one. You learned their names in autumn, so hold them as they fall, and don't look away. We didn't, when we told it.

In the last spring of peace, twenty months before the banner, swallows nested in the gate arches, and nobody ever broke a nest. The havens had no warships, and their walls were built for feast days and weather. At night their wide gates stood open.

Then, without warning, the dark sails came out of the grey. No messenger came that anyone understood, only a knocking from the grey like endless thunder. Our soldiers call that the drums. We watched those ships for a captain, a crew, one man on one rope, but the decks were empty. I swear to that.

So we called it the ghost fleet, and its senders the Mystaeri, after the mist. A man has to name his death. Those sails were simpler than today's, and each came alone, and one was enough. No haven sent for help, since none had any to give. *Then each man took the stone in turn, Corlen first.*

**Eldhythe.**

On that spring's first morning, the dark sails rode the rising tide into the harbour. All day the harbour burned, first the boats at the dock and then the harbour-houses, where the coast's ship-masters had sat by the fire.

*Then he waited, as he does at his guns, and the hall waited with him.*

That evening the wind spread smoke over the city, so the city never saw its harbour go. It held three months, and on its last evening the tide crept over the ash, as always. The sea hadn't finished. *Corlen passed the stone to Tamm and never held it again.*

**Tidesmeet.**

It took three days, and from the high road we saw and heard the spans fall into the river. The upper went, then the lower, and the middle lasted longest, and we waited, and it went. After dark I climbed down for a stone from each.

**Fenholm.**

It sank in one night, as if the marsh had been waiting. A town's just new stone on old, we'd say, laughing, but nobody laughed that night. My grandmother carried her stone out on her back, and wouldn't give it to me until the marsh covered the last row.

**Holtward.**

It burned with its trees, and burning leaves came down on the wall-walk we'd swept. I touched the last tree standing, and its bark was warm, like a living hand.

**Carnhold.**

When its forges burned, I heard the smiths' hammers stop one after another, until the last anvil rang on alone.

Then it stopped.

**Emberhythe.**

It held five months on its burning shore, and the column spent the winter there, warm on bare ground. As it fell, a mason carried out a burnt plank in his pack. I told him to burn it.

**Sandreach.**

We lost it gallery by gallery. Each night, with my ear on the floor, I'd hear the one above me go before any runner came. At last we got out at the canyon's foot, in the dark. Every night since, I've slept on open ground with my ear to it.

**Rimewatch.**

On the first ice, fire caught the great hall on the lake, its lamps still lit. The fire went window by window, like my mother's candle at dusk, and it burned the way it used to shine. I went into the smoke and took her lamp from its window, still lit.

I headed for land over new ice, and it talked under me, far off, then near. Once the wind nearly put the flame out, and I cupped my hand round it till it rose. The roof fell in behind me, and ahead the only light was in my hand.

The ice held.

I brought the lamp out lit. Since then I've lit it on our north wall every dusk, and the night-watch steer by it.

Someone has to keep a light.

**Highreach.**

The wind comes before the sails. I smelled the grey on it a whole day ahead, and that day was good for nothing but leaving. We carried the children down the cliff stairs in the dark, the cranes burning on the crags behind us.

**Glasspire.**

At first snow it sank into its own cellars overnight, spire and glass together, and the dark hole is still open. Some who escaped say a shadow bigger than any ship stood in that night's fog. I stood on the last stair, with the glass falling round me in pieces, and the snow too.

I looked close. I saw nothing.

*Then Pellow sat down without holding up his glass, and gave me his place at the stone.*

On each haven's last day, when masons' hands could do nothing more, the guild left by the land gate with only their chisels. The masters went last, with their trowels cleaned as usual and wrapped in sacks for the road.

The Stonewrights did not fight. They simply walked, and I was one of them.

They left each haven's staff against its falling wall. It's what a woman does on her final night in her mother's house: she covers the fire with ash, like any other night. A staff is cut for its work and stays with it. Not one of the ten staffs came up the mountain.

*Then Kael took the stone back for the Road.*

After Glasspire fell, the column began to climb the mountain. It had grown haven by haven, and none was left behind it. We spent a month in the passes, and on the night of the pass, when the cold was worst, many children died. Some old people died with them. They'd given the children their cloaks.

By morning it was still snowing, and whoever could get up, got up and walked. I counted us at the bottom of the passes and again at the top. I won't tell you the difference.

I have laid this tale the way it was laid for me.

*And everyone at the hearth said: We remember.*
