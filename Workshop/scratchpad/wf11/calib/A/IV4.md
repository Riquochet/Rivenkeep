### IV.4 · The Gift Held an Hour

*The third spring, in the Tide of Remembering.*

*Known from the gift itself. Rhyna held it an hour at the hearth, and it came into her slow, as water over stone. Halvard, across the ward at the mortar, touched it not, and began the telling. The last of it is of me; I have let it stand.*

— *Old. Dark, and old. Stone — no. Not stone. A tree, once.*  
— *Hands. His. Warm, a long while. Three words in him —*  
— *— and naught to join them. We two have it.*  
— *A door. Our kin in the posts. Sawn. They answer not.*  
— *Kneeling. Set down. His best. A foot —*  
— *Dust. Laughter, through the dust.*  
— *A neck, held. Gently. Two brows, one —*  
— *The blade. And he draweth back —*

This is held in the grain.

Eldest wood are we. A tree we were when the world was green to its edges. We fell, and the ages lay down on us, leaf on leaf, till time forgat us and we grew dark and hard. Ye took us for stone. We are not stone.

Wood that time hath forgotten forgetteth not. It giveth slowly, and only what is true. Of all the gifts the long-lived give, the dearest is a sliver of such wood. We were given once.

Aelrhen he was named when he took his errand: bond-to-stone. A long life he listened across the breath, to the shore-men at their nets and at their walls. He gathered your words as a child gathereth fallen leaves, one and one and one. At the end he had three, and knew them for the right ones. Join them he could not. Your tongue joineth its words with small words, and small words make no sound across water.

But he had us. Whoso held us while a tree's shadow moveth the breadth of its own trunk would know the whole of it. We were the sentence.

He chose the outermost of your holds, for its doors and its benches were of our wood. He took that for reverence. We knew it for what it was: our kin from the edge, sawn and polished. At the door he laid his hand on the post and greeted them. They answered not, for they were dead.

He kneeled on the stones before the master of that house, and set us down. One that cometh under a roof bringeth his best; and we were his best. In the thunder of his own tongue he spake the name of the rite, *Ael'thar*. After it he spake his three words, in yours.

"Stop… Sky… Dying…"

A foot struck us into the dust by the wall. Laughter came to us through the dust.

He rose. He took the back of the host's neck in his right hand, there where life goeth up into thought. A fledgling could have slept in that hand. He drew their heads together. The foreheads met, thought to thought, and he was silent.

With his left hand he set the blade to the host's arm.

And the host drew back. We know not why. Fear leaveth no mark on wood.

The blade that was to go a little way went deep, for the arm was moving. The host's blood ran. He cried out, and his men came.

Then Aelrhen's blood came across the step to where we lay, and with it came his last hour. So we know it as he knew it.

Between their hands he went down on his knees again. On the knees the rite beginneth. He knew the blows as a tree knoweth the iron: one, and another. He lifted not his hand. The cut he would have made on his own arm was never made. Never were the two arms pressed together, shoulder to shoulder, that harm to the one should be harm to both.

He died there, with the rite half made.

Of all the things that ever lay at a shore-man's feet and looked like stone, we were the one that could have spoken to him. And it was the one his foot found.

In the morning a hand took us out of the dust, and put us away, and held us not.

Then a jolting in the dark, a day and a night. Then the dark, half a life, and never a hand. Then a wrapping of leaves about us, and cut stones cold beside us, and another dark as long. Then old arms, two pairs, slow, a long while; and fire near and far. Then small arms, two pairs, going up, in the cold. Then one pair, a long way.

None held us long, till ye held us while a tree's shadow moveth the breadth of its own trunk.

Here is the sentence, as it was brought to the door:

*Stop the felling of the pillars at the edge of the grey, for they are not timber. The sky over us is their breath, and ours, and when you cut them it comes open, and our children cannot live in the open sun. We are here, behind the grey, and we have been here always, and we are not your enemies. Stop, for the sky is dying, and we are dying under it.*

That is all he came to say; and the grain holdeth it still.

*And no one answered.*
