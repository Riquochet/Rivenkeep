### I.1 · The Torn Cloak

*The first winter, the night after the Rite.*

*Laid by Kael the Counter, the first night that this hearth asked a tale. He forbade me to change one word of it. I changed a good many; and when I read it him, he sware it was word for word as he had told it.*

Give me the stone. No, I will stand. Enough have I sat this winter.

By the lintel of the western stair I swear it, where the old wardens cut their names. A teller am I, and no singer. What I told not, that will I not tell.

Twelve were we that followed the Captain. One by one he found us, in garrisons and on roads, and bound us with naught but trust. We kept our oaths. We built what we promised. We fought never but in a just cause. That is the whole of a True Man. I say it that ye may know it is not much. Nor is it little.

At dusk the column came in under the outer gate, and I stood on the gate and told it. Old men, and women with bundles, and children asleep on their feet, and soldiers of six broken companies that knew their officers no more. Two thousand and some hundreds. A month had we walked from the ashes of Glasspire to come to a castle. We came to a heap of stones.

The gate hung by one hinge. In the ward the grass stood to the knee. Where the songs had set a great hall, the roof was fallen in, and snow lay on the hearth where ye sit now.

Some shook. Some wept. Some fell, and would not rise.

Then began the crying, and it was not one cry. Some cried that we should send to the grey sails and beg terms, though no man living ever understood one word the grey sails spake. Some cried for the high passes, and the snow beyond them. Some cried that we should go down into the cellars, and bar the doors, and bide, as if the sea would forget us.

Men had I heard break ere that day. Never had I heard a people break. It is a sound like ice going out on a river.

The Captain looked for a general, and the generals were slain. He looked for the lords, and the lords were scattered. He looked to the Stonewrights, and they sat in the grass with their chisels in their laps.

He would not lead. All the long road he had waited for some man whose place it was, and none came, for they were dead. Mistake me not. It was not that the greater men were gone. There was no greater man. I think not that his equal ever lived.

The True Men looked to their Captain. The Captain looked at the wall.

Then he clomb it. As he came through the hall he had taken up a coal from the dead hearth, and I wist not why. Alone he went up the broken face of the inner wall, by the stones that stood proud of the mortar. We stood at the foot and let him, for he had not bidden us follow. I have told those stones since. Thirty-one there are, and four of them move.

Down off the ridge came the wind, with the whole of the winter in its mouth. At the top, where the wall yet stood whole, he took off his cloak. A plain soldier's cloak it was, crimson once, and gone to the colour of old brick. In that wind it was all he had. No man in the ward spake. Among the women a child coughed, and his mother hushed him. After that there was naught but the wind.

He tare it down the back.

A small sound it made, the sound of old cloth. Over the wind I heard it.

The one half he spread on the capstones, and kneeled on it with the coal, and wrote. Slowly he wrote, as a man writeth that hath not written much. That half he bound to a pike out of the rubble. The other half, whereon naught was written, he put back on his shoulders.

First up after him was I, and first to read it. Not once had his hand shaken. I swear to that. I turned to the ward and read it out, and my voice was not steady. I will not feign it was.

**IN MEMORY OF OUR HOME, OUR FAMILIES, OUR GOD, OUR FREEDOMS, OUR PEACE.**

He set the pike in a cleft of the wall and gave it to the wind, and the wind took it. That was the last unbroken wall of the Shorelands. That was the banner over it.

Now hear who answered, and in what order, for I told them. A count is no count till it be named.

First the True Men answered, each calling his name up the wall. Stannard of the ridge. Corlen of the harbour. Tamm of the three spans. Marl the fen-born. Aske of the canopy. Garvel of the smithies. Harl of the burning shore. Ulden of the galleries. Hesk of the mere. Lanner of the crags. Pellow of the spire. And Kael, of no haven, for he found me on a road. Twelve. I counted us twice, and it took not long.

After us answered the soldiers of the six companies. They are our troops now, a troop to each True Man, and they trust us as we trust him. Then answered the people of the column, that bare their children on their backs through the passes. One voice, and a dozen, and then hundreds, till I could tell them no more, and I stopped. It is the only time I ever stopped.

No man bade them. He gave no command. He had but written down what we had left, and it was enough.

Therefore is our pennant crimson, on this keep and on every keep we raise.

Look to the end of the bench, ye children at the front. He sitteth there now, with his hood up. On his back is the other half.

He will tell you it was not like that. It was like that.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*Then the True Men sang, as they sing it still:*
