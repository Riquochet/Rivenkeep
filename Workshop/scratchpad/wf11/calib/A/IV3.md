### IV.3 · The Warden's Door

*The third winter, in the Tide of Remembering.*

*The words of Brenn of the outpost, as {{Wendhessa}} of the guild at Eldhythe took them from his mouth in his old age. {{Halyna}} read them at this hearth. The annal after them is mine.*

"We two hold the stone,"

"but the words are not ours."

Nineteen I was. Twelve were we at that post, and the warden. It was the last post of the land, where the rock runneth out toward the grey. I was its runner.

Black wood had the warden set everywhere: in the doorposts, in his table, in the frame of his bed. ▒▒▒▒ was a rich man's son, and scarce older than his own runner. He held the post by his father's name, and not by his own. For his temper they had sent him to the edge, and at the edge there were but the twelve of us for it to wound. He had never known battle. He had never known patience.

In the morning the stranger came, in a small grey boat, out of the grey. There was no wind, and the boat came anyway. He beached it, and walked up to the warden's door. Tall he was, and pale, and clad in a mist-cloth that moved when he moved not. No weapon saw I on him.

First he spake in his own tongue, hard sounds, like a storm-wind in old trees. It affrighted us. We wist not that it is but how they speak. Then said he three words of ours. Carefully he said them, as a man setteth down a thing that might break.

"Stop… Sky… Dying…"

They were the right words, and truly spoken. I know it now. But words without a sentence are but sounds, and sounds were all we heard.

Then kneeled he on the warden's step, and set before him a dark stone, the bigness of two fists. He set it down as ye would set down bread before a guest. Behind him a gull came down on the shingle.

▒▒▒▒ kicked it aside.

Into the dust by the wall it went. Then laughed his men. I laughed. I have not laughed at aught since.

The stranger rose. His right hand he laid on the back of the warden's neck, gently, as my mother did when I had a fever. He drew their heads together till the foreheads touched, and held them so, and was silent. ▒▒▒▒ stood and let it be done. Had it stopped there, I think all our lives had gone otherwise.

Then took the stranger a little blade in his left hand, and touched it to the warden's right arm.

▒▒▒▒ tore himself away. Never had he had a wound. In the tearing, the cut that should have been shallow went deep, and the blood came. He screamed at us, "Seize him."

They seized him. He fought them not. Between their hands he went down on his knees again, on the step where he had kneeled with his gift. I understood it not.

"Kill him," screamed ▒▒▒▒.

They took up what was there: staves, and stones of the shingle, and the boat-poles. One of them took up my pole, the pole I pushed the boats off with.

He lifted not his hand. If he cried out, it was in his own tongue, and I took it for the wind. The blows I counted. I count them yet.

When he lay still, ▒▒▒▒ had not left off screaming, and some went on. Both his arms they brake: the one that had held the warden's neck so gently, and the one that had held the blade.

We obeyed.

I say we, for the post was one thing that day, and I was of it. No hand laid I on him. I did not stop them. A boy was I among the men.

Then was it quiet, and the gull was gone.

They drew him by the heels down the shingle, and his head went on every stone. Into his boat they cast him, as a fisher casteth a torn net into the bilge.

▒▒▒▒ held his arm. "Burn it," said he, "and shove it off, and give it the tide. Leave naught."

They set the boat alight. Higher than any fire it went up, as though the wood itself screamed. With the same poles they shoved it off, and the tide took it. Old Tobe went into the sea to his knees and washed the poles. The rest of us stood on the shingle, and would not look, and looked.

Well out, the boat turned. Against the tide it went, into the grey, burning still.

That same day ▒▒▒▒ had the clerk write to the Council. I stood by. I can say it by heart:

*Unknown intruder. Hostile intent. Neutralized. Vessel burned and released.*

No word of the gift. No word of the kneeling.

On the morrow, ere any man woke, I picked the stone out of the dust. Why I did it, I know not. ▒▒▒▒ gave me the letter at the door. So the gift and the lie went into one pack, and I ran it.

A day and a night I ran the coast road. Every mile I said over the oath I sware at fifteen, mine hand flat on the runners' stone: "What I saw, I carry. I carry nothing else."

In the Council hall at Eldhythe an old clerk read the letter. There was ink on his fingers. He looked up at me. "Is it so?"

I said not that it was so. I said, "It is written." Forty years have I weighed that small cleverness.

I carried the lie a day and a night. I carried the truth forty years, and gave it to no one.

That autumn three axemen sware to a burning boat out of the grey, that brake their boats under them. The shore laughed. I laughed not. The Council had it in writing.

▒▒▒▒ wore long sleeves all the rest of his life, through the hot summers of Eldhythe, and I know why. Last winter he died in his bed of black wood. I am the last of the twelve.

Forty years the stone hath lain at the bottom of my pack. Not once have I held it. I give it up now with these words, and I ask God to forgive me, for there is none left to ask but God.

So far the words of Brenn. At every blot {{Halyna}} stopped, and ye heard a silence in the place of the name.

Hold the stone, Seren.

The warden bare a mark on his arm: the half of something, cut off in the middle.

Brenn died within the year, as a man setteth down a load. The guild buried him under his name. The warden's name it cut away.

{{Wendhessa}} sealed his words in the chest of the mute stones, and told no one. When the havens fell, they bare it out of Eldhythe, his hand on the one handle and hers on the other. On the Road their arms failed. The masons said it held naught of worth, and gave it to the youngest, Tarnel and me, a handle each. Tarnel knotted his net with his free hand.

On the night of the pass {{Wendhessa}} lay down under one cloak. In the morning there was frost on the chest, on both its handles, and neither rose.

Tarnel did not wake.

There I stopped tonight, with my hands on the stone. Then {{Halyna}} laid their hands by mine.

"She carried it every step,"

"and after the passes she carried it alone."

Hale the runner sat with bowed head, and Wick at his feet.

There is no older law among any people than this: that one who kneeleth at your door is under your roof. We were a people of doors.

*Then the three of us said it together:*

This we lay as it was laid for us.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

*Never before had this hearth said it over a killing by our own. Old Ebba began it, and the rest were slow to follow. But they followed.*

*And the Captain put his hood back.*

*In the morning the runners sware their oath again, and added four words: Not as Brenn carried.*
