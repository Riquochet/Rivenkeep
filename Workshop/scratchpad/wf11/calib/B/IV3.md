### IV.3 · The Warden's Door

*The third winter, in the Tide of Remembering.*

*The words of Brenn of the outpost, as {{Wendhessa}} took them from his mouth at Eldhythe in his old age. {{Halyna}} read them at the hearth by turns. The annal after them is mine.*

"We two hold the stone,"

"and the words are not ours."

Nineteen was I. Twelve were we at that post, and the warden.

It lay at the end of the land, toward the grey. I was its runner, sworn at fifteen with my hand flat on the runners' stone: "What I saw, I carry. I carry nothing else."

One morning at summer's end, out of the grey came a small grey boat. There was no wind, and the boat came anyway.

He that was in it beached it and walked up to the warden's door.

Black wood was in the posts of that door, and in the warden's table, and in the frame of his bed. A rich man's son he was, hardly older than his runner, and he held the post by his father's name. For his temper they had sent him to the edge, where there were only the twelve of us for it to wound. He had never known battle. He had never known patience.

The stranger was tall and pale, and clad in a mist-cloth that moved when he did not. No weapon saw I on him.

Loud was his own tongue, and it affrighted us. We knew not that it is only how they speak. Then he said three words of ours, carefully, like a man setting down something that might break.

"Stop… Sky… Dying…"

They were the right words, and truly spoken. I know it now. But words without sentences are only sounds, and we heard only sounds.

Then he knelt on the step and set before him a dark stone, the size of two fists, the way you would set down bread before a guest. No man of us moved. Behind him a gull came down and walked among the stones.

▒▒▒▒ kicked it aside.

It went into the dust by the wall. And his men laughed. I laughed. I have not laughed at aught since.

The stranger rose. His right hand he laid on the back of the warden's neck, gently, the way my mother did when I had a fever. He drew their heads together till the foreheads touched, and held them so, in silence. ▒▒▒▒ stood, and let it be done. Had it stopped there, I think, all our lives had gone otherwise.

Then the stranger took a little blade in his left hand, and touched it to the warden's right arm.

▒▒▒▒ tore himself away. He had never had a wound. In the tearing, the cut that was meant to be shallow went deep, and the blood came.

"Seize him," he screamed.

The men seized him. He fought them not. Between their hands he went down on his knees again, on the step where he had knelt with his gift. I understood it not.

"Kill him."

They took up what lay to hand: the boat-poles, the staves, the stones of the shingle. One of them took up my pole, the pole I pushed the boats off with.

He lifted not his hand. If he cried out, it was in his own tongue, and I took it for the wind. I counted the blows. I count them yet. When he lay still, ▒▒▒▒ was screaming yet, and some went on, for ▒▒▒▒ was screaming yet. Both his arms they broke: the one that had held the warden's neck so gently, and the one that had held the blade.

We obeyed.

I say *we*, for the post was one thing that day, and I was of it. I did not stop them. A boy was I among the men.

Then it was quiet, and the gull was gone. By the heels they drew him down the shingle, and his head went on every stone. Into his own boat they threw him, as a fisher throws a torn net into the bilge.

▒▒▒▒ held his arm, and looked not at the boat.

"Burn it," he said. "Burn it, and shove it off, and give it the tide. Let there be naught to show."

They set it alight, and it burned not as wood burns. There was a screaming in it. The stranger was dead. It was no man that screamed. It was the wood itself.

With the poles they shoved it off. Old Tobe went down into the sea to his knees and washed the poles, one and then another. The rest of us stood on the shingle, and would not look, and looked.

Well out, the boat turned. Into the wind it went, against the current, which no boat can do, and into the grey, burning.

That day ▒▒▒▒ bade the clerk write to the Council. I stood by. I can say it by heart:

*Unknown intruder. Hostile intent. Neutralized. Vessel burned and released.*

No word of the gift. No word of the kneeling.

Next morning, ere any man woke, I picked the stone out of the dust. Why I did it, I know not. ▒▒▒▒ gave me the letter at his door. So the gift and the lie went into one pack, and I ran it.

A day and a night I ran the coast road to Eldhythe, and every mile I said the oath over. What I saw, I carry. I carry nothing else.

The Council's clerk, an old man with ink on his fingers, read the letter, and looked up at me.

"Is it so?"

I did not say it was so. I said, "It is written." Forty years have I weighed that small cleverness.

I carried the lie in a day and a night. I carried the truth forty years, and gave it to no one.

That autumn the shore laughed at three axemen, home on a spar, who swore a burning boat came at them out of the grey. I laughed not, for I had seen it turn. Naught said I. The Council had it in writing.

▒▒▒▒ wore long sleeves the rest of his life, through the hot summers of Eldhythe, and I know why. Last winter he died in his bed of black wood. I am the last of the twelve.

Forty years the stone has lain at the bottom of my pack, and not once have I held it. I give it up now with these words. I ask God to forgive me, for there is none left to ask but God.

So far the words of Brenn.

At every blot {{Halyna}} were silent. On the warden's arm was one mark: the half of something, cut off in the middle. Within the year Brenn died, as a man sets down a load.

{{Wendhessa}} sealed his words about the stone and told none. At the Fall they bore the chest out of the burning, his hand on one handle and hers on the other. On the Road their arms failed, and it passed to the two youngest, Tarnel and me. Tarnel knotted his net with his free hand.

On the night of the pass {{Wendhessa}} lay down under one cloak. At morning there was frost on the chest, on both its handles. Neither rose.

Tarnel did not wake.

There I stopped. {{Halyna}} laid their hands on the stone beside mine.

"She carried it every step,"

"and after the passes she carried it alone."

There is no older law among any people than this: that one who kneels at your door is under your roof. We were a people of doors.

This we lay as it was laid for us.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

*Never before had this hearth said it over a killing by our own. Old Ebba began it. The rest were slow, but they followed.*

*And the Captain put his hood back.*

*In the morning the runners swore their oath again, and added four words: Not as Brenn carried.*
