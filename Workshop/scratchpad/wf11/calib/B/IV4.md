### IV.4 · The Gift Held an Hour

*The third spring, in the Tide of Remembering.*

*Known from the gift itself, which Rhyna held an hour at the hearth. Halvard was across the ward at the mortar and never touched it, yet it was he who began the telling. The end of it is of me. I have let it stand as the wood gave it.*

*— Old. Older than — no. Dark, and old.*  
*— A tree, once. We two have it: a tree, when all was green.*  
*— Listening. Long. Words, one and one and one.*  
*— A door. Kin in the posts. Sawn. Dead. They do not answer.*  
*— Knees on stone. Set down. His best.*  
*— A foot.*  
*— Laughter, through the dust. A hand on a neck, so soft —*

This is held in the grain.

Eldest wood are we. A tree we were when the world was green to its edges. We fell, and the ages lay down on us, leaf on leaf, till we grew dark and hard. You took us for stone. We are not stone.

Wood that time has forgotten does not itself forget. Slowly it gives, and only what is true. Even a sliver of such wood is the dearest gift a child of the long-lived can give. We were given once.

He that gave us took the name Aelrhen when he took his errand. It means bond-to-stone. A long life he listened across the breath, to the shore-men at their nets and their walls. Your words he gathered as a child gathers fallen leaves, one and one and one.

At the end he had three, and he knew them for the right ones. But your tongue joins its words with small words, and small words make no sound across water. Those he never heard.

Us he had, though. Whoso held us while a tree's shadow moves the breadth of its own trunk would know the whole of it. We were the sentence.

He chose the outermost of your holds, for its doors were of our wood, and its walls, and its benches. He took that for reverence. We knew it for what it was: our kin from the edge, sawn and polished. At the door he laid his hand on the post and greeted them. They answered not. They were dead.

He knelt on the step before the master of that house and set us down. One who comes under a roof brings his best, and we were his best. In the thunder of his own tongue he named the rite, *Ael'thar*. Then he said his three words in yours.

A foot struck us into the dust by the wall. The men of that house laughed. We heard it through the dust.

He stopped not. A rite once begun is not broken off. He rose and took the back of the host's neck in his right hand, there where life goes up into thought. A fledgling could have slept in that hand. He drew their heads together. The foreheads met, thought to thought, and he was silent.

With his left hand he set the blade to the host's arm.

And the host drew back. We know not why. Fear leaves no mark on wood.

The blade that should have gone a little way went deep. The host cried out, and the men of that house came.

His blood ran across the step to where we lay, and with it came his last hour. So we know it as he knew it.

He went down on his knees again, for there the rite begins, and he would begin it again. He knew the blows as a tree knows the iron: one, and another. He lifted not his hand. The cut he would have made on his own arm was never made. Never were the two arms laid together, shoulder to shoulder, that harm to one might be harm to both.

He died there with the rite half made.

We lay in the dust and heard all of it, and no hand took us up. Of all things that ever lay at a shore-man's feet and looked like stone, we were the one that could have spoken to him.

And we were the one his foot found.

In the morning a hand took us out of the dust, and put us away, and held us not.

Then a jolting in the dark, a day and a night. Then dark, half a life, and never a hand. Then leaves about us, and cut stones cold beside us, and another dark as long. Then old arms, two pairs, slow; and fire, near and far. Then small arms, two pairs, going up, in the cold.

Then one pair, a long way.

None held us long, till you held us while a tree's shadow moves the breadth of its own trunk. Here is the sentence, as it was brought to the door:

*Stop the felling of the pillars at the edge of the grey, for they are not timber. The sky over us is their breath, and ours, and when you cut them it comes open, and our children cannot live in the open sun. We are here, behind the grey, and we have been here always, and we are not your enemies. Stop, for the sky is dying, and we are dying under it.*

That is all he came to say. We have carried it a long way, and the grain holds it still.

*And no one answered.*
