### I.1 · The Torn Cloak

*The first winter, the night after the Rite.*

*Laid by Kael the Counter, the first night this hearth asked for a tale. He forbade me to change one word of it. A good many I changed; and when I read it him, he swore it was word for word as he had told it.*

Give me the stone. No, I will stand. Enough have I sat this winter.

I swear on the lintel of the western stair, where the old wardens cut their names, that I will tell this plain. No singer am I. I stood on the gate that day and told the column in, head by head; and what I told not, I will not tell you.

At dusk it came in under the outer gate. I heard it ere I saw it: many feet, and no voice among them. Old men. Women with bundles. Children asleep on their feet. Soldiers of six broken companies, that knew their officers no more. Last of all came the Captain, for he had kept the rear the whole road, and we twelve with him, his True Men. Two thousand and some hundreds, all told.

A month we had walked from the ashes of Glasspire to come to a castle. We came to a heap of stones. The outer gate hung by one hinge, and the ward behind it was grass to the knee. Where the songs had set a great hall, the roof was fallen in, and snow lay on the hearth where you sit now.

Some shook. Some wept. Some fell and would not rise.

Then the crying began, and it was not one cry. Some cried that we should send to the grey sails and beg for terms, though no man living had understood one word the grey sails said. Some cried for the high passes, and whatsoever lay beyond the snow. Some cried that we should go down into the cellars, and bar the doors, and bide there, as if the sea would forget us.

Men I had heard break, on the long retreat. Never till that dusk a people. It is a sound like ice going out on a river.

Down off the ridge came the wind, with the whole of the winter in its mouth.

The people looked for a general, and the generals were slain. They looked for the lords, and the lords were scattered. They looked to the Stonewrights, and the Stonewrights sat in the grass with their chisels in their laps.

All the long retreat the Captain had waited for some man whose place it was to lead, and none came. Mistake me not. It was not that the greater men were gone. There was no greater man. His equal, I think, never lived. But he wanted it not. I swear to that.

The True Men looked to their Captain. The Captain looked at the wall.

Then he climbed it. In the hall he had taken a coal from the dead hearth, from this hearth, and why, none of us knew. Alone he went up the broken face of the inner wall, by the stones that stood proud of the mortar. We stood at the foot and let him, for he had not bidden us follow. I have counted those stones since. Thirty-one there are, and four of them move.

At the top, where the wall yet stood whole, he took off his cloak. A plain soldier's cloak it was, crimson once, and gone to the colour of old brick. Naught else had he against that wind.

None in the ward spoke. A child coughed, and his mother hushed him. After that there was naught but the wind.

He tore it down the back.

A small sound it made, the sound of old cloth giving. Over the wind I heard it. So did every soul in the ward.

The one half he spread on the capstones. He knelt on it with the coal and wrote, slowly, as a man writes who has not written much. When it was written he bound that half to a pike out of the rubble. The other half, that bore no word, he put back on his shoulders.

I was first up after him, and first to read it. Not once had his hand shaken. I turned to the ward and read it out, and my voice was not steady, and I will not pretend it was:

**IN MEMORY OF OUR HOME, OUR FAMILIES, OUR GOD, OUR FREEDOMS, OUR PEACE.**

He set the pike in a crack of the wall and gave it to the wind. That was the last unbroken wall of the Shorelands, and that was the banner above it.

Hear now who answered, and in what order, for I counted. A count is no count till it be named.

First answered the True Men, each calling his name up the wall. Stannard of the ridge. Corlen of the harbour. Tamm of the three spans. Marl the fen-born. Aske of the canopy. Garvel of the smithies. Harl of the burning shore. Ulden of the galleries. Hesk of the mere. Lanner of the crags. Pellow of the spire. And Kael the Counter, of no haven, for the Captain found me on a road.

Twelve. I counted us twice, and it did not take long.

We kept our oaths, and built what we promised, and fought only where the cause was just. That is the whole of a True Man. I say it so you will know it is not much. Nor is it little.

After us answered the six companies, that are our troops now, a troop to each True Man. Then the people of the column, that had borne their children through the passes on their backs. One voice. A dozen. Then hundreds, till I could count no longer.

I stopped. It is the only time I ever stopped.

By no command did they answer, for he gave none. He had but written down what we had left, and it was enough.

Crimson is the pennant on every keep of ours. It is the colour of that cloak.

Look to the end of the bench, you children at the front. There he sits, with his hood up.

On his back is the other half.

He will tell you it was not like that. It was like that.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*Then the True Men sang, as they sing it still:*
