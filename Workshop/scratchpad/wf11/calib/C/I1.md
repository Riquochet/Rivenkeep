### I.1 · The Torn Cloak

*The first winter, the night after the Rite.*

*Laid by Kael the Counter, the first night this hearth asked for a tale. He forbade me to change one word of it. I changed a good many; and when I read it him, he swore it was word for word as he had told it.*

Give me the stone. No, I will stand. Enough have I sat this winter.

By the lintel of the western stair I swear it, where the old wardens cut their names: as I counted it, so will I tell it. *Ston.*

A teller am I, and no singer. Heads I tell, and stones, and days. What I told not, I will not tell you.

At dusk the *trenn* came in under the outer gate, and I counted it as it came. Old men, and women with bundles, and children asleep on their feet, and soldiers of six broken companies that knew their officers no more: two thousand and some hundreds. A month had we walked from the ashes of Glasspire to come to a castle, and we came to a heap of stones. On one hinge hung the gate. The ward behind it was grass to the knee. Where the songs had set a great hall, the roof was fallen in, and snow lay on the hearth where you sit now.

Some shook. Some wept. Some fell and would not rise.

Then began the crying, and not one cry was it, but many. Some cried that we should send to the grey sails and beg for terms, albeit no man living had understood one word the grey sails said. Some cried for the high passes, and whatever lay beyond the snow. Some cried that we should go down into the cellars, and bar the doors, and bide, as if the sea would forget us.

Men had I heard break ere then. Never had I heard a people break. It is a sound like ice going out on a river.

All the long retreat the Captain had waited for some man whose place it was to lead. None came. Now, in the ward, he looked about him for one. He looked for a general, and the generals were slain. He looked for the lords, and the lords were scattered. He looked to the Stonewrights, and they sat in the grass with their chisels in their laps.

Mistake me not. It was not that the greater men were gone. There was no greater man. His equal, I ween, never lived.

Down off the riven ridge came the wind, with the whole of the winter in its mouth. The True Men looked to their Captain. The Captain looked at the wall.

Then he climbed it. A coal had he taken from the dead hearth as he came through the hall, from this hearth; and why, I knew not. Up the broken face he went alone, from *trenn* to *trenn*, by the stones that stood proud of the mortar. We stood at the foot and let him, for he had not bidden us follow. Thirty-one those stones are, and four of them move. I have counted them since.

At the top he took off his cloak. Crimson it was once, ere the road wore it to brick. In that wind it was all he had. None in the ward spoke now. Among the women a child coughed, and his mother hushed him, and after that was there naught but the wind.

He tore it down the back.

A small sound it made, the sound of old cloth. Over the wind I heard it.

The one half he spread on the capstones, and knelt on it with the coal, and wrote. Slowly he wrote, as a man writes who has not written much. When it was written he bound that half to a pike out of the rubble. The other half, that had naught written on it, he put back on his shoulders.

First up after him was I, and first to read it. Not once had his hand shaken. I swear to that. I read it out to the ward, and my voice was not steady, and I will not feign it was:

**IN MEMORY OF OUR HOME, OUR FAMILIES, OUR GOD, OUR FREEDOMS, OUR PEACE.**

*Ston*, said I after it. None bade me.

He set the pike in a crack of the wall and gave it to the wind. That was the last unbroken wall of the Shorelands, and that was the banner above it.

Now hear who answered, and in what order, for I counted. A count is no count till it is named.

First the True Men. One by one he had found us, in garrisons and on roads, and bound us by naught but trust. We kept our oaths. We built what we promised. We fought only where the cause was just. That is the whole of a True Man. I say it so you will know it is not much. Nor is it little.

Stannard of the ridge. Corlen of the harbour. Tamm of the three spans. Marl the fen-born. Aske of the canopy. Garvel of the smithies. Harl of the burning shore. Ulden of the galleries. Hesk of the mere. Lanner of the crags. Pellow of the spire. And Kael the Counter, that is me, of no haven, for the Captain found me on a road.

Twelve. I counted us twice, and it did not take long.

After us the six companies, that walked the long retreat beside us. They are our troops now, a troop to each True Man, and they trust us as we trust him. Then the *trenn* itself, that had carried its children on its back through the passes: one voice, and a dozen, and then hundreds, till I could count no more, and I stopped. It is the only time I ever stopped.

By no command was it, for he gave none. He had only written down what we had left, and it was enough.

Therefore is the pennant crimson on every keep of ours. It is the colour of that cloak.

Look to the end of the bench, you children at the front. There sits he now, with his hood up.

On his back is the other half.

He will tell you it was not like that. It was like that.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*Then the True Men sang, as they sing it still:*

> *He wrote five things a man might die for,*  
> *and none of us has needed more.*
