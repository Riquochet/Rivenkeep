### IV.3 · The Warden's Door

*The third winter, in the Tide of Remembering.*

*The words of Brenn of the outpost, as {{Wendhessa}} of the guild at Eldhythe took them from his mouth in his old age. {{Halyna}} read them at the hearth. The annal after them is mine.*

"We hold the stone, but these words are not ours."

"We two but read them, as they were written."

Nineteen I was. Twelve were we at that post at the end of the land, and the warden.

Black wood had ▒▒▒▒ set everywhere: in the doorposts, in his table, in the frame of his bed. A rich man's son was he, hardly older than I, and he held the post by his father's name. For his temper they had sent him to the edge, where there were but the twelve of us to wound. Never had he known battle. Never had he known patience.

Out of the *myst* came the stranger one summer morning, in a small grey boat. There was no wind, and the boat came anyway.

Tall he was, and pale, in a mist-cloth that moved when he did not. No weapon had he.

His own tongue he spoke first, like a storm-wind in old trees, and it frighted us sore. We knew not that it is only how they speak. Then three words of ours he said, carefully, like a man setting down something that might break.

"Stop… Sky… Dying…"

They were the right words, spoken truly. I know it now. But words without sentences are only sounds, and we heard only sounds.

Then he knelt on the warden's step, and set before him a dark stone the size of two fists. He set it down the way you would set down bread before a guest. No man of us moved. Behind him a gull came down, and walked about among the stones.

▒▒▒▒ kicked it aside.

Into the dust by the wall it went. Then laughed his men. I laughed. I have laughed at naught since.

The stranger rose. His right hand he laid on the back of the warden's neck, gently, the way my mother did when I had a fever. He drew their heads together till the foreheads touched, and held them so, and was silent. And ▒▒▒▒ let it be done. Had it stopped there, I think, all our lives had gone otherwise.

Then he took a little blade in his left hand, and touched it to the warden's right arm.

▒▒▒▒ tore himself away. Never had he had a wound. In the tearing, the cut that was meant to be shallow went deep, and the blood came. He screamed at us, "Seize him."

They seized him. He fought not. Another cut he meant to make, I think now, on his own arm. He never made it. Between their hands he went down on his knees again, where he had knelt with his gift. I understood it not.

"Kill him," screamed ▒▒▒▒.

They took up what was there, poles and staves and stones. One of them took up my pole, the pole I pushed the boats off with.

He lifted not his hand. If he cried out, it was in his own tongue, and I took it for the wind. The blows I counted. I count them yet. When he was still, ▒▒▒▒ was still screaming, and some went on. Both his arms they broke: the one that had held the warden's neck so gently, and the one that had held the blade.

His men obeyed.

We obeyed. I say we, for the post was one thing that day, and I was of it. I did not stop them. A boy was I among the men.

Then was it quiet, and the gull was gone.

By the heels they drew him down the shingle, and his head went on every stone. Into his boat they threw him, as a fisher throws a torn net into the bilge. ▒▒▒▒ stood holding his arm.

"Burn it," he said, "and shove it off, and give it the tide. Leave naught to show."

They set the boat alight, and it burned not as wood burns. There was a screaming in the fire. The stranger was dead. It was no man that screamed. It was the wood.

With the same poles they shoved it off. Old Tobe went into the sea to his knees and washed the poles, one and then another. The rest of us stood on the shingle, and would not look, and looked.

Well out, the boat turned. There was no wind. Against the tide it turned, and into the *myst* it went, still burning.

That day ▒▒▒▒ had the clerk write to the Council. By heart I can say it:

*Unknown intruder. Hostile intent. Neutralized. Vessel burned and released.*

No word of the gift. No word of the kneeling.

Ere any man woke, I picked the stone out of the dust. Why I did it, I know not. At the door ▒▒▒▒ gave me the letter, for I was the post's *haskard*. So the gift and the lie went into one pack, and I ran it.

A day and a night I ran, and every mile I said over the oath I swore at fifteen, my hand flat on the runners' stone:

"What I saw, I carry. I carry nothing else."

The clerk of the Council, an old man with ink on his fingers, read it, and looked up at me.

"Is it so?"

*Tumar* said I not. I said, "It is written." Forty years have I weighed that small cleverness.

The lie I carried a day and a night. The truth I carried forty years, and gave it to no one.

That autumn three axemen swore in the harbour-houses to a burning boat, and the shore laughed. I laughed not, for I had seen it turn, yet I said naught.

▒▒▒▒ wore long sleeves all the rest of his life, through the hot summers of Eldhythe, and I know why. Last winter he died in his bed of black wood. I am the last of the twelve.

Forty years has the stone lain in my pack, and not once have I held it. I give it up now with these words. I ask God to forgive me, for there is no one left to ask but God.

So far the words of Brenn.

The warden's name is not kept. At every blot tonight {{Halyna}} stopped, and you heard a silence in its place. On his right arm was the only mark the Kneeling Guest ever made on one of us: the half of something, cut off in the middle.

Within the year Brenn died, as a man sets down a load.

{{Wendhessa}} sealed the bundle among the mute stones, and told no one. When the havens fell they bore the chest out, his hand on the one handle and hers on the other. On the Road their arms failed. The masons said it held nothing of worth, and gave it to the two youngest: to Tarnel and to me. Tarnel knotted his net with his free hand as he walked. I watched the knots.

On the night of the pass {{Wendhessa}} lay down under one cloak. In the morning there was frost on the chest, on both its handles. Neither of them rose.

Tarnel did not wake.

I stopped there tonight. {{Halyna}} laid their hands on the stone beside mine.

"She carried it every step,"

"and after the passes she carried it alone."

There is no older law among any people than this: that whoso kneels at your door is under your roof. We were a people of doors.

*Then the three of us said it together:*

This we lay as it was laid for us.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

*Old Ebba began it. The rest were slow to follow her; but they followed.*

*And the Captain put his hood back.*

*In the morning the runners swore their oath again, and added four words to it: Not as Brenn carried.*
