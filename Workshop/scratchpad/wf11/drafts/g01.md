## OF THIS BOOK

*The third summer, in the Tide of Remembering.*

*By Seren Two-Inks, scribe of this Book, and the third of the hearth of {{Halyna}}.*

It is the short dark of the year. The hall sleepeth about me, and the Long Hearth is sunk to coals. On the wall I see the banner lift, in a wind I hear not.

The Book is half full. One of its tellers, that held up a wet finger to every wind, is under the east cairns already. Tonight have I turned back to the first leaf. I left it bare, for the lintel is not cut ere the posts are raised.

At this hearth the first builders of the Keep laid their tales. When the Keep was left, the tales went cold with the hearth. Now sit we where they sat, when naught is left to do with the hands but hold them to the fire.

Beside me in its niche is the stone, the old capstone of the inner gate. Face down it lay in the gateway, with its vow to the earth, till {{Halyna}} turned it at dusk on the day of the banner. It is a stone that two must turn. Its vow is sharp yet.

::: native legend_stonwryt_hal line
The Stonwryt of the first builders, on the tale-stone's underside, in their old hand, the Hal
*This wall will stand. We two laid it, and we two answer for it, while stone is stone. It stands.* It is signed under one lintel with the pair-name of {{Aldwena}}, who laid the capstone.
:::

So write I every bonded pair, under one stroke, as a lintel on two posts. It is one name, and it is the name of two.

{{Halyna}} are Halvard Stone-Warden and Rhyna of the Inner Gate. {{Idrenna}}, the eldest, taught them, and nod by the door when a tale is laid true. {{Orvenna}}, our wall-masters, never raise their voices. Of {{Enrella}}, the youngest, the one cannot begin a jest but the other will end it.

When a tale is done we say not that it was told, but *laid*. A tale that is but told is gone by morning. A tale that is laid is part of the wall.

Two words answer every tale of ours, and never other two: *We remember.*

The first night this hearth asked a tale, none of us wist what to say. Old Ebba of Fenholm spake them into the quiet. None bade her, and none bade us follow. But the hearth took the words from her. Most nights she is first yet, a breath before the rest, and in behind her we come, as a tide behind its first wave.

Forgetting came on our fathers, and none chose it. Remembering we choose, as masons choose which stones to keep.

Some tales are the wood's, most out of the wreck-wood the tides bring in. One came out of a dark stone, sealed in the chest at my feet till {{Idrenna}} gave leave to break the seal. The folk behind the grey we call the Mystaeri, a word of ours, not theirs.

The wood is not read but known, whole, as a memory is known. Words can be turned, and a knowing cannot. So {{Halyna}} give it up in pieces, the one taking up where the other's words fail.

I set the pieces down as they fell, in two inks. They would not have me mark whose is whose. Ye cannot tell. Out of the pieces I make the tale. After it the hearth is silent, and that silence I have written down.

{{Halyna}} would have the wood's leaf on the right, facing ours.

"A tale told from one side of a wall,"

"is half a tale."

Where the wood's leaf could not yet be told, I have left it bare, with only the grain showing. Whoso openeth this Book may see there the shape of what we understood not yet, and wait.

I am not of the Bonded, though {{Halyna}} took me into their hearth as its third. When they know the wood, I hold the lamp. Once I laid mine own palm on the grain, and let go. Mine is the hand to write, and not the hand to know.

Every scribe before me was one of a pair. I write alone.

The Captain will hear all, and tell naught. When the dead are named, he will put back his hood.

Where a carving was broken, I have not mended it with guesses. Where the guild cut out a name, I have set a blot. Where the wood speaketh, I have written its own names for things, and not ours.

Each night I set the tale in our tongue as masons set a course, every joint broken over the joint below. Each word I say aloud three times ere I write it, to hear whether it will hold. The tale is the teller's. The words are mine. I read each leaf to him that laid it, and no teller yet hath asked for his own back.

Most nights the coals are gone to ash, and the last word is yet in my mouth. So it is tonight.

I love our tongue.

Up the mountain I carried two things, our tongue and a chest; and only the chest was heavy.

This Book is ink. {{Halyna}} would have it cut in the granite of this hall, course on course, when peace is come. Till then it lieth in that chest, among the mute stones.

## BOOK ONE · THE BOOK OF THE WALL

*How we came to stand.*

### I.1 · The Torn Cloak

*The first winter, the night after the Rite.*

*Laid by Kael the Counter, on the first night this hearth asked a tale. He forbade me to change one word of it; I changed a good many, and when I read it him, he sware it was word for word as he had told it.*

Give me the stone. No, I will stand. Enough have I sat this winter.

By the lintel of the western stair I swear it, where the old wardens cut their names. A teller am I, and no singer. Heads I tell, and stones, and days. What I told not, that will I not tell.

At dusk the column came in under the outer gate. I heard it ere I saw it: many feet, and no voice among them. From the gate I told it, head by head.

Old men came, and women with bundles, and children asleep on their feet, and soldiers of six broken companies, that knew their officers no more. Last came the Captain, that had kept the rear the whole road. Two thousand and some hundreds, all told.

A month had we walked from the ashes of Glasspire to come to a castle. We came to a heap of stones. The gate hung by one hinge, and in the ward the grass stood to the knee. Where the songs had set a great hall, the roof was fallen in, and snow lay on the hearth where ye sit now.

Some shook. Some wept. Some fell, and would not rise.

Then began the crying, and it was not one cry. Some cried that we should beg terms of the grey sails, though no man living ever understood one word they spake. Some cried for the high passes, and the snow beyond them. Some cried that we should go down into the cellars, and bar the doors, and bide, as if the sea would forget us.

Men had I heard break, on the long retreat. Never till that dusk a people. It is a sound like ice going out on a river.

Down off the riven ridge came the wind, with the whole of the winter in its mouth.

The people looked for a general, and the generals were slain. They looked for the lords, and the lords were scattered. They looked to the Stonewrights, and the Stonewrights sat in the grass with their chisels in their laps.

To the Captain looked none of them. Captain was he of a small company, and we were his company. All the long road he had waited for one whose place it was to lead, and none came, for they were dead. Mistake me not: there was no greater man, and his equal, I think, never lived. But he sought not to lead. I swear to that.

The True Men looked to their Captain. The Captain looked at the wall.

Then he climbed it. In the hall he had taken a coal from the dead hearth, this hearth, and why, none of us wist. Alone he went up the broken face, by the stones that stood proud of the mortar. We stood at the foot and let him, for he had not bidden us follow. I have told those stones since: thirty-one there are, and four of them move.

At the top he took off his cloak. Crimson it was once, ere the road wore it to brick. In that wind it was all he had.

No man in the ward spake. Among the women a child coughed, and his mother hushed him. After that there was naught but the wind.

He tore it down the back.

A small sound it made, the sound of old cloth giving. Over the wind I heard it.

The one half he spread on the capstones, and kneeled on it with the coal, and wrote. Slowly he wrote, as men write that have written little. That half he bound to a pike out of the rubble. The other half, with naught written on it, he put back on his shoulders.

First up after him was I, and first to read it. Not once had his hand shaken. I turned to the ward and read it out, and my voice was not steady, and I will not feign it was.

Now hear who answered, and in what order, for I told them. A count is no count till it be named.

First the True Men. One by one he had found us, in garrisons and on roads, and bound us with naught but trust. We kept our oaths, and built what we promised, and fought never but in a just cause. That is the whole of a True Man. I say it that ye may know it is not much. Nor is it little.

Stannard of the ridge. Corlen of the harbour. Tamm of the three spans. Marl the fen-born. Aske of the canopy. Garvel of the smithies. Harl of the burning shore. Ulden of the galleries. Hesk of the mere. Lanner of the crags. Pellow of the spire. And Kael the Counter, their oath-keeper, of no haven, for the Captain found me on a road.

Twelve. I counted us twice, and it took not long.

After us answered the six companies, a troop to each of us, and they trust us as we trust him. Then answered the people of the column, that bare their children on their backs through the passes. One voice. A dozen. Then hundreds, till I could tell them no more.

I stopped. It is the only time I ever stopped.

He gave no command. He had but written down what we had left, and it was enough.

Crimson is the pennant on this Keep, and on every keep we raise. It is the colour of that cloak.

Look to the end of the bench, ye children at the front. There he sitteth, with his hood up.

On his back is the other half.

He will tell you it was not like that. It was like that.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

  - the vow sits next to the drawing that shows it;

  Every other part of the re-ordering is applied.
