## BOOK FOUR · THE BOOK OF THE WOUND

*The wrong, told from both sides of the grey.*

### IV.1 · Of the Felling of the Black Pillars

*The second spring, in the Second Tide.*

*Laid by Seren Two-Inks, out of the Harvest rolls of the guild. {{Halyna}} read them me by night, and I copied after them so fast that my letters leaned like reeds in a wind.*

Hold the stone, Seren.

A year had we read, when on a night of thaw we came to the bottom of the archive chest. The cord on the oldest roll was rotten, and broke in Rhyna's fingers. No line of it would {{Halyna}} read me till I had written this:

"We two lived it not,"

"but our fathers' fathers lived it. They wrote it down, and never read what they had written."

In the oldest roll the grey is but a blackish veil on the rim of the sea. After that it is a question, asked in the night-watches and never answered: what it was, and who breathed it. The answer, when it came, was not written in our hand.

Then came the high summer of the havens' pride. Farther than their fathers had dared went the ships, and at the hem of the grey they found the black pillars in their ranks, straight as a plumb-line. The ship-masters named them the Mystholders, for they seemed to hold the grey up as posts hold a roof. Harder than oak was their timber, and lighter than iron, and proof against rot.

Two generations the Trade Council sent out the axes. The rolls keep the count, cargo by cargo. Hear it now, and hold it, for it is ours.

Black wood, for buttresses and bridges. Black wood, for the three spans at Tidesmeet, and the pilings of Fenholm. Black wood, for the roof-trees of Holtward, and the cranes of Highreach. Black wood, for the galleries of Sandreach, and the hall on the ice at Rimewatch. Black wood, burned slow in the forges of Emberhythe. And at Carnhold the axes were beaten out, and went back to the edge for more.

Then the things that needed naught of the kind.

Black wood, to floor the dancing-hall of a lord of Eldhythe.

Black wood, for the trade-tables of the harbour-houses, and their dice-boards.

*[Here the roll is stained, and three lines are lost.]*

Last of all, a merchant of Tidesmeet had one pillar hewn into a single bed.

He slept in it, and was proud.

Halvard turned the leaf. Neither of them spake.

Beside that count the rolls keep a stranger one. Where a pillar was felled, the stump put up new growth, straight and black. Back went the axemen to cut it, for it made good poles, and the guild bought them for scaffold.

They cut it, and it grew. They cut it, and it grew. They cut it.

Below a count of poles Rhyna's finger stopped. A clerk had written one line more, so small that twice I bent the lamp to it ere she could read it.

*All along the edge, the stumps put up nothing.*

No man wrote why, and no man asked. Whose hand it was, the roll saith not. I would have kept his name.

Season by season the grey thinned, like a dying enchantment, and the Council named it fortune. I tried that word under my tongue, and it gave, like mortar gone to sand.

The Stonewrights warned the Council. It is in our own hand, on a leaf that no thumb had darkened:

*"These trees grow in strange soil. The carvings we find beside them bear a tongue we cannot read. Where wisdom is absent, let restraint be our guide."*

But trade sat in the high seat by then, and our counsel was a thing grandmothers said by the fire. Once was the warning read aloud, in the Council hall at Eldhythe. Then was it laid on a shelf.

For there were carvings. At the feet of the pillars the axemen found stones set upright, cut with curling letters, as beautiful as frost on a window. No man could read them.

Some hung in the harbour-houses, reduced to ornament. Men drank under them, and did their reckoning. The rest the guild locked in its archives, and its own warning with them.

Some of those lie in the chest at my knee, that I carried up the mountain. {{Halyna}} have laid their four hands on them, and been given naught. Stone does not translate.

Among the counts of one late summer Halvard came on a letter from the outermost post. A clerk had copied it between a count of poles and a count of oars, with the same care. Halvard read it as he had read the oars.

*Unknown intruder. Hostile intent. Neutralized. Vessel burned and released.*

At its foot a name is cut out: ▒▒▒▒. Rhyna laid her finger on the blot, and took it away.

On the next leaf two timber-boats are struck off a cargo-list, and beside them, in the same hand: *lost at the edge.*

I copied it as the clerk had, between the poles and the oars.

What I held, I knew not.

The Harvest ended not by wisdom, but by the fickleness of men. The near pillars grew scarce, and cheaper timber came down out of the northern hills. After the year of the burning boat no crew would row within sight of the grey, and the rolls say not why. So the axes stopped. No one decided it.

Then the havens forgat. Over the ale and the dice the stones hung on, and children asked what they said, and were told naught. Some of you that sit at this fire were those children.

Late, very late, the wheel of pride turned. The temples were mended, and men climbed our worn steps again for counsel, glad as men that mend a sea-wall in the calm. Of the stones none asked, and we said naught. It was we who turned the key. That is our share of it.

But humility came too late.

The first dark sails were already on the sea.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

### IV.3 · The Warden's Door

*The third winter, in the Tide of Remembering.*

*The words of Brenn of the outpost, as {{Wendhessa}} of the guild at Eldhythe took them from his mouth in his old age. {{Halyna}} read them at this hearth, and the annal after them is mine.*

"We two hold the stone,"

"but the words are not ours. As they were written, so we read them."

Nineteen I was. Twelve were we, and the warden, at the last post of the land, where the rock runneth out toward the grey. Old Tobe had fished the edge thirty years. I was the runner, sworn at fifteen with mine hand flat on the runners' stone: "What I saw, I carry. I carry nothing else."

Black wood had ▒▒▒▒ set everywhere: in the doorposts, in his table, in the frame of his bed. He held the post by his father's name on the Trade Council, and was scarce older than his runner. For his temper they had sent him to the edge, where there were but the twelve of us for it to wound. He had never known battle. He had never known patience.

One morning at summer's end, out of the grey came a small grey boat. There was no wind, and the boat came anyway.

The stranger beached it, and walked up to the warden's door. ▒▒▒▒ stood within, and came no further. Tall was the stranger, and pale, and clad in a mist-cloth that moved when he moved not. No weapon saw I on him.

First he spake in his own tongue, loud, like a storm-wind in old trees, and we were afraid. We wist not that it is but how they speak. Then said he three words of ours, carefully, as a man setteth down a thing that might break.

"Stop… Sky… Dying…"

They were the right words, and truly spoken. I know it now. Yet words without sentences are but sounds, and we heard but sounds.

Then kneeled he on the step, and set before the warden a dark stone, the size of two fists. He set it down as ye would set down bread before a guest. No man of us moved. Behind him a gull came down, and walked among the stones.

▒▒▒▒ kicked it aside.

Into the dust by the wall it went. Then laughed his men. I stood among them, and I hear that laughter yet.

The stranger rose. His right hand he laid on the back of the warden's neck, gently, as my mother did when I had a fever. He drew their heads together till the foreheads touched, and was silent. ▒▒▒▒ stood and let it be done. Had it stopped there, I think all our lives had gone otherwise.

Then took the stranger a little blade in his left hand, and touched it to the warden's right arm.

▒▒▒▒ tore himself away. Never in his life had he bled. In the tearing, the cut that should have been shallow went deep, and the blood came.

"Seize him," he screamed.

They seized him. He fought them not. The blade was yet in his hand, turned to his own arm. Between their hands he went down on his knees again, where he had kneeled with his gift. I understood it not.

"Kill him," screamed ▒▒▒▒.

They took up what lay to hand: staves, and stones of the shingle, and the boat-poles. One of them took up my pole, the pole I pushed the boats off with.

He lifted not his hand. If he cried out, it was in his own tongue, and I took it for the wind. The blows I counted. I count them yet. When he lay still, ▒▒▒▒ screamed on, and some went on. Both his arms they broke: the one that had held the warden's neck so gently, and the one that had held the blade.

His men obeyed.

We obeyed.

I say *we*, for the post was one thing that day, and I was of it. No hand laid I on him. I did not stop them. A boy was I among the men.

Then was it quiet, and the gull was gone.

By the heels they drew him down the shingle, and his head went on every stone. Into his own boat they cast him, as a fisher casteth a torn net into the bilge.

▒▒▒▒ held his arm, and looked not at the boat.

"Burn it," said he, "and shove it off, and give it the tide. Leave naught to show."

They set it alight, and it burned not as wood burneth. There was a screaming in it. The stranger was dead. It was no man that screamed. It was the wood itself.

With the same poles they shoved it off. Old Tobe went into the sea to his knees and washed the poles. The rest of us stood on the shingle, and would not look, and looked.

Well out, the boat turned. Into the wind it went, and against the current, and into the grey, burning.

That same day ▒▒▒▒ had the clerk write to the Council. I stood by, and I can say it by heart:

*Unknown intruder. Hostile intent. Neutralized. Vessel burned and released.*

No word of the gift. No word of the kneeling.

On the morrow, ere any woke, I picked the stone out of the dust. Why I did it, I know not. At his door ▒▒▒▒ gave me the letter. So the gift and the lie went into one pack, and I ran it.

A day and a night I ran, and every mile I said the oath over.

At Eldhythe an old clerk of the Council read it, with ink on his fingers, and looked up at me.

"Is it so?"

I said not that it was so. I said, "It is written." Forty years have I weighed that small cleverness.

I carried the lie in a day and a night. I carried the truth forty years, and gave it to no one.

That autumn the shore laughed at three axemen, home on a spar, that sware to a burning boat out of the grey. I laughed not, for I had seen it turn. Yet I said naught. The Council had it in writing.

▒▒▒▒ wore long sleeves the rest of his life, through the hot summers of Eldhythe, and I know why. Last winter he died in his bed of black wood. I am the last of the twelve.

Forty years the stone hath lain at the bottom of my pack, and not once have I held it. I give it up now with these words, and I ask God to forgive me, for there is none left to ask but God.

<!-- HALYNA END -->

So far the words of Brenn. At every blot {{Halyna}} stopped, and ye heard a silence in the place of the name.

The warden's arm bare one mark: the half of something, cut off in the middle. The Council kept the letter.

Brenn died, as a man setteth down a load. The guild buried him under his name. The warden's name it cut away.

{{Wendhessa}} sealed his words, and told no one. When the havens fell they bare the chest out, his hand on one handle, hers on the other. On the Road their arms failed. The masons said it held naught of worth, and gave it to the youngest, Tarnel and me. Tarnel knotted his net with his free hand. I watched the knots.

On the night of the pass {{Wendhessa}} lay down under one cloak. At morning frost lay on both handles. Neither rose.

Tarnel did not wake.

There I stopped tonight, with my hands on the stone. Then {{Halyna}} laid theirs beside them.

"She carried it every step,"

"and after the passes she carried it alone."

There is no older law among any people than this: that whoso kneeleth at your door is under your roof. We were a people of doors.

This we lay as it was laid for us.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

*Never before had this hearth said it over a killing by our own. Old Ebba began it, and the rest were slow to follow. But they followed.*

*And the Captain put his hood back.*

*In the morning the runners sware their oath again, and added four words: Not as Brenn carried.*
