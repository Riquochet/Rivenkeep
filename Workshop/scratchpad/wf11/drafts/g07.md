### IV.5 · The Burning Boat

*The second summer, in the Second Tide.*

*Laid by Voss who asked, as his grandmother Elspet told it him at her fire on the quay at Eldhythe. It is a tale the ship-masters would not tell, and a rhyme the children would not stop singing; the shore laughed at it, and the sea laughed not.*

Give me the stone. This one is not mine. It is my grandmother's, and she had it from her brother, and he had it from the sea.

In the first holding of this war, on the shingle, I heard one of their hulls burn. It screamed. Along the wall the old sailors turned their faces from the sea, and Merrick first. On the east wall Jory whistled.

A rhyme went round in me like a skipping-rope. At seven I had skipped to it. At forty I understood it.

A boy at her table, I asked my grandmother wherefore the ship-masters would not hear it. She answered me with a tale. Ye may weigh it.

At twelve she kept the fire in her mother's harbour-house. In out of the rain came the ship-masters, with the weather in their beards. Over the fire hung three flat grey stones, with letters fine as the tracks of birds on wet sand. What they said, none knew. Only the children asked. The ship-masters knocked out their pipes on the lowest one.

At every bold man home from the grey with a tale she laughed, and filled his cup. "The Mystlands make liars of honest men," said she.

She said it of her brother.

Daveth was an axeman of the felling. That summer he was coming home from the edge in one of the last timber-boats, low with black wood, and a second in his wake.

Out of the grey a boat came at them, burning, into the wind and against the current, which no boat can do. Screaming it came, as gulls come at boys that rob a nest. Low it struck them both, where the load rode heaviest, and the black wood in their holds took its fire. After came the rain, on the heads of two crews.

Three came home.

Daveth came home on a spar, with the sea running out of his coat onto the floor my grandmother had swept. So she told it me. All that autumn the three told it, and never again set foot in a boat.

After them the outpost men sware in their cups to a boat shoved off burning, with a dead man in it. Well out, they sware, it turned. To the scream they sware above all, and to three words of ours the grey man spake; but which three, no two agreed.

She counted the boats for me on her fingers, as her brother had counted them for her. One finger, and another. Then she laid her hand flat on the board.

There were two boats.

Now the ship-masters say twenty.

The shore laughed at the three from the spar. By day she laughed with it, and knocked out her pipe on the lowest stone.

At night she did not laugh.

Daveth drank on his tale, and the harbour drowned him a stone's throw from his mother's house.

Wherefore would the ship-masters not hear it? I have seen one set down his cup and go out into the rain sooner than hear it. Three of them have I heard since, in the passes, each saying set down like a cup.

"A small boat burning, far out in the grey," said the first. The second: "It turned into the wind, and after it, weather." The third: "It is the boat that cometh for you when ye die." And one: "Let the shore laugh, for the shore will never have to meet it."

The cities had it in writing: a stranger come armed, and put down, and his boat burned and set adrift. And who will argue with writing?

But the children had a rhyme for it, and children argue with anything. We skipped to it on the quay, two turning and one jumping. Jory and I turned the rope, for we were the biggest and the slowest, and for the least of them Jory turned it slower yet.

*Then the children sang, as they sing it still:*

> *Grey boat, grey boat, out of the grey,*  
> *knelt at the warden's door one day;*  
> *a stone for a present, a cut for a kiss,*  
> *and who ever heard of a greeting like this?*  
> *Burn it and shove it and give it the tide;*  
> *but the grey boat turned, and the grey boat cried.*

Two lines more there were, that we sang not, lest a ship-master hear.

> *Grey boat burning, and after, the rain;*  
> *and the one it comes for will not come home again.*

One evening Merrick heard us at it. He took our rope, and coiled it on his arm, as men coil a line they mean to keep. "Not that one," said he.

His father was Daveth.

Merrick, eldest of the ship-masters, sitteth by the door tonight. He is not gone out.

All that first winter Jory whistled it on the east wall, the tune and never the words. He meant, when the havens came home, to drink again at my grandmother's fire. Every morning Merrick bade him stop, and every morning Jory laughed. "It is only a rhyme."

On the morning he died I was at his side, and the tune went before him along the wall-walk. Slow he whistled it, as he had turned the rope for the least of us. Then the rhyme stood up hard in me, as a mooring-line at the flood. I heard the two lines we never sang.

Merrick told him to stop.

I did not tell him to stop.

Below us a fire-ship came in on the stones, burning. It screamed as it came.

The tune stopped.

His is the first cairn on the east face.

If my grandmother's house stand yet, the three grey stones hang there over a cold hearth, under no roof but the rain.

We forget. We have always been good at that. But Mystwood remembers.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

## BOOK FIVE · THE BOOK OF THE TIDES

*The war: a doom set in motion by the dead, crewed by the living, against a people who had never known they were the foe.*

### V.1 · The First Glyph

*The first winter, at the first month's end.*

*Laid by Seren Two-Inks, that held the lamp. The sand of that shingle is in the binding of this Book yet.*

Hold the stone, Seren.

On the shingle {{Halyna}} held the wood, and I held the lamp: their four hands on the grain, and my one. As it began, so it hath gone on.

Nigh a month had the grey sails lain off this wall, and what they were, none of us wist. The night the first assault was broken, the tide brought their wreck in under the wall: grey planks, broken ribs, a keel as long as a gate. Over and over it rolled in the shallows, as though the sea were turning each piece to see its underside.

The old sailors on the wall would not go down to it. The soldiers went, and made their fires of it along the tide-line, for wood was dear on the ridge. Harl of the burning shore broke a rib of it across his knee, and fed it in. One by one the fires went up, as lamps go up along a quay at dusk. The Captain let them burn the most of it, and stood among them, still.

While the first fires caught, Hale the runner came up from the water's edge. Past the fires he went, and past the Captain, to {{Halyna}}.

"Marks on the wood."

At the turn of the tide {{Halyna}} went down, Halvard's hand under Rhyna's elbow on the wet stones. I came behind with the lamp, for I was their third, and the light was mine to carry. The men hauled one plank up out of the surf, and I set the lamp down by it.

Along its grain ran one figure, fine and shallow as a mason's first scoring. One long edge was raw, split down the grain, as if the rest had been torn away.

Of things that might speak, we had the mute stones in the archive chest, and a bundle under the seal of {{Wendhessa}} that no pair would break. Now we had this. Axemen of the felling had sworn in their cups that the black wood would put a thought into a man's hand, if he rested it there long enough. The shore had laughed at them. Naught else was left us to try.

So Rhyna sat down by the plank, and laid her palms flat on the grain. Halvard kneeled behind her, and laid his hands over hers. They spake not. The tide came and went. The lamp burned down a finger's width.

Then the knowing came up into Rhyna's palms. It burned, she said, not as fire, but as cold iron in the deep of winter. Its shape was in the burning: one stroke curling forward, like a wave about to break. With no word from her, Halvard lifted his head.

"Shore," he said.

Rhyna said that he was right.

How he knew it, Halvard could not say. It was as though someone had put the word into his mouth, he said, and gone away. More than the word they knew, but the rest was a forest's thought, and they had but the shore's. Till the lamp was low they held it, and one word was all they could tell.

They rose stiff from the stones, and others came down to try it. First came Jory of Eldhythe, that sitteth tonight at the back of this hall, beside Voss. He laid his hand on it, laughing. "Wet planks," said he, and went up the shingle whistling, the tune and never the words.

Then came Enno and Della, that walked up the mountain hand in hand. Their four hands they laid on the plank together. He said the wood was cold, and she said it was not, and both were right.

When they had gone, I sat where Rhyna had sat. Alone I laid mine own palm on the grain. Something came up into it out of the wood, too great to hold. It was a river in flood, and I a girl with a cup.

I let go.

Halvard put the lamp back in my hand.

Before we went up, I opened this Book on my knees at a clean leaf, though leaves were dear then. In the middle I set one word, *shore*, and naught else. Ere the ink touched it, I said it over three times, as I do with every word, to hear whether it would hold. It held. Short it was, and square, and it sat in the mouth as a dressed stone in its bed.

On the facing leaf I drew the sign. In the sea air the ink would not dry.

The word looked very small on its leaf.

We came up with the fires low behind us. At the gate {{Halyna}} stopped.

"The grey man of the old tale came to a door with three words, and no sentence."

"Now it is we who have one word, and no sentence. The right sign, known truly; and a sign without a sentence is only a mark."

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*
