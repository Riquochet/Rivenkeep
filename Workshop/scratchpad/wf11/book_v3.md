## OF THIS BOOK

*The third summer, in the Tide of Remembering.*

*By Seren Two-Inks, scribe of this Book, and the third of the hearth of {{Halyna}}.*

It is the short dark of the year. The hall sleepeth about me, and the Long Hearth is sunk to coals. On the wall I see the banner lift, in a wind I hear not.

The Book is half full. One of its tellers, that held up a wet finger to every wind, is under the east cairns already. Tonight have I turned back to the first leaf. I left it bare, for the lintel is not cut ere the posts are raised.

At this hearth the first builders of the Keep laid their tales. When the Keep was left, the tales went cold with the hearth. Now sit we where they sat, when naught is left to do with the hands but hold them to the fire.

Beside me in its niche is the stone, the old capstone of the inner gate. Face down it lay in the gateway, with its vow to the earth, till {{Halyna}} turned it at dusk on the day of the banner. It is a stone that two must turn. Its vow is sharp yet.

So write I every bonded pair, under one stroke, as a lintel on two posts. It is one name, and it is the name of two.

{{Halyna}} are Halvard Stone-Warden and Rhyna of the Inner Gate. {{Idrenna}}, the eldest, taught them, and nod by the door when a tale is laid true. {{Orvenna}}, our wall-masters, never raise their voices. Of {{Enrella}}, the youngest, the one cannot begin a jest but the other will end it.

When a tale is done we say not that it was told, but *laid*. A tale that is but told is gone by morning. A tale that is laid is part of the wall.

Two words answer every tale of ours, and never other two: *We remember.*

The first night this hearth asked a tale, none of us wist what to say. Old Ebba of Fenholm spake them into the quiet. None bade her, and none bade us follow. But the hearth took the words from her. Most nights she is first yet, a breath before the rest, and in behind her we come, as a tide behind its first wave.

Forgetting came on our fathers, and none chose it. Remembering we choose, as masons choose which stones to keep.

Some tales are the wood's, most out of the wreck-wood. Our rolls forget, and can be made to lie. The wood cannot lie, but it cannot see fear.

One came out of a dark stone. It lay in the chest at my feet, under the seal of {{Wendhessa}}, that bare the chest out of Eldhythe at the Fall. No pair would break that seal till the wood showed {{Halyna}} what the mute stones beside it were. Then {{Idrenna}} gave leave.

The wood is not read but known, whole, as a memory is known. So {{Halyna}} give it up in pieces, the one taking up where the other's words fail.

I set the pieces down as they fell, in two inks. They would not have me mark whose is whose. Ye cannot tell. Out of the pieces I make the tale. After it the hearth is silent, and that silence I have written down.

{{Halyna}} would have the wood's leaf on the right, facing ours.

<!-- HALYNA -->

"A tale told from one side of a wall,"

"is half a tale."

<!-- HALYNA END -->

Where the wood's leaf could not yet be told, I have left it bare, with only the grain showing: the shape of what we understood not yet.

I am not of the Bonded, though {{Halyna}} took me into their hearth as its third. When they know the wood, I hold the lamp. Once I laid mine own palm on the grain, and let go. Mine is the hand to write, and not the hand to know.

Every scribe before me was one of a pair. I write alone.

The Captain will hear all, and tell naught, and keep his hood up.

Where a carving was broken, I have not mended it with guesses. Where the guild cut out a name, I have set a blot. Where the wood speaketh, I have written its own names for things, and not ours.

Each night I set the tale in our tongue as masons set a course, every joint broken over the joint below. Each word I say aloud three times ere I write it, to hear whether it will hold. The tale is the teller's. The words are mine. I read each leaf to him that laid it, and no teller yet hath asked for his own back.

Most nights the coals are gone to ash, and the last word is yet in my mouth. So it is tonight.

I love our tongue.

Up the mountain I carried two things, our tongue and a chest; and only the chest was heavy.

This Book is ink. {{Halyna}} would have it cut in the granite of this hall, course on course, when peace is come. Till then it lieth in that chest, among the mute stones.

## BOOK ONE · THE BOOK OF THE WALL

*How we came to stand.*

### I.1 · The Torn Cloak

*The first winter, the night after the Rite.*

*Laid by Kael the Counter, on the first night this hearth asked a tale. He forbade me to change one word of it; I changed a good many, and when I read it him, he sware it was word for word as he had told it.*

Give me the stone. No, I will stand. Enough have I sat this winter.

By the lintel of the western stair I swear it, where the old wardens cut their names. A teller am I, and no singer. Heads I tell, and stones, and days. What I told not, that will I not tell.

At dusk the column came in under the outer gate. I heard it ere I saw it: many feet, and no voice among them. From the gate I told it, head by head.

Old men came, and women with bundles, and children asleep on their feet, and soldiers of six broken companies, that knew their officers no more. Last came the Captain, that had kept the rear the whole road. Two thousand and some hundreds, all told.

A month had we walked from the ashes of Glasspire to come to a castle. We came to a heap of stones. The gate hung by one hinge, and in the ward the grass stood to the knee. Where the songs had set a great hall, the roof was fallen in, and snow lay on the hearth where ye sit now.

Some shook. Some wept. Some fell, and would not rise.

Then began the crying, and it was not one cry. Some cried that we should beg terms of the grey sails, though no man living ever understood one word they spake. Some cried for the high passes, and the snow beyond them. Some cried that we should go down into the cellars, and bar the doors, and bide, as if the sea would forget us.

Men had I heard break, on the long retreat. Never till that dusk a people. It is a sound like ice going out on a river.

Down off the riven ridge came the wind, with the whole of the winter in its mouth.

The people looked for a general, and the generals were slain. They looked for the lords, and the lords were scattered. They looked to the Stonewrights, and the Stonewrights sat in the grass with their chisels in their laps.

To the Captain looked none of them. Captain was he of a small company, and we were his company. All the long road he had waited for one whose place it was to lead, and none came, for they were dead. Mistake me not: there was no greater man, and his equal, I think, never lived. But he sought not to lead. I swear to that.

Then he climbed it. In the hall he had taken a coal from the dead hearth, this hearth, and why, none of us wist. Alone he went up the broken face, by the stones that stood proud of the mortar. We stood at the foot and let him, for he had not bidden us follow. I have told those stones since: thirty-one there are, and four of them move.

At the top he took off his cloak. Crimson it was once, ere the road wore it to brick. In that wind it was all he had.

No man in the ward spake. Among the women a child coughed, and his mother hushed him. After that there was naught but the wind.

He tore it down the back.

A small sound it made, the sound of old cloth giving. Over the wind I heard it.

The one half he spread on the capstones, and kneeled on it with the coal, and wrote. Slowly he wrote, as men write that have written little. That half he bound to a pike out of the rubble. The other half, with naught written on it, he put back on his shoulders.

First up after him was I, and first to read it. Not once had his hand shaken. I turned to the ward and read it out, and my voice was not steady, and I will not feign it was.

### I.2 · The Cry of the Bonded

*The first winter, after the first assault.*

*Mine own, and the first tale that ever I laid; this hearth had heard but one before it. On the day I tell of I was twelve winters old, and a child of the guild.*

Tonight I was afraid to speak, and {{Halyna}} laid my two hands on the stone.

<!-- HALYNA -->

"Hold the stone."

"Hold it, and it will hold thee."

<!-- HALYNA END -->

So I hold it.

At dusk on the day of the banner the guild sat in the grass of the old ward, some hundreds of us, with our chisels in our laps. Thin as ash lay the last light on the snow. On the wall the soldiers shouted for the banner. In the grass we said naught.

There sat I, that had no craft yet but the carrying of water. Over the high passes I had walked beside a boy of the guild, that knotted a net as he went. At night I slept at his side.

I came into that courtyard alone.

A little after me came two children with their hands joined, Enno and Della, of fifteen winters. Of the children's pairs, none came up the mountain whole save those two. They sat down together, and held on.

Ten havens had we watched die in twenty months. From each we walked away with our chisels in our packs. No hand had we lifted, for our creed forbade it: *Build, do not destroy. Protect, do not attack.* Mortar and chisel were our only arms, and they had not been enough.

The faith of twelve generations sat on us yet, like a wet cloak. Every elder there turned over the same hard thing: that walls raised for peace must at the last be held with blood. And if that were so, whose blood? Whose hands?

{{Halyna}} I knew not then, save as two elders that sat apart under the broken inner gate, and spake to no one, for they had no need. Her line had laid that gate. His had cut its stone out of the mountain. Husband and wife they were, and one since the cradle.

Then came the Captain down from the wall. I had seen the cloak go up, and wist not what it was to us. Not tall was he. The coal-black was yet on his fingers, and the half of a cloak on his back. He looked at the elders, and past them at the broken gate, and asked one question. It is the only speech of his that any at this hearth can say over:

"Your walls are falling. Will you build, or will you watch and die?"

No one answered him. I remember the wind in the grass.

Then {{Halyna}} stood up.

To the torn cloak they looked up, and bowed to it in the same breath. Up went their chisels as one.

Their two chisels took the last of the light.

She began the cry, and he ended it:

<!-- HALYNA -->

"The mortar cries out from the ground. Rise, Stonewrights! Lay stone upon stone. Stand every wall to the very end."

"Rise! Rise! Rise!"

<!-- HALYNA END -->

One cry it was, in two throats. Hers came first for no cause but that a cry must begin somewhere. Some tell it that she called and he answered, with a silence between.

There was no silence.

Then stood the guild, like an arch when the frame is struck from under it, every stone on its neighbour. I stood with them, and remember not the standing. We took up the cry, and the soldiers on the wall took it from us, and the column from the soldiers.

Old stone trembleth not. Under my feet I felt it tremble.

That night this hearth had its first fire in more years than any could tell. By it {{Idrenna}} made the terms with the Captain, and I carried in the water. Slow they spake, as the old speak, and the Captain hurried them not.

The Stonewrights would bear no arms, and he asked it not. But they would build between the volleys, and mend a breach under the guns. So the terms stand yet, in the guild's own words: *with the speed and precision that only Aetherbonded pairs could achieve*. Then {{Idrenna}} gave it him in two halves:

"Not the sword, but the stone."

"Not the killing, but the keeping."

The Captain bowed his head. It is the guild's word now.

Ere that fire burned down, {{Orvenna}} split the siege day in three, as quarrymen split a block along its own seam. To the Captain they said:

"Every gun ye haul is a course we lay not,"

"so spend a gun as a careful man spendeth silver."

*Then the masons sang, as they sing it still:*

> *Lay the stone while the sea lies bare,*  
> *haul the gun while the guns are cold,*  
> *hold the wall when the grey sails come,*  
> *and the wall will hold.*

And wherever ye see a Stonewright on the wall in the holding, look again. Ye will see two.

Late that night, by torches that hissed in the falling frost, we laid the first course of the war in the gap of the inner gate. Every joint we broke over the joint below. For the old gate-song of the havens {{Orvenna}} asked the clearest voices. Up stood Enno and Della, and took the first voice between them, the one to the comma and the other the rest.

*Then they sang, and the guild after them:*

> *Stone upon stone, and the stone keeps faith;*  
> *hand over hand, and the hand holds true;*  
> *two at the gate when the grey sails gather,*  
> *two at the gate, and the gate stands through.*

I sang to the comma, and then I sang the rest.

When the torches were stubs, Enno and Della lay asleep against the new course, their hands yet joined, as they had come up the mountain. I lay down a little way off, by the water-pails, and slept.

By morning the mortar had set.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

### I.3 · The Captain's Long Breath

*The first winter, in its second month.*

*Laid by Hale the runner, that was seventeen and in his first weeks on the wall. He told it standing, and as fast as ever he ran; I have set it down slower.*

Give me the stone, then. I am not a teller. I run.

Every haven had its runners' stone. Ours we made the first week, of a fallen block by the inner gate, and on it we sware the old oath:

"What I saw, I carry. I carry nothing else."

Wick sware it last, the youngest of us and the swiftest. Naught would he sooner do than carry a word to the Captain.

At the grey of the morning of the first assault I stood by Kael's guns, that are yet all we have. The Captain gave Kael his mind: where to watch, what shot to lay, and when to hold. Then went he on, to the far end of the wall. Kael laid his hand on my shoulder.

"Naught may change that mind but his own voice at my side, lad, and a man can stand but in one place."

*Then the troops drilled the three calls, as they drill them still:*

> *Shift your ground, and watch new water;*  
> *change your shot for the sea, the shore, the sky;*  
> *hold, or loose, when the Captain is by.*

The drill done, Jory of Eldhythe leaned on the parapet of the east wall and whistled a tune that the old ship-masters hate. Merrick, eldest of them, coiling rope at the stair-foot, bade him stop. Jory laughed down at him.

"It is only a rhyme."

Merrick did not laugh.

Ere the morning was half gone, the sails of my first holding came out of the grey faster than I could tell them. In the laying the hours go slow, for stone is heavy, and in the hauling slower. In the holding an hour is gone in a heartbeat.

Late in the holding, when the rest were spent and the masons were come out on the breach, Kael's lookout saw a hull in a far-glass of Pellow's grinding. It had hung back in the grey, where none had hung before. Now it came in, late and alone.

Between the Captain and that hull lay the smoke. Kael gave me three words.

"Hull hangeth back."

I ran it.

I run. Under the smoke, head down, past the masons, past the ropes. To no man speak I, and I stop not. Up into the clear, above the gate.

The Captain standeth still.

That is what I remember first. Everything on that wall was moving but him. One hand he had on the parapet, his face to the sea. He looked not round at me. Yet he heard me to the last word.

Then he walked toward Kael's guns at his own pace, and I at his shoulder. As he walked, the hour began to stretch.

It stopped not short. It slowed, like a stone-cart on a long hill, till a boy behind might count the spokes. I have no better way to say it.

I saw the water sheet off the late hull's bow. I counted the ripples going down its grey sail, as ye count the rings on a cut log: one, and then the next. Each, I think, was a minute. The gulls hung. The smoke stood. On he walked.

I had time to be afraid. Then I had time to be ashamed of it. Then I had time to stop.

At Kael's shoulder he stopped. Kael was counting under his breath. Then the Captain gave the call, with his own voice.

"Hold."

The battery held. Below us the masons laid on, two and two, and looked not up. The late hull came on through the smoke, and on, till I could see the wet on its timbers.

"Loose."

The hour let us go, slow, like a held breath going out. The guns went. Out in the smoke the hull broke up, in the one stretch of that water where it could hurt no one, where the Captain had meant it to break.

I know not what the Captain thought. I know what he did.

That evening I walked the tide-line, as we runners do after every holding. In the shallows I saw marks cut in a plank, and carried them up past the fires to {{Halyna}}:

"Marks on the wood."

I ran it.

That night I found my way back by Hesk's lamp, his mother's lamp, on the north wall. By it I wist the north was awake.

Now the men say it along the wall: "For the Captain, an hour lasteth as long as a day." I think it began with me, though Voss will have it older. They say it as though the Rite had given it him. So said I myself, and I know better now. Kael mended it, and I carry it as he said it, and nothing else:

"It is not the world that sloweth, lad. It is the Captain who will not hurry. The moment he chose was the moment that came."

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

### I.4 · The Rite of the Cornerstone

*The first spring of the siege.*

*Laid by Seren Two-Inks, the third of the hearth of {{Halyna}}. In the Fourteen Nights I was a child of the guild, and they trusted me with naught but water.*

Hold the stone, Seren. I tell myself so every time.

On the third night after the cry a light moved, and I woke. Rhyna was risen, and in the same breath Halvard took up the lamp. I called not out. Down from me the light went, turning by turning, till it was a spark in a well.

Over their one cradle Rhyna's mother had sung the rhyme of {{Aldwena}}'s line, and what it meant, none had asked:

> *Under the hall and under the floor,*  
> *under the stair that goes no more,*  
> *down where the rope let the first stone go,*  
> *the first two keep what the first two know.*

Under the stair that went no more, Rhyna wist where to look, and down the shaft went Halvard. She paid out the rope, and neither spake a word.

At the foot, as they told it me, stood a chest of black stone, the dust a finger deep on its lid. Halvard swept it with his sleeve.

It was the mark of {{Aldwena}}.

Naught moved in them.

At the fire {{Halyna}} read the slates for their meaning, a line and a breath and a line, and I set my bucket down and forgat it.

At the slates beneath, the breath between the lines grew long. Walls sloped to turn a shot. Gates set crooked against the ram. Courses laid that a breach should fall outward. The first builders had known war. Against what, the slates say not.

Three oils the Rite must have, that none living had made.

For the cedar, Stannard led {{Halyna}} through the snow to the stand where the first staff was cut. A living tree would they not fell. On the fallen trunks Stannard knocked with his knuckle, and listened.

"I know which of these will hold."

They rendered the heartwood at this hearth, and on a wet night its stones breathe it out yet.

Bloom the masons call the pale crust on worked stone untended for lives of men, for it flowereth where none come.

### I.5 · The Naming of the Rivenmen

*The sixth winter, the eve of the going-down.*

*Laid by Voss who asked, of Eldhythe, True Man of the harbour battery, that was raised in his grandmother's harbour-house on the quay.*

Give me the stone. I have owed this hearth an answer a long while.

Near the end of the first winter I buried a friend on the east face, under a cairn, for the ground there will not take a spade. As boys we had turned the rope together on the quay, while the little ones jumped. The last stone on him I laid myself.

His name was Jory.

That night at this fire I asked a question. Cold I was, and I meant it hard.

"Wherefore fight we for ice and stone?"

Once on the hearthstone knocked Stannard, and answered me.

"It is not the ice I fight for."

That was a good answer, but it was his. Mine hath taken me the whole war to cut.

A slow answer is a slow tide, so my grandmother told it me: it cometh in over the same stones.

It began the week after the Rite, the whole Keep in the ward under the torn cloak. I stood among the companies in the slush, and Jory at my shoulder. For once in his life he was not whistling. The Captain stood with us, and the staff in its corner.

Kael the Counter climbed the fallen stones by the gate. One thing had he to say, and he said it once.

"The Commander fought."

And the whole guild ended it, in one voice, from {{Idrenna}} down to the least child that carried its water:

"The Stonewrights built."

That was all the speech there was. It was enough. We looked at one another, soldier and mason. We were not two peoples sharing a ruin. One were we, as two courses become one wall when the mortar taketh.

That day we laid the Keep's name on the land. Every haven the grey sails hold is Rivenkeep, and the edge of the sea is the edge of the Keep. It is a vow.

We named ourselves the Rivenmen. Our fathers we call Shorelanders, for so they were, and ourselves but *we*. In that ward it began.

All that day we went up one by one to the stone under the pike. Each laid a hand on it and sware, as men make fast their ropes on the quay at evening. Jory went up ere me, and came down whistling. By dark the Keep was held.

We sware to take back every stone. Not one stone that was not ours. But every one that was.

The eldest never liked the new name, and said naught. Into their shawls they murmur "the Shorelands" yet.

This winter, when the ten great hulls had sat down in the havens like herons in the shallows, I asked old Ebba of Fenholm wherefore she kept it. She looked not up from the fire, but held her hands to it.

"It is a name to come home to, when the last wall shall stand, and the last Mystarch lie broken."

To the fire and not to me she said one word more.

"Perhaps."

I did not understand it then. I think I do now.

Four nights since, she sat late on that bench-end, nearest the coals, her shawl to her chin. We went to our blankets and left her sleeping. In the night the fire burned down, and no one mended it.

In the morning she did not wake.

At dusk we laid her on the east face by the soldiers. {{Idrenna}} said the prayer for the dead in two halves. Marl, her grandson, laid the last stone. Over it he said the old name, "the Shorelands." None of us corrected him. The Captain bound a strip of his half-cloak on a pike above her.

It was she that gave this hearth its answer. Most nights she began it, a breath before the rest. These four nights there hath been a space at the front of it, the width of one old voice. None of us have tried to fill it.

Now here is mine, and ye may weigh it. Jory, for whose sake I asked it, never heard it asked.

On the east wall I said once:

"We do not fight because we are right. We fight because our children are behind these walls."

It is true, but not the whole of it. Child have I none of mine own, and yet I said *our*.

From the east tower ye may see the keeps we raised in this war, like posts along a quay, a crimson pennant on every one. But a keep is no place to stay in for ever. It is a place to go out from.

We fight not for ice and stone. Every stone of it is Rivenkeep, and Rivenkeep is home, and on the banner we sware to bring it home.

Old Ebba bideth on the east face. Marl hath in his pack the stone she bare out of Fenholm.

Tomorrow we go down to the havens.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*The hearth waited a breath for Ebba.*

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

*Who we were, and who they were.*

### II.1 · Of the Keep That Was Forgotten

*The first summer of the siege.*

*Laid by Seren Two-Inks. The founders' leaf within it is theirs and not mine: {{Halyna}} read it me out of the old hand, and those words I have not dared to mend.*

The Stonwryt the masters call it: *a vow sealed in living rock, earned by toil and skill*, and no gold buyeth it. Yet the master-pairs of the havens struck their marks on coin, and men took the coin as they took the mark: *trust, sealed in stone*. I have no mark. Never shall I cut one.

Under the first warden's name his sons cut theirs, one beneath another, and beneath the last the stone is smooth. Of that line is Halvard Stone-Warden, and Rhyna of the Inner Gate is of {{Aldwena}}'s. Two lines were one, long ere those two lay in their one cradle.

Then went the folk down to the warm harbours, and the guild after them. Ere they went, the founders laid the Keep's slates and its first staff in the black chest, as masons lay by their tools at dusk. On the coast they raised Eldhythe, that men call the Eldest. It is the eldest only of the havens.

Behind the last of them, whose names are on no slate, the wind went in under the gate that no one kept.

It had never fallen. It had only been left.

Out of mind it went, till only the songs held it. No pair came up to *point* its mortar, and the rain found every joint. I love that word. After the rain came the frost, and after the frost the ice.

In some winter that none told, the capstone of the inner gate came down from its two posts.

No one heard it fall.

The Fall of the havens the whole coast heard. After Glasspire we climbed to the one place the songs said would hold, and found a shadow.

Forgetting had done what no enemy ever could.

In the gateway lay the capstone. Halvard swept the snow from it with his sleeve. On its upper face the bloom was grown as pale as frost on a gatepost.

It was their own.

Its underside bare the mark of {{Aldwena}}, and the vow. Sharp they were, as on the day they were cut. Beside them was the bite of the wardens' chisels. Rhyna laid her fingers on the mark of her forebears. Halvard set his thumb in the bite his fathers left.

It was whole. Out in the ward the folk came in yet. Their feet made no sound on the snow.

The stone kept its vow. It was we who did not keep ours.

Ere the light was off the snow, they cried the guild to its feet.

They that hold it hold the vow of the first builders, and the shame of their children. Tonight it lieth under my two hands.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

It was the air we breathed.

Flat lay our land under the trees, past all measuring, to the far edge of the world. In their long sleep the trees breathed, and their breath lay down grey among the trunks. It fed the trees, and it fed us, and we tended it as ye tend a hearth.

Through the breath the sun came softened: the between-light, neither day nor dusk. For that light were we made, and never were we children of the dark.

At evening the light went low among the trunks. Then we sang to the pale saplings, and they answered, one stand and then the next, till the deep wood was one choir. Our little ones lay down among the roots to hear it. One by one their small voices left the song, and they slept in the singing.

Ferns of the deep wood, cast on bare sand, wither in the open sun. So wither we.

In the years of the iron we saw the open sun wither our children.

<!-- RING -->

All our long summers we led the wood, root to water, and where we led it, there it grew. One life it was, in two kinds, and the wood kept its own will besides.

Without the breath the wood is sick. Without the wood the breath cometh not again. Without either we fail, and slowly, for we live twice as long as ye, and half again: the eldest last, and the youngest first.

Among the twisted boughs we kept our rites, and under the silver elders our council, for none lie beneath a silver elder.

Our speech is the storm in the high boughs, all cracks and breaks. Soft is all we carve. The thunder is for the moment, the softness for always.

What is carved is not heard but known: lay a hand on it, and ye know what the carver meant, whatever tongue he spake.

We wist not that there was any other way to be believed.

<!-- RING -->

At the very edge, where the breath came down to your sea, stood our pillars in their long ranks. Between them they held the breath, as a mast holdeth a sail. We call them the Naelsaen, the mist-hearts: the anchors of our sky, and the only trees of ours ye ever found.

From among them we watched your shore, summer on summer. We saw your havens raised. We saw your lamps lit at dusk. We saw your boats come a little way in, and turn back.

Once a boat came farther than any. So near it came that we heard the oars stop. A man stood up in the bow and looked full at us.

We wove the breath thicker.

He saw but grey. His face went white, and the oars went back the way they came.

We wist not why.

Here is our fault, and the grain will not leave it out. We went not down to your shore. We hid, and were glad. So long hid we, and so well, that never once were we seen.

Since your first haven we had listened to you. Ye had never once heard us. We had made sure of it.

This is our home: the flat wood under the breath, the between-light, the pale saplings singing at evening, the silver elders at council. And at the edge of the world, the pillars, with the breath held up between them.

If any green be left of us, it remembereth this; and the grain holds it still.

## BOOK THREE · THE BOOK OF THE HAVENS

*Ten havens, and ten men to tell them: what we had, and how we walked away from it.*

### III.1 · Of Their Raising

*The first autumn of the siege.*

*Laid by the True Men, each for his own haven, as Kael the Counter called them, and every man stood to answer, as under the banner. {{Halyna}} had found the rolls in the chest I carried. What was in them and in no man's memory, I read out.*

Give me the stone. No, I will stand. At a roll men stand.

At first light I went up the east tower. From there that coast may be seen yet, as far as the haze. Not one hearth on it sendeth up smoke.

Hear now the names of the havens, and hold them, for they are ours.

Another man's city I tell not. I call the haven, and its own man shall answer, and swear what he will do when it is come home. Oath-keeper am I. I hold them.

*First I read from the rolls, as he bade me. Builders were our fathers, and no seafarers. The open water they feared, and built where the mountains go down in long falls to the sea, and over the mountains they paved roads like veins. Each haven's roll beginneth with the one line:* This day was the first course laid, and a staff cut for the work. *Small is the clerks' hand, and patient, as if they were loath to waste the leaf. Every wall of the ten was Stonewright work. Ten havens, and ten staffs.*

**Eldhythe, the Eldest Haven.**

Corlen of the harbour. Fifty years the evening tide brought the boats home past my quay, slow, to the warmest harbour-houses on the coast. The first boat of ours to come home, I will steer it in.

*Then Corlen sat, eldest of the Twelve, and wet a finger and held it up, as at every holding on the wall. There is no wind in this hall. The young men at the back laughed. To them he said what he saith to his gunners when they would fire: "Wait. The sea hath not finished."*

**Tidesmeet, where the river meeteth the tide.**

Tamm of the three spans. On both banks it stood, and three great spans bound it across the water.

*Tamm set three stones out by the fire, in their order, as by his blanket every night.*

One span is a plank. Three is a city. On the bank where they stood, I will set them out.

**Fenholm, the town that was always sinking.**

Marl the fen-born. Our town stood on pilings, and sank a hand's breadth a lifetime, and every generation laid a new course on the old. A fen-man that cannot laugh at that had best go and live on a hill.

Old Ebba, my grandmother, laid the last course of our house. A stone of it is in my pack, and I would carry her too, if she would let me. She is there by the fire, nodding. I am to lay her stone on the old course.

**Holtward, the keep under the canopy.**

Aske of the canopy. It lay so close among the trees that a traveller might pass under it and see no wall. No bough would we cut, and every autumn we swept the wall-walk of its leaves by hand.

*By the hearth lay the night's wreck-wood. On every plank of it Aske laid his hand, ere it went on the fire.*

These were trees once. No axe shall come into Holtward while I live.

**Carnhold, the stronghold of the cairns.**

Garvel of the smithies. We hewed it among the cairns of the first quarrymen, and a man could tell by the ring which of its forges was at work. Every gun on this wall I listen to, as a mother to a cough. Guns that ring true speak true. But at an anvil of Carnhold I will make a thing that is not a gun, and hear it ring.

**Emberhythe, the forge on the burning shore.**

Harl of the burning shore. It stood where the mountain smoketh into the sea, and the ground was so warm that our children ran barefoot in the snow. My shore I will have again, and the grey I will burn, and the sea too, if it would take fire.

**Sandreach, the citadel carved in the canyon wall.**

Ulden of the galleries. Sandreach had no harbour. We called it a haven all the same, for it sheltered us. Dug it was, not built, gallery below gallery into the red rock. Every night till the havens fell I slept with the whole mountain for my roof, and mine ear to the floor, and heard it hold. Under that roof I will sleep again.

**Rimewatch, the watch on the frozen mere.**

Hesk of the mere. All winter its great hall stood out on the ice, and the town feasted under its roof, and was warm. Every dusk a taper went from window to window, and the fishers out on the ice steered home by the lamps. My mother kept those lamps. One of them is lit on the north wall tonight. On the ice I will light it again.

**Highreach, the tower of the floating stones.**

Lanner of the crags. So high were its crags that every course went up on cranes, and hung on the wind as hawks hang over a field. A wind I can smell a day off. A boy of those crags can whistle one up ere he can walk. I will climb the highest again, and whistle for the wind.

**And Glasspire, the last and proudest.**

Pellow of the spire. Seven generations raised it, and it caught the sun like a blade of ice. At the top stood the great glass, that threw the sun a day's sail out into the grey. My father ground it. What I carried of it up the mountain in straw, I have ground into our far-glasses. When Glasspire is come home, mine shall be the first eye on it.

*Then Pellow, youngest of the Twelve, held his glass up to the fire, as he holdeth it up to everything, his supper among the rest. To the children at the front he said, "Look close."*

Ten cities, ten harbours, ten kindreds that called themselves one nation.

Twelve of us answered under the banner. Ten have sworn tonight. Stannard of the ridge sware naught, for the ridge was never a haven. He knocked once on the hearthstone with his knuckle. No stone will he trust till he have knocked on it.

Ten havens have I told tonight, and ten oaths I hold. I have no haven.

The Captain found me on a road.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

### III.2 · Of Their Fall

*The second winter, in the Second Tide.*

*Laid by the True Men again, each for the fall of his own city, on a night of hard frost when the guns were cooling and the mortar was not yet set. Kael the Counter called them, and I spake for the guild.*

Give me the stone. No, I will stand. This is no tale to sit for.

Their names ye held in the autumn. Hold them now through the falling, and look not away, for we looked not away in the telling.

In the last spring of the peace, twenty months ere the banner, the swallows nested in the arches of the gates. No man of the havens ever broke a nest. The havens had no fleet of war. Their walls were built for the high days and the weather. Their gates were wide, and stood open at night.

Out of the grey, without warning, came the dark sails. No envoy came that any man could understand, but a knocking out of the grey, like thunder, on and on. The soldiers on this wall call it the drums. We looked for a captain on those ships, and a crew, and one man at one rope. No one stood on the decks. I swear to that.

So we called it the ghost fleet, and its senders the Mystaeri, after the mist. A man must call his death by some name.

Plainer were those sails than the sails that come against us now. Each came alone, and needed no more. No haven sent to another for help, for none had any help to send.

*Then each laid his hand on the stone in his turn, Corlen first.*

**Eldhythe.**

On the first morning of that spring the dark sails came in at the harbour mouth, on the flood. From dawn to dusk the harbour burned: the boats at their moorings first, then the harbour-houses, where the ship-masters of all the coast had sat at the fire.

*Then he waited, as at his guns, and the hall waited with him.*

At evening the wind laid the smoke across the city, so that the city saw not its harbour go. Three months it held, and on the last evening the tide came in over the ash, slow, as it always came, and the sea had not finished.

*Corlen took his hand from the stone for Tamm. It was the last time he held it.*

**Tidesmeet.**

Three days it took. From the high road we watched its three spans go down into the river, and heard them go. The upper went, and then the lower. The middle stood longest, and we waited for it, and it went. That night I climbed down in the dark, and took a stone of each.

**Fenholm.**

In one night it went under, as if the fen had only been waiting. A town is but the last course on the old, we used to say, and laugh. That night no one laughed. My grandmother carried her course-stone out on her own back, and would not give it me till the fen had closed over the last course.

**Holtward.**

It burned in its trees, and the leaves came down burning on the wall-walk we had swept. I laid mine hand on the last tree standing. Its bark was as warm as a living hand.

**Carnhold.**

It burned in its smithies. In the street of the smiths I heard the hammers stop, one forge and then the next. The last anvil rang a while alone.

Then it did not ring.

**Emberhythe.**

Five months it held on its burning shore, and the column wintered with us, warm on the bare ground. When it fell, a mason of ours carried a charred plank out in his pack. I told him to burn it.

**Sandreach.**

Gallery by gallery it was taken from us. Each night I lay with mine ear to the floor, and heard the gallery above me go ere any runner came. At the last we came out in the dark at the canyon's foot. Every night since, I have slept on the open ground, with mine ear to it.

**Rimewatch.**

On the first ice it was taken. The great hall burned on the mere with its lamps yet lit. Window by window the fire went, as my mother's taper went at dusk. It burned as it had always shone.

Into the smoke I went, to her window, and took down her lamp. It was lit.

Then I turned for the shore. The ice was new, a night old. All the way across the mere it spake under me, far off and then near. Once the wind took the flame down to the wick, and I put mine hand about it till it stood again. Behind me the roof went in. Before me there was no light but the one in mine hand.

The ice held.

I carried the lamp out lit. Every dusk since, I have lit it on the north wall, and the night-watch steer by it.

Someone must keep a light.

**Highreach.**

Before the sails cometh the wind. A whole day ahead of them I smelled the grey on it, and there was naught to do with that day but go. Down the crag-stairs we came in the dark, with the children on our backs. Behind us the cranes stood burning on the crags.

**Glasspire.**

At the first snows it went down in one night into its own cellars, spire and glass together. The dark beneath it lieth open yet. Some that fled say a shadow stood off in the fog that night, greater than any hull. I was on the last stair of the spire, with the glass coming down about me in pieces, and the snow with it.

I looked close. I saw nothing.

*Then Pellow sat down with his glass in his lap, and held it not up. His place at the stone he gave to me.*

On the last day of every haven, when a mason's hands could do no more, the guild went out by the landward gate. Chisels they carried in their packs, and naught beside. Last went the masters. They had cleaned their trowels, as after every day's work, and wrapped them in sacking against the road.

The Stonewrights did not fight. They simply walked; and I was among them.

The staffs they carried not. The masters leaned each haven's staff against its falling wall, as a woman banketh the fire on her last night in her mother's house, the same as every night. A staff is cut for its work, and leaveth not its work.

Ten staffs there were, and not one came up the mountain.

*Kael laid his hands on it again, and told the Road.*

When Glasspire was gone, the column turned up the mountain. Haven by haven it had gathered, till behind it no haven was left. A month were we in the passes.

On the night of the pass, the coldest of the road, many of the children died. Some of the old died with them. Their cloaks they had given the children.

In the morning the snow fell yet. We rose out of it, as many as could rise, and walked.

At the foot of the passes I counted us, and I counted us at the top. I will not tell you the difference.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

### IV.5 · The Burning Boat

*The second summer, in the Second Tide.*

*Laid by Voss who asked, as his grandmother Elspet told it him at her fire on the quay at Eldhythe. It is a tale the ship-masters would not tell, and a rhyme the children would not stop singing; the shore laughed at it, and the sea laughed not.*

Give me the stone. This one is not mine. It is my grandmother's, and she had it from her brother, and he had it from the sea.

In the first holding of this war, on the shingle, I heard one of their hulls burn. It screamed. Along the wall the old sailors turned their faces from the sea, and Merrick first. On the east wall Jory whistled.

A rhyme went round in me like a skipping-rope. At seven I had skipped to it. At forty I understood it.

A boy at her table, I asked my grandmother wherefore the ship-masters would not hear it. She answered me with a tale. Ye may weigh it.

At twelve she kept the fire in her mother's harbour-house. In out of the rain came the ship-masters, with the weather in their beards. Over the fire hung three flat grey stones, with letters fine as the tracks of birds on wet sand. What they said, none wist. Only the children asked. The ship-masters knocked out their pipes on the lowest one.

At every bold man home from the grey with a tale she laughed, and filled his cup. "The Mystlands make liars of honest men," said she.

She said it of her brother.

Daveth was an axeman of the felling. That summer he was coming home from the edge in one of the last timber-boats, low with black wood, and a second in his wake.

Out of the grey a boat came at them, burning, into the wind and against the current, which no boat can do. Screaming it came, as gulls come at boys that rob a nest. Low it struck them both, where the load rode heaviest, and the black wood in their holds took its fire. After came the rain, on the heads of two crews.

Three came home.

Daveth came home on a spar, with the sea running out of his coat onto the floor my grandmother had swept. So she told it me. All that autumn the three told it, and never again set foot in a boat.

After them the outpost men sware in their cups to a boat shoved off burning, with a dead man in it. Well out, they sware, it turned. To the scream they sware above all, and to three words of ours the grey man spake; but which three, no two agreed.

She counted the boats for me on her fingers, as her brother had counted them for her. One finger, and another. Then she laid her hand flat on the board.

There were two boats.

Now the ship-masters say twenty.

The shore laughed at the three from the spar. By day she laughed with it, and filled their cups under the lowest stone.

At night she did not laugh.

Daveth drank on his tale, and the harbour drowned him a stone's throw from his mother's house.

Wherefore would the ship-masters not hear it? I have seen one leave his cup and go out into the rain sooner than hear it. Three of them have I heard since, in the passes, each saying set down like a cup.

"A small boat burning, far out in the grey," said the first. The second: "It turned into the wind, and after it, weather." The third: "It is the boat that cometh for you when ye die." And the first again: "Let the shore laugh, for the shore will never have to meet it."

The cities had it in writing: a stranger come armed, and put down, and his boat burned and set adrift. And who will argue with writing?

But the children had a rhyme for it, and children argue with anything. We skipped to it on the quay, two turning and one jumping. Jory and I turned the rope, for we were the biggest and the slowest, and for the least of them Jory turned it slower yet.

One evening Merrick heard us at it. He took our rope, and coiled it on his arm, as men coil a line they mean to keep. "Not that one," said he.

His father was Daveth.

Merrick, eldest of the ship-masters, sitteth by the door tonight. He is not gone out.

All that first winter Jory whistled it on the east wall, the tune and never the words. He meant, when the havens came home, to drink again at my grandmother's fire. Every morning Merrick bade him stop, and every morning Jory laughed. "It is only a rhyme."

Merrick told him to stop.

I did not tell him to stop.

Below us a fire-ship came in on the stones, burning. It screamed as it came.

The tune stopped.

If my grandmother's house stand yet, the three grey stones hang there over a cold hearth, under no roof but the rain.

We forget. We have always been good at that. But Mystwood remembers.

<!-- RING -->

And the roots drank.

From that grove the knowing went to every grove, root to root, to the far edge of the world. Every young tree that was to be a hull drank it with the rain. So every hull that came against your wall bare our grief in it; and the grain holds it still.

*And no one answered.*

### V.1 · The First Glyph

"Marks on the wood."

Of things that might speak, we had the mute stones in the archive chest, and a bundle under the seal of {{Wendhessa}} that no pair would break. Now we had this. ### V.2 · The Night of the Naming

*Late in the second summer.*

*I held the lamp that night, and laid this at the hearth on the evening after. The planks I tell of lie yet in the lowest vault, and whoso will may go down and lay a hand on them.*

Hold the stone, Seren.

All that first winter this fire laughed at the grey sails. "They have picked the wrong fortress," said Kael, and I laughed with the rest.

One night late in the Second Tide, {{Halyna}} went down to the oldest room under this hall, where the Rite was found. I came behind with the ink and the lamps. As cold it is in summer as in winter. The rows lay grey on grey, like a field the plough hath turned up stony.

Fifty-three planks of the first winter lay there, and every one carved. The runners had carried them home two to a plank, from wherever the sea gave them up, the salt running down their arms. The soldiers' fires had the rest, and the tide. The wall called them the tablets. They were but planks.

Each but one had given {{Halyna}} one word, and each word I wrote on a leaf of its own. That one had given naught, though it was carved as finely as any. The sea brought it in weeks after the first.

The planks of the Second Tide bare two signs apiece, cut close, as names are cut close on an old lintel. On the shingle {{Halyna}} learned to hold two at once, and there were gaps in every telling. I have not mended them with guesses. Till that night no one had gone down to ask the fifty-three for more.

The night was short and the rows were long, so they began at the two ends, Rhyna at the stair and Halvard at the far wall. Never ere then had I seen them work apart. They were not far apart: the length of the vault.

Rhyna took up the first plank of all, the wave that had said *shore*, with its one long edge split raw. Halvard took up the silent one, and its long edge was raw also.

I stood between them with the lamp. No wind cometh so far below, and the flame stood up straight in mine hand.

Rhyna lifted her plank. At the far wall Halvard lifted his. So rise the far knots of a net with the near, for it is all one net.

Their two faces changed in the same moment.

Then Rhyna spake, and it was not a word. It was a whole order.

All night {{Halyna}} went along the rows toward each other, and I went between and wrote. By the middle rows mine hand ached to the wrist, and I stopped not. Here are the others, as I wrote them:

In the summers ere Aelrhen came home, the sky was thin along the edge of the wood. Gone were the pillars at the edge, in long gaps, like teeth from a jaw. Through the gaps the breath ran out. The between-light went hard and white.

Frailer every spring came the newborns of the long-lived. Some did not wake. The eldest dwindled ere their years were full.

The elders counselled the patience of the trees. "The iron cometh seldom now," said they. "The pillars have put up naught. They are waiting. Wait with them, and when the iron stoppeth, they will grow the sky back."

Of the very wound for which they counselled patience, the elders were dying. They wist it, and counselled patience still.

Then came the Aelvaren home by the channels, burned to the keel, with the bones of Aelrhen in its ribs.

### V.5 · The Tides, as the Wall Counted Them

*At each Tide's end, for six winters.*

*Laid by Kael the Counter, that would tell no Tide till it was over. In the sixth winter he bade me mend all six, as I had mended his first; I have mended them less.*

Give me the stone. No, I will stand.

**I · The Hasty Tide**

I count. That is what I am for.

Eight sails the first morning, and not one kept station with another. Each struck the first wall it touched till it sank.

No man stood on the decks. Off the transports the root-men came like spilled kindling. Treelings the wall called them, as if a name could make them small.

By noon their fire slackened, and we learned to say, "The fleet hath spent its fire."

A breach on the south face fell outward, as the slates of the black chest said, and all behind it lived. I swear to that.

All that winter I said at this fire that the grey sails had picked the wrong fortress. Every night it went round the benches with the cup, and every man drank to it. We laughed. God forgive us, we laughed.

Jory of Eldhythe, that whistled on the east wall, laughed the loudest. His name is first under the east cairns.

A fire-ship took him. That night the Captain cut a hand's breadth from his half of the cloak, and bound it on a pike over his cairn.

It was the first strip.

**II · The Second Tide**

The second count, and a harder.

In pairs they came, the front to take the shot and the back the ground.

Off the north point a hull was struck. A mile south, another's sail went grey-green to white, like a man's face, and it turned.

It had not seen. It had felt.

Then came the crown-hulls, and their winged seed, to wherever our guns were quiet.

When we sank a bearer, another took up the standard, and the fleet took on the new bearer's temper. To the young gunners I say it yet: choose which heart ye leave them.

Corlen of the harbour, eldest of the Twelve, held a wet finger to the wind before every holding. "Wait," he would say. "The sea hath not finished."

In a seed-fall a hull came slow on his water. By his own judgement he held his guns quiet, to let it close. He had eyes for the water, and for naught behind him.

His were.

He died at the parapet, watching the water, and three of his crew with him. That night the Captain cut the second strip.

Corlen did what the Captain would have done. That is the whole of a True Man. It is also what killed him.

Voss took his battery, and was raised a True Man. At the next holding he wet a finger, as Corlen did. The Captain at his shoulder looked up, and not out to sea.

"Change your shot."

**III · The Tide of Remembering**

Ye knew Wick. He ran everywhere, even to this hearth, and sat at the front where the children sit, his knees to his chin. The wall-walk he could run in the time it took to say the Title twice. Naught he wanted, save to run a word to the Captain.

By night the Captain had the south gun moved forty yards. At grey morning Wick stood at the old gun-place, to watch the water. On the north wall Hesk's lamp burned yet.

One hull came, alone and slow, sounding. Not to the wall. To the place where the gun had been. It fired there, to see whether it would answer.

The shot took the parapet, and Wick fell with it.

I did not see it. Hale did. Wick gave him three words.

"They are looking."

His word came to the Captain, as he had wanted all the war.

Hale ran it.

That night the Captain cut Wick's strip. So a boy died of a gun that was not there.

We had thought them beasts, that learn by the stick. They look before they trust. Our walls they knew, and never once our Captain. After that we moved our guns by night, and it was a quieter war, and I liked it less.

**IV · The Joined Tide**

All the night before, the drums went in the grey, hull to hull. At first light the sails came on three roads at once, north and east and south. Three roads, one hour.

Lanner of the crags whistled for the wind, as crag-men do. The Captain stood at his shoulder, and was still. Lanner stopped whistling.

"Loose."

An hour after dawn Lanner's battery sank their bearer.

The fleet faltered not. At the hour the dead heart had carved, they turned in on the east road, into empty water, one sail pale and then the next, a breath apart. They kept a dead mind's hour. Only the bearer's own went wild.

Ere dusk I walked the wall to count. Enno of the guild I found in the east breach, and a mile on, in the north, Della.

**V · The Tide that Learned Deceit**

The fifth count, and I like it not.

From the first the long-lived guided the wood, and the wood breathed out the sky they lived by: one life, in two kinds. A ship is but a tree led far.

To the trees that were to be hulls the long-lived sang. The trees bent to the singing, for naught had ever been asked of them but song. Hulls they sang from the breathing trunks, keels from the roots that fed them, masts from saplings trained by many summers of song in the wind.

Of the fine roots, that find their way in the dark, they sang the warriors of the landings, for none of the long-lived could be spared. What those roots touched, the wood knew. A tree we were, and old, in the first groves. Many summers they sang to us, and we bent. Then the singing ceased.

The blade went into our heart.

In the wound they cut the orders. Through the bond of roots we carved them on into every hull about us, and every hull obeyed. The wood obeyeth the wood.

The long-lived made that bond into a leash.

In the night, while ye laid your stone, we carved the morrow. Ye and we were both at work in the dark.

Yet the carving said what, and not whether. Each hull kept its own grain, and its own dread. One might balk at burning water, another turn savage where it bled: one mind, and many.

In grief were the bearers chosen, and we were chosen so. Nine grains there were, and each bare its grief its own way, save the green, that was too young to have a way.

Of the rest, some come again heavier where they failed, and some turn from the first failure. Some go where they lost the most, and some never turn aside. Some play no trick twice. Some wait, and burn, and wait again. Some throw all they have at once.

And some know you best, and bend least.

As many years as a wood had when it was chosen, so much could it hold, and the older it was, the later it came ready. Against that law no council can carve.

So the crudest came first. The council would have the shore silent soon. Green wood is quickest grown: one season, angry hands, one sign to a hull. At the edge of your sea it lay, while your children had children.

Your boats passed it in the breath, and saw naught.

Naught but song had ever been asked of those trees. Now one word was asked of them, and in your havens the word was *Burn*.

The havens had no walls for war. The last of that green wood followed you into the mountains. There, at your wall, for the first time, the shore struck back.

Through many summers the growing went on, longer than a shore-man's life. Each growing after the first went out as tides go, at the leaf-fall, when its ring was laid, and came out of the breath with the winter. Ralenthae was the tide before us. Aelthae is ours. Two more were growing behind us, and what is cut in them, we know not.

They that cried for war died ere their years, under the thin sky, and their own forgat them. At every growing the carvers were fewer, till none were left but scattered souls, tending the last groves.

We were sent. We are still sent. No voice among us can call us home.

Over us the groves sang this as we grew, and it is in our rings:

> *The green wood holds one word.*  
> *The wood of forty summers holds a word, and waits for its brother.*  
> *The wood of a shore-man's life holds a sentence, and a wall remembered in it.*  
> *The wood of a long life holds the morrow, and carves it, and waits, and carves again.*  
> *And the eldest of us, the pillars of the breath, the hearts of the grey, the anchors of the sky,*  
> *hold one thing only now, and it is not a word, and it is not a sentence, and it is not a morrow,*  
> *and we have carved it into every hull we sent you, and it is grief.*

*And no one answered.*

For the stem Tarnard took the two I had laid raw edge to raw edge: the one that said *shore*, and the silent one. {{Orvenna}} keyed them as stone is keyed. Penna held the last key, and Ormund struck it, once.

The broken grain met, and held.

That evening {{Halyna}} cut their mark across the join, the one setting the chisel and the other striking. Never ere then had a mark of ours gone into wood. I held the lamp, and it burned down a finger's width.

The wood took it.

Rhyna laid her palms on the cut, and Halvard his hands over hers.

The lashing of the bow Tarnard left for me. I tied it with Tarnel's knot, that he taught me on the Road, a knot to a mile. Long looked Tarnard at it. Then he said his son's saying, low:

"A net is only knots holding hands."

A day it rode off the point in a hard swell, and took no water. It was small, and it was sound.

At the evening tide came the whole hearth down, the old leaning on the young. {{Halyna}} laid the green sliver in the bow on its moss, against the keyed stem. Beside it they set a Stonwryt coin, part gold and part black stone: trust, sealed in stone. It was our dearest gift. Then they laid their two chisels by it, and in the bow the chisels took the last of the light.

"for we two have cut our last mark."

No one on the shingle spake. {{Halyna}} kneeled on the wet stones before the bow, and behind them the whole hearth kneeled. The soldiers laid down their pikes. Kael the Counter, that will not sit to tell a tale, kneeled with the rest. Last in the line, with his hood up, kneeled the Captain.

There he sitteth now, at the end of the bench. The wet of the shingle is dark yet on his knees.

From the front {{Halyna}} said:

<!-- HALYNA -->

"So far can one people go alone: the gift, and the kneeling."

"The rest needeth the other."

A wave came up the shingle and wet the keel. Then rose Harl, and took the bow in his two arms, and walked it down into the tide. The fisher-children pushed it off. Foremost, to her waist in the cold sea, was the girl that had asked whither it would go.

Well out on the ebb, the boat turned. Into the wind it went, and against the current, and into the grey.

Along the shingle stood the old ship-masters, and Merrick first. Six winters had they turned their faces from the sea when a hull burned.

They did not look away.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*And no one answered.*

When we three took our hands from the stone, the hearth was silent a long while. The fire burned down, and none rose to lay wood on it. We waited, I think, the width of one old voice. Ebba of Fenholm is under the east cairns.

Then a child near the front spake, not loudly. It was the girl that went first into the sea. Old Ebba was her great-grandmother.

"We remember."

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

## EPILOGUE

*What the grey sent back.*

### The Stone out of the Grey

*The fourth year after the homecomings.*

*Set down on the leaf that faceth Kael's torn cloak. From the first night that leaf stood empty. Later I drew the grain on it, and one line in mine hand:* Untold. {{Halyna}} cannot yet hold this deep. *In the end no knowing of {{Halyna}}'s filled it.*

Hold the stone, Seren.

I write this alone, in one ink, for there is none now to know the wood for me. Dry on my table stand their two inks.

{{Halyna}} are gone.

In the seventh winter they went in one night, as the Bonded go: Rhyna first, in her sleep, and Halvard ere the lamp was out, for he knew. They lie under the new capstone of the inner gate, in one grave, with one stone, and one name on it. Face down above them are their mark, their vow and the Title, where no eye will see them again till the wall fall.

*At the grave sang {{Orvenna}}, Ormund to the comma and Penna the rest:*

> *Stone upon stone, and the stone keeps faith;*  
> *hand over hand, and the hand holds true;*  
> *two at the gate when the grey sails gather,*  
> *two at the gate, and the gate stands through.*

{{Idrenna}}, that were old when {{Halyna}} were young, have outlived them both. In the year of the homecomings, ere their chisels went into the grey, {{Halyna}} cut Halvard's name under his fathers' in the western stair. Beneath it the stone is smooth, and none of his line is left to cut it.

That night the Captain cut a hand's breadth from his half of the cloak, and bound it on a pike over the gate. It was the last of his half. Into that winter went he in a plain grey cloak.

I went not with {{Halyna}}. Alone, they told me once, I could go where they could not, and carry this Book on. Now I understand the Covenant. God taketh not the one without leaving the other a work to do.

This is mine.

The night I laid my first tale, I was afraid to speak, and {{Halyna}} laid my two hands on the stone.

"Hold the stone."

"Hold it, and it will hold thee."

There is none now to finish it, and I say both halves alone.

In the seventh spring, on a morning of thin rain, out of the grey came a small grey boat. It ran itself up the shingle, and no one was in it.

The old ship-masters stood at the head of the shingle, and came no further. The children went down in a crowd. The first laid both her hands flat on the bow. A child had answered the green wood, when none of us could.

It was the same child.

Then down came Merrick, slowly, that had turned his face from every hull that burned.

Merrick laid his hand on its gunwale.

Last went I down, and the children parted before me, as water before a keel. By its stem I knew the boat: the first heart keyed, and {{Halyna}}'s mark across the join.

The mark of {{Halyna}} across the stem of the boat come home: the same mark that was cut there at the sending

It had gone home, and it had come home. In its bow, where {{Halyna}} laid the green sliver and their chisels, lay a stone.

It was not stone. Or it was stone that had been wood, like the Guest's gift. Myststone the Rivenmen call it, and say it remembereth all.

Stone does not translate. But this was never only stone. The hand that cut it learned the chisel late, and learned it well: young by their reckoning, I think, and seasoned by ours.

The rain had stopped. Grey as slate lay the sea, and the tide drew back with a long sound.

I knew the bite.

Twice had I held the lamp for that chisel, under the new capstone and across this stem.

It was cut in our own letters. I laid not my palms on it. It was cut so that one could read it alone. I read it out to the children.

The letters are read at once, below. The grain is told only by this, added beneath the round in the wood's words:

*In the memory of our home, our families...*

There the cutting broke off, and its last stroke is sharp yet. Our cloak he never saw, for he never sailed. Our letters he learned. The Last Carver I call him, for I think that when he cut this there was no other.

One word is in it that our Title never had: *the*, set before *memory*. I read it as it was cut, and no child laughed. I have wondered whether it stoppeth there because his hand stopped.

No one came with it, and perhaps that is wise. We have not yet earned a guest.

Ere that spring was out the staff was gone from the gatehouse, and no one saw the Captain take it. Where it leaned there is a clean stroke in the dust of seven winters. If you doubt the tale, go and look at the dust. The work is done. I know *the stair that goes no more*.

I have not gone down to look.

In the archive chest I keep this Book, among the mute stones, by the Guest's gift and this Stone. Grief is an inheritance, and it is paid out to the children. Over the one grave the torn cloak flieth yet. The sea hath not finished.

What we will send back, I know not. I know only that it must be sent kneeling.

Every night is the inner gate left open, as a guest would find it, and on the north wall a child keepeth a light.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

*And the Captain put his hood back.*

## APPENDIX

*Every carving the wood gave us, under its root.*

### The Book of Knowings

*Through the war, and closed four years after.*

*Set down at the back of this Book, one carving at a time, as {{Halyna}} knew each from the wreck-wood and the Thrones. It is the fleet's own half of the Book, for the wood cut every carving in it, and we cut none.*

Every carving the wood gave us began with one stroke, and that first stroke is its root, as the first course is the root of a wall. Of roots there are ten.

Their shapes I learned by eye, at the tide-line and in the vault, long ere I could make out anything else in the grain. The names are mine own, for a shape without a name slippeth out of the hand like a wet stone. Each I said three times ere I wrote it, and each held.

The young wood cut the root and naught more. Each Tide after cut the same root again, and added to it, as trees lay ring on ring. So the eldest carvings are but the youngest grown old.

From the wall, in the late Tides, I could tell a root in the time a wreck taketh to roll over. More than the root I knew never, for the rest the wood giveth only to the Bonded, and it must be known, not read.

Here are but the heads, from the youngest wood to the eldest. The parts, who waited on whom and when the heart meant to turn, I have written in their places. The seven of the young wood bear the soldiers' names, worn smooth with handling, like the haft of an old mallet.

One sign more came up the mountain in a mason's pack, out of Emberhythe: *Burn*. It was cut in the Fall, and came never against our wall. In the year of the homecomings the mason laid it down on the black crust where the Burning went out. I count it not among these.

Seven carvings came of the young wood, eight of each of the three Tides after it, and seven of the Tide that Learned Deceit. The Last Tide carved no new head, but whole morrows out of heads already cut, and at the end of each a turning for home.

The Thrones gave eight Judgements of their own. Two Thrones carried an older carving whole, for the Wave and the Lone Stroke are the roots in which every other is written. Forty-six there are in all. Every one have I set down, and not one of them did I know.

**The Last Note · Three Stones over the Hearth**

*Laid last of all the leaves.*

Hold the stone, Seren.

On the first night of this winter a child at the front asked what they say, the three grey stones over this hearth. In the havens the answer was ever that they said naught, and so was I answered when I was small. I said it not. I said that I wist not.

Every night since, when the hall was empty, I have held the lamp to the lowest stone. No meaning sought I there, but the sounds only, for stone does not translate. The Mystaeri cut our words among their letters by the sound, as near as their letters would go. And three words of ours I had from Brenn.

Last night the hall was empty, and the fire was low, and the lamp burned down a finger's width while I looked.

Then I had them, the three together on the lowest stone, the sounds close and the symbols wrong.

*Stop… Sky… Dying…*

At the feet of the black pillars they were cut, and carried off with the timber, and sold. Three generations they hung over a harbour-house fire at Eldhythe. Under them the shore laughed at three axemen, home on a spar. On the lowest the ship-masters knocked out their pipes. And no one read them.

Here no pipe is knocked on them. I think I have read them. I say I think.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*
