## OF THIS BOOK

*Written by Seren Two-Inks, apprentice to Halyna and the third of their hearth, at the front of this Book, when the Book was already half full.*

This is the Book of the Riven Stone. It holds the tales that are laid at the Long Hearth of Rivenkeep, in the hall behind the inner gate, under the wall where the torn cloak flies. They are laid in the hours after an assault, when the guns are cooling and the mortar has not yet set, and there is nothing left to do with the hands but hold them to the fire.

We did not make the custom. The first builders of the Keep made it, and we forgot it, and found it again at the bottom of a chest in the deepest vault, as you will read. When their stone was set and their tools were cleaned, the first builders sat at this hearth, and each night one of them laid a tale. We do as they did. Our wall is rising again, and so is this Book.

Beside the hearth, in a niche of the hearth-wall, lies the old capstone of the inner gate. It fell in the forgotten years and lay in the gateway until Halyna turned it over. Halyna are the elders of the guild at this Keep. They are of the Aetherbonded, whom the guild calls simply the Bonded, and Halyna is the one name the two of them share; singly they are Halvard and Rhyna. Halvard's fathers cut that stone and Rhyna's forebears laid it, and the Stonwryt of the first builders is still sharp on its underside. Whoever holds it tells. When the tale is done the teller sets it back in its niche, and we say that the tale is *laid*; for a tale that is only told is gone by morning, and a tale that is laid is part of the wall.

When a tale of ours is laid, the hearth answers it, all together: *We remember.* The answer comes from the first words of the Captain's Title, and it is the only thing at this hearth that is always said the same way.

Not every tale in this Book is ours. Some are the wood's. They come from the wreck-wood that the tide brings in under our wall, and from the dark stone that lies in the chest I carried up the mountain, and from other wood besides. The wood is not read, for there are no words in it. Halyna touch it, and its tale is known to them whole, as a memory is known, the beginning and the middle and the end of it at once. One of them need only touch it, for what the one knows, the other knows. The knowing is not the hard thing. The telling is. Words can be turned, and a knowing cannot; so they tell it in the fewest and plainest words they can find, and set each word down like a marking-stone where the knowing lies. The wood's tales are not told with the stone. When a wood telling ends, the hearth does not answer, for it does not know how. I have written that silence down each time, as it was.

I do not know the wood myself. I am not of the Bonded: the guild calls me unbonded, and after I came up the mountain, Halyna took me into their hearth as its third. I laid my hand on the wood once, and let go, for what came into me was too great for me to hold. So I write down what Halyna tell. Every scribe of the guild before me was one of a bonded pair; why this Book is written by one who is not, Halyna tell in its own place, to the children.

Halyna would have the wood's tales in this Book. "A tale told from one side of a wall," one of them said, and the other finished, "is half a tale." So where one thing was seen from both sides, the two tellings are set facing each other, ours on the left leaf and the wood's on the right. Where the wood's leaf could not yet be told, I have left it blank, with only the grain showing. Whoever opens this Book may see the shape of what we did not yet understand, and wait with us.

When Halyna tell, they take the tale by turns, a paragraph each, for they know the tales as they know each other, and neither waits on the other. I have used two inks, one for each of them, and they would not have me mark which is whose. When they speak a single line between them, one beginning it and the other ending it, that is in two inks as well.

The Captain does not tell. He sits at the end of the bench with his hood up and hears his own deeds told, and says nothing, unless it is to say that it was not like that. On the wall and in the orders he is the Commander, for so the Rite named him. At this hearth he is the Captain still, as he would have it.

When we speak of our fathers we call them Shorelanders, for so they were, and we call ourselves *we*. The folk behind the grey we call the Mystaeri, the mist-folk. That is our word for them, and not theirs.

Where a carving was broken, or a knowing could not be told, I have said so, and have not mended it with guesses. Where the tales do not agree, I have kept both.

This Book is ink. Halyna mean it to be cut into the granite of this hall when there is peace enough to cut it. Until that day it is kept in the chest I carried, with the mute stones.

### I.1 · The Torn Cloak

*Laid by Sergeant Kael, Kael the Counter, oath-keeper of the True Men, on the first night this hearth asked for a tale. He would not let me change a word of it, and I have not.*

Give me the stone. No, I will stand. I have sat enough this winter.

I swear on the lintel of the western stair, where the old wardens cut their names, that I will tell this plain, for I have no other way of telling. I was on the gate that day. I am a counter of things and not a singer, and what I did not count I will not tell you.

The column came in at dusk, and I counted it as it came: old men, and women with bundles, and children asleep on their feet, and soldiers of six broken companies who no longer knew their officers. Two thousand and some hundreds. We had walked a month to reach a castle, and we reached a heap of stones. The outer gate hung from one hinge. The ward behind it was grass to the knee. Where the songs had put a great hall there was a roof fallen in, and snow on the hearth where you sit now.

Some shook. Some wept. Some fell and would not rise.

Then the crying began, and it was not one cry. Some cried that we should send to the grey sails and beg for terms, though no one had ever understood a word the grey sails said. Some cried for the high passes and the snow beyond them. Some cried that we should go down into the cellars of the Keep and bar the doors and wait, as if the sea would forget us. I had heard men break before. I had never heard a people break. It is a sound like ice going out on a river.

Now I will tell you what the Captain was, and first what he was not. He was no general, and the generals were slain. He was no politician, and the politicians were forgotten. He was no nobleman, and the nobles were scattered. He was no Stonewright, and the Stonewrights sat in the grass with their chisels in their laps and despaired. He was the captain of a small company, and we were his company. He had found us one by one, in garrisons and on roads, twelve of us in all, and bound us by nothing but trust. We were soldiers who kept our oaths, who built what we promised, who fought only when the cause was just. That is all a True Man is. I say it so you will know it is not much. It is not little, either.

He did not want to lead. I swear to that as well. All through the long retreat he waited for someone whose place it was to lead to take it up, and none did, for the leaders were dead. Do not mistake me. It was not that the greater men were gone. There was no greater man. I do not think his equal ever lived.

### I.2 · The Cry of the Bonded

*Laid by Seren Two-Inks, apprentice to Halyna and scribe of this Book, on the night after the first assault the Rivenmen held. She was a child of the guild on the day she tells of, and sat in the courtyard with the rest.*

"Hold the stone," Halyna told me when I was small and afraid to speak at this hearth. "Hold it, and it will hold you." So I hold it.

I was in the courtyard that day among the Stonewrights, for I had been called to the guild already, though I had no craft yet but carrying water. We sat in the old ward where it had gone to grass, some hundreds of us, masters and prentices, and the bonded pairs a little apart. I had walked the passes beside a boy of the guild, and I came into that courtyard alone. We had come out of ten havens. We had watched every one of them die, and we had walked away from each with our chisels in our packs, and not one of us had lifted a hand, for our creed forbade it. Build, do not destroy. Protect, do not attack. No Stonewright ever bore a weapon. Mortar and chisel were our only arms, and they had not been enough.

The faith that had sustained us through twelve generations was on us still, but it sat on us like a wet cloak. I think every elder in that courtyard was turning over the same hard thing and would not say it aloud: that walls built for peace must sometimes be defended with blood. And if that was so, whose blood? And whose hands?

Then the Captain came down from the wall. I had seen the cloak go up, and heard the soldiers shout, and did not know what it had to do with us. He walked into the middle of the courtyard. He was not tall. There was coal-black on his fingers. He looked at the elders, and at the broken gate behind them, and he asked one question. It is the only speech of his that anyone at this hearth can repeat:

"Your walls are falling. Will you build, or will you watch and die?"

No one answered him. I remember the wind in the grass.

Then Halyna stood up. You know them by that name now. The guild gave it to them when they were wed, as it gives one to every bonded pair, and by it they are called. The guild calls the Aetherbonded simply the Bonded, and Halyna are the Bonded of the Keep. I did not know them then, save as two elders who sat apart and spoke to no one, for they had no need. I will name them singly this once, for the tale needs it. She was Elderess Rhyna, Rhyna of the Inner Gate, whose bonded forebears had laid the capstone of that very gate in the age before the havens. He was Elder Halvard, Halvard Stone-Warden, of the quarry-wardens of the high ridge, whose fathers had cut that same stone. They were husband and wife, as the Bonded are, and had been one since the cradle. They looked up at the torn cloak, and they bowed to it in the same breath, for the Bonded do not wait on each other. Then their two chisels went up as one and took the last of the light.

The cry came out of them both, and it was one cry. She began it:

"The mortar cries out from the ground. Rise, Stonewrights! Lay stone upon stone. Stand every wall to the very end."

And he ended it, with his chisel beside hers:

"Rise! Rise! Rise!"

Some tell it that she called and he answered, as though there were two cries and a silence between them. There was no silence. It was one cry in two throats, and hers came first only because a cry must begin somewhere.

Then the whole guild was on its feet, and I with them, though I do not remember standing. We took up the shout, and the soldiers on the wall took it from us, until the walls of Rivenkeep itself seemed to tremble. Old stone does not tremble. I felt it do so under my feet.

The terms were made that night in the ruin of this hall, and Halyna made them. The Stonewrights would not take up arms. They never had and never would, and the Captain did not ask it. But they would build between the volleys, and mend a breach while the guns still spoke, with the speed and precision that only Aetherbonded pairs could achieve, for a bonded pair works as one body with four hands. One of them said the first half to the Captain and the other the rest, and I could not tell you which. It has been the guild's word ever since: "Not the sword, but the stone. Not the killing, but the keeping."

So the siege day was shared out, and this hearth has names for its hours. There is the laying, when the Stonewrights raise and mend. There is the hauling, when the guns are dragged to where the Captain wants them. There is the holding, when the grey sails come in and the wall must stand, and every battery fights under one of the Captain's True Men. Every gun that is hauled is a course of stone not laid, for it is the same hands on the ropes; and the Captain knows it, and moves a gun as a careful man spends silver. And wherever you see a Stonewright on the wall in the holding, look again. You will see two.

We sang that first night by torchlight in the gap of the inner gate as we laid, one voice to the comma and the other the rest, as Halyna taught us:

> *Stone upon stone, and the stone keeps faith;*  
> *hand over hand, and the hand holds true;*  
> *two at the gate when the grey sails gather,*  
> *two at the gate, and the gate stands through.*

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

### I.3 · The Captain's Long Breath

*Laid by Private Hale, Hale the runner, who carries word of what is seen along the wall in the holding, in the first year of the siege. It is a saying of the hearth, and he tells where it came from; Kael the Counter mends it at the end, as he mends most things.*

Give me the stone, then. I am not a teller. I run.

There is a saying at this hearth that you will hear before you have been a month on the wall. I think it began with me, though Voss says it is older.

A day has four-and-twenty hours in it, siege or no siege. In the laying they go slowly, for stone is heavy. In the hauling they go slowly, for guns are heavier. But in the holding, when the grey sails come in, an hour goes by in a heartbeat, and a whole day in four-and-twenty of them, and a man cannot hold one hour still long enough to think in it. So it was in my first holding. The sails came out of the grey faster than I could count them, and the guns spoke, and the smoke came down, and afterward I could not have told you whether an hour had gone or a morning.

Every gun on this wall stands where the Captain set it, and every battery has one of his True Men over it. Before the sails come, the Captain tells each True Man his mind: where to watch, what shot to lay, and when to hold. In the holding each True Man fights his battery by his own judgment, for they know the Captain's mind; that is why each battery fights on its own. The Captain does not send his word along the wall. When he must, he calls it himself, by the horn, to one battery at a time. There are three calls, and every True Man knows them. *Shift your ground*: that is the water a battery watches, and not the stones it stands on. *Change your shot*: for the water, or the shore, or the sky. And *hold, or loose*. A runner carries none of these. What I carry is what is seen.

In those first days there was only one battery on the wall, and it was Kael's. That day Kael sent me with what his lookout had seen: a hull that had hung back in the grey, where none had hung before, and was coming in now, late, when the rest were spent. I ran it along the wall-walk with my head down, and came to the Captain.

He was standing still. That is what I remember first. Everything on that wall was moving but him. He had one hand on the parapet above the gate and the horn in the other, and he was watching one hull among a dozen, the grey-sailed thing coming in late on the tide, and he did not look round at me. He heard me out. Then he lifted the horn.

And the hour began to stretch. It did not stop short, as a cart stops against a stone; it slowed, as a cart slows on a long hill, until it hardly moved at all. I have no better way to say it. I stood at his shoulder and the hour went long around him, and I could see the water sheet off that hull's bow, and I could count the ripples going down its sail, one and two and three, as you count the rings on a cut log; and each of them, I think, was a minute. I had time to be afraid. Then I had time to be ashamed of it. Then I had time to stop.

Then he gave Kael's battery its call, and the horn carried it, and the hour let go of us slowly, as a held breath goes out, until the world was back at its own pace. The guns went. The hull broke up in the one stretch of that water where it could hurt no one, where he had meant it to.

So the men say: *For the Captain, an hour lasts as long as a day.*

They say it as though it were a gift the Stonewrights laid on him. I said it so myself, the first winter. I know better now. It is not that he is quick. It is that he will not be hurried.

Kael said it better, from across the fire, and I give it to you as he said it: "It is not the world that slows, lad. It is the Captain who will not hurry. The moment he chose was the moment that came."

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

### I.4 · The Rite of the Cornerstone

*Laid by Halyna, the Bonded of the Keep, in the first spring of the siege, and set in two inks, for they tell it by turns, a paragraph each, as all their tellings are told. They speak of themselves by name and from far off, as the annals speak of the dead; when I asked them why, one said that a tale is not a boast, and the other said that a wall does not know whose hands laid it.*

<!-- HALYNA -->

We hold the stone together, as we hold all things. Hear how the Rite of the Cornerstone was found again, and what was done with it.

Beneath this hall there are vaults, and beneath those vaults are others, cut down into the living ridge before ever a haven was raised on the coast; and the deepest of them no Stonewright had entered in the memory of the guild. On the third night after the cry, Rhyna of the Inner Gate rose from her blanket, and Halvard Stone-Warden rose in the same moment and took up the lamp. They went down stair after stair, until the stairs ended at the lip of a shaft where the first builders had let their stone down on ropes. She knew where to look, and he went down, and she paid out the rope, and neither had said a word. What she knew and what he found were one thought, as their thoughts are.

At the foot of the shaft lay a chest of black stone, with the mark of the first builders cut in its lid. In it were the texts of the Rite of the Cornerstone, cut small on thin leaves of slate. It was the most ancient ceremony of the guild, the rite by which the first Stonewrights had consecrated the leaders of Rivenkeep to build Rivenkeep itself.

Halyna read the leaves by lamplight in the vault, and what they read troubled them. For the texts held the secrets of building for battle: walls sloped back to turn a shot, gates set crooked so that no ram could run at them straight, courses laid so that a breach would fall outward into the ditch and not inward on the men behind it. The first builders of the Keep had known war. For twelve generations the guild had taught that walls were raised for peace and weather, and had believed it, and had been proud. The first fathers of the guild had known better, and we had forgotten them.

The new capstone of the inner gate lay in the ward, cut and dressed and not yet raised, and over it the Rite was done, before all who had come up the mountain, in the shadow of the Title where it flew above the gate. The Captain stood where the leaves said the overseer of the work must stand, with his hand on the new stone and his face to the people. He did not like it. He stood there.

There were three oils, as the Rite requires, and Halyna held each flask together and tipped it with both their hands. One named the oil and the other its meaning, for so the Rite is spoken. Cedar, for endurance. Granite-bloom, for strength. Myrrh, for the burden of command.

Then they laid in his hand the staff of the guild: a rod of polished wood capped with the finest polished ashlar, borne by every overseer of a great Stonewright work since the founding. Four hands laid it in two. By it he was named Commander of Rivenkeep.

When the people had gone to their fires, Halyna knelt by the new capstone and cut a Stonwryt into its underside for his work, which was the wall, and the war. The one set the chisel and the other struck. It was the first Stonwryt cut in Rivenkeep since the founding, and the first the guild ever cut for a soldier. The stone was raised into the gate that night.

He did not carry the staff into battle. He did not wear the oils beyond that day. He led as he had always led, from the wall, beside his True Men, with mortar upon his hands and a soldier's plain cloak upon his back. The staff leans in the corner of the gatehouse where he left it that night. No one has moved it. If you doubt the tale, go and look at the dust on it.

One leaf more lay in the chest, at the very bottom, and Halyna did not find it until the Rite was done. It was in a smaller hand than the rest. It told how the first builders of the Keep ended their day: that when the stone was set and the tools were cleaned, they sat at the hearth, and one of them laid a tale, as a course is laid upon a wall, one course each night, so that the wall and the telling should rise together. So this hearth is older than the havens. We did not make its custom. We forgot it, and found it again at the bottom of a chest.

The people called him Commander. In his own heart he was still only the Captain.

This we lay as it was laid for us.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

### I.5 · The Naming of the Rivenmen

*Laid by Corporal Voss, Voss who asked, on the eve of the going-down to the havens. He asked his question in the first winter of the siege, and waited the whole war to answer it.*

Give me the stone. I have owed this hearth an answer a long while.

In the first winter I asked a question at this fire, and some of you were here to hear it. I asked why we fight for ice and stone. I was cold, and I had buried a friend under a cairn on the east face because the ground would not take a spade, and I meant it as a hard question. A mason told me it was not the ice he was fighting for. That was a good answer, but it was his. This one is mine, and it has taken me the whole war to cut it.

It was the week after the Rite of the Cornerstone. The Captain of the Torn Cloak had held the staff for a day and set it down, as you know. In that week Halyna called the whole Keep into the ward, soldiers and Stonewrights and the people of the column together, and they stood beneath the torn cloak, and one of them said, and the other finished:

"The Commander fought."

"The Stonewrights built."

That was all the speech there was. It was enough. We looked at one another then, soldier and mason, and saw that we were not two peoples sharing a ruin. We had become one, as two courses become one wall when the mortar takes. A people was remade in that ward, out of the fighting and the building both, and neither half would have stood without the other.

So we gave the name of the Keep to the land. Rivenkeep, we said: all of it, from the high ridge to the farthest fallen harbour. Every haven the grey sails hold is Rivenkeep. Every road between them is Rivenkeep, and the edge of the sea is the edge of the Keep. I say it once so, and you will understand it ever after. When we say Rivenkeep we mean this hold on its ridge, and we mean the whole of our land, and that is not two names tangled into one. It is a vow.

And we named ourselves the Rivenmen, the people of the riven stone, the keepers of the keep that would not fall. When we tell of our fathers we call them Shorelanders, for so they were, and we call ourselves we. That is the custom of this hearth, and now you know where it began.

Then every one of us laid a hand on the foot of the pike where the banner flies, one after another, all that day until dark, and swore on it to take back every stone. Not one stone that was not ours. But every one that was.

The eldest did not like the new name, and did not say so. You still hear them murmur "the Shorelands" at the fire when they think no one is listening. I asked an old woman once why she did it, and she said it was a name to return to, perhaps, when the last wall stood and the last Mystarch lay broken. I did not understand her then. I think I do now.

Here, then, is my answer, and you may weigh it. I said a thing once on the east wall that men still say back to me: "We do not fight because we are right. We fight because our children are behind these walls." It is true, and it is not the whole of it. We do not fight for ice and stone. We fight because every stone of it is Rivenkeep, and Rivenkeep is home, and we swore on the banner to bring it home. Go up the east tower on a clear morning and look along the coast. You will see the nearest of our keeps, and the smoke of the next beyond it; and past those, farther than any eye can reach, they stand one after another across all ten haven-lands, with a crimson pennant on every one. We raised each of them in this war, stone upon stone, and the grey sails have come against each of them, and each still stands. But a keep is not a place to stay in forever. It is a place to go out from. In every one of the ten havens a Mystarch sits now, one of the great Thrones of the grey, and waits.

Tomorrow we go down to the havens.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

## BOOK TWO · THE BOOK OF THE BUILDERS

*Who we were, and who they were.*

### II.1 · Of the Keep That Was Forgotten

*Laid by Halyna in the manner of the old annals of the guild, in two inks, by turns, in the first summer of the siege. It is the stone leaf of the two homes, and its facing leaf belongs to the wood.*

<!-- HALYNA -->

Hear the annal of the Keep, as the guild kept it, and as we did not keep it.

In the age before the havens, when there was no city yet on the eastern coasts and no road between the mountains and the sea, the first Stonewrights came up onto the high ridges. They found a place where the ridge is riven in two, as though a great blade had come down on it, and in the cleft they raised a hold out of the ridge's own grey stone. This was Rivenkeep, the first work of the first builders. Eldhythe is called the Eldest Haven, and rightly; but it is the eldest only of the havens, for the Keep was old before the first stone of Eldhythe was cut.

The quarry-wardens of the high ridge cut its stone, and their names are cut in the lintel of the western stair, one beneath another, for as many generations as the quarry was worked. Halvard Stone-Warden is of their line. And Aldwena, a bonded pair of the first builders, laid the capstone of the inner gate with their own four hands, and Rhyna of the Inner Gate is of their line. So the gate you came under tonight was cut by his fathers and laid by her forebears, and it was two lines made one long before Halvard and Rhyna were laid in their one cradle.

Under that capstone, as under the foundation of every great work of the guild, they cut the Stonwryt. It is a vow sealed in living rock, earned by toil and skill: a master's promise that the wall will hold. It cannot be bought. It can only be earned, by the hands that laid the wall, and it is cut where no eye will see it again unless the wall falls.

In later days, when the havens were raised and grew rich, the Shorelanders named their coin for it. The Stonwryt coin was part gold and part black stone, and it was minted only by bonded master-pairs of the guild, in workshops no outsider had ever entered. So the markets of the havens stood on the same footing as their temples: trust, sealed in stone.

Then the folk went down. The ridges were cold and the harbours were warm, and trade came up the coasts and not over the mountains. The Stonewrights followed the folk, for the folk needed temples and walls. Generation by generation the Keep was left, until it was more myth than memory: the castle that had never fallen, a name in the songs, a place the children of the havens believed had never been.

No one kept it. No Stonewright climbed up to point the mortar or to clear the drains. The rain came into the joints, and the frost came after the rain, and every winter the ice split what the rain had loosened. It had never fallen. It had only been left.

When the havens fell one by one, the folk who lived fled up the mountain roads to the one place the songs said would hold. What they found was a shadow: broken gates, crumbled walls, a hall with its roof lying in its hearth. What man could not destroy, time had withered. Forgetting had done what no enemy ever could.

Halyna came under the inner gate at dusk, with the column at their backs, and found the capstone lying in the gateway. It had fallen in the forgotten years, and no one had heard it fall. It was whole. They knelt and turned it over together, for it is a stone that takes two, and on its underside was the Stonwryt of the first builders, as sharp as on the day it was cut, and beside it the bite of the quarry-wardens' chisels.

The stone kept its vow. It was we who did not keep ours.

Then they cut a new capstone for the gate, and when the Rite was done they raised it and set it in its place together. The old stone they carried into this hall and set in the niche of the hearth-wall. It is the stone you hold when you tell. Whoever holds it holds the vow of the first builders, and the shame of their children.

This we lay as it was laid for us.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

You called it the fog, the grey, the Mystlands. You called it a wall. It was the air we breathed.

Our land lay flat beneath the trees, measureless, to the far edge of the world. The trees breathed out the grey as sleepers breathe. It lay down among the trunks and fed them, and fed us, and we tended it as you tend a hearth.

The sun came through it softened, a gentler radiance: the between-light, neither day nor dusk. We were made for that light, and were never children of the dark. The open sun withers us, as it withers a fern of the deep forest thrown down in the desert. In the last years we saw it wither our children.

We guided the wood's growing. We pruned it, sang to it, led its roots to the water, and it grew where we led it and breathed out the grey that was our life. One life, in two kinds: we tended the wood, and the wood kept us, and it kept its own will besides.

The wood needed the breath, the breath needed the wood, and we needed both. Without the breath the wood sickens. Without the wood the breath does not come again. Without either we fail, slowly: the youngest first, the eldest after.

We live twice as long as you and half again, and we were old in the world by more lives of yours than you have names for.

Nor was the wood one wood. The twisted boughs we bent in our rites. The pale saplings we sang to, and they answered; a stand of them at evening was a choir. Under the silver elders the council sat, for no one lies under a silverbark.

At the very edge, where the grey came down to your sea, the black pillars stood in their long ranks and held the breath to the earth, as a mast holds up a sail. You call them the Mystholders. The wood was our tongue of truth. We speak as the storm speaks in the high boughs, in cracks and breaks, and each deep of the forest speaks it its own way. But what is carved in living wood is not heard. It is known. Whoever lays a hand on the carving knows what the carver meant, whatever tongue the carver spoke, so that no word could be twisted and no promise misheard.

We say *Ael'thar* with the break in it, as a branch breaks, and carve Aelthar whole and soft, and the two are one word: the thunder is for the moment, the softness for always. Anything sacred we gave to the wood, and anything binding, and anything that must not be mistaken. We did not know there was any other way to be believed.

From the edge we watched your shore. We saw your havens raised, and the lamps lit in them at dusk. We saw your boats come a little way into the grey and turn back with white faces. We did not know why.

We hid so long and so well that when your axes came, no one in all your havens knew there was anyone behind the grey to wound. We had listened to you since your first haven. You had never once heard us. We had made sure of it.

This is our home: the flat wood under the grey, the between-light, the pale saplings singing at evening, the silverbarks at council, the black pillars at the edge of the world with the breath held up between them. If any green is left of us, it remembers this; and the grain holds it still.

**Rhyna.** Come closer, all of you, and sit where you can see. This is a tale for the young, and the old may listen if they are quiet.

**Halvard.** Not all Shorelanders were Stonewrights, yet all Stonewrights were Shorelanders. That is the first thing, and you must hold on to it. The guild was never a house you were born into. Children were called to it in their cradles, from any household in the land: most from Stonewright blood, it is true, but some from fishers' huts and some from merchants' halls, and once, the annals say, from a gaoler's house in Carnhold. It was a great honour to be called, and no mother or father in all the annals of the guild ever refused it. For the Stonewrights kept the faith of the Shorelands as they kept its walls, and taught it in the temples they raised, and a child called to them was called to both.

**Rhyna.** The seers of the guild went among the cradles and read the children. The Stonewrights held, and hold still, that God had joined each pair before either drew breath, and that the seers did not choose, but only read what was already written. When a seer had read two names together, the two children were laid in one cradle, and both families raised them, turn and turn about, in one house and then the other. The guild calls that joining the Aetherbond, and the two it joins the Aetherbonded, or simply the Bonded; and so shall we. So it was with Halvard and Rhyna, who have never in their lives slept a night under different roofs. And mark this, for you will need it later: the bond is ordained in the cradle, but it is made whole only at the sealing.

**Halvard.** When the bonded children were old enough to hold a chisel, they were given to the Theoliths. The Theoliths were bonded pairs themselves, great masters of the guild, called away from their works for a season to teach. They taught the children to cut and to lay and to set, and to pray, and to keep the count of the stones. And they taught them the vow. The Theoliths named the vow the Covenant of Completion, and they would say no more of it to children, and neither will we. You will understand it when it is yours to understand. Our own Theoliths were Idrenna, who are sitting by the door tonight, and will nod when we tell it right.

**Rhyna.** When bonded children come of age they are wed, and the wedding says aloud only what the cradle said first. The guild calls it the sealing, and from that day the bond is whole. At the sealing the guild gives the two of them one name, made of both, as a gate is made of two posts and the stone laid across them; and by that name they are called ever after, though the names of the cradle are kept. In that way Halvard and Rhyna were sealed, and named Halyna, and by that name you know us. So Halvard is Rhyna's husband and Rhyna is Halvard's wife, and they were one pair long before they were either.

**Halvard.** The Bonded lived as one, and worked as one, and worshipped as one, and they did it without words. You have seen Halyna on the wall in the holding. You have seen that neither calls to the other. The work of such a pair is not doubled but multiplied twelvefold, as the old masters counted it, and every great wall and soaring temple of the ten havens was the work of such pairs. The guild calls the man of a pair Elder and the woman Elderess, and the two names are one rank, for the Elderess stood equal in all things. So he is Elder Halvard and she is Elderess Rhyna, and neither is the elder of the other. There was never a stone she laid second.

**Rhyna.** What the one feels, the other feels. If Rhyna cuts her hand in the south tower, Halvard feels it on the north wall, and knows which hand. What the one thinks, the other has thought already. Two made one: that is the whole of the bond. The old masters had a saying for it, and you will hear it again before these tales are done. Harm to one is harm to both.

**Halvard.** The Bonded are seldom apart, and never far. No bond was ever broken. It is not in the annals, not once, in all the generations of the guild. Separation, God did not permit.

**Rhyna.** And so the Bonded die together. That is the deepest thing we will tell you tonight, and we tell it plainly, for it is not a sad thing to us. When one of the Bonded is killed, by a fall or a fever or the sea, the other does not live long after. There is no wound on the one who is left, and no sickness. The heart breaks, for it knows; and the body stills, for its work is done; and they die in peace. When the left hand dies, does not the right one die too? Eight days, men say, is the longest that ever one lived on alone; but the Stonewrights say it was hours, and they should know, for they keep the count. Many have drawn their last breath in the same moment, a mile apart, and each knew the other was going, and went too.

The guild calls such a child unbonded. It is rare, and since the Fall it is less rare, for the long road killed children. By custom an unbonded child is taken into the hearth of a bonded pair as its third, and is theirs. ### III.1 · The Book of the Havens

*Laid by Halyna in two parts, by turns, a haven each, as a catalogue is laid: Of Their Raising, in the first autumn of the siege, and Of Their Fall, in the winter after. It is the stone leaf of the havens; the wood's leaf faces it.*

**Part One · Of Their Raising**

Hear now the names of the havens, and hold them, for they are ours.

Along the eastern coasts the mountains and the high hills come tumbling down to meet the sea, and there our fathers lived. They were builders and not seafarers. They feared the open water and kept to the sheltered shores, and they paved vast roads like veins across the mountains to join the places they had built. They raised ten havens, which the old songs call the ten harbour-cities, and every wall of them was Stonewright work; though a haven among the Shorelanders was not always a harbour for ships, but any place of shelter the Stonewrights had walled. Hear them, from the eldest.

Eldhythe, the Eldest Haven, whose harbour burned in a day. The first masters to come down from the Keep laid its walls, and its harbour-houses were the richest in the land.

Tidesmeet, where the river meets the tide. It stood on both banks at once, and three great spans joined it across the water.

Fenholm, the town that was always sinking. It stood on pilings in the fen and went down a hand's breadth in every lifetime, and every generation laid a new course on the old.

Holtward, the keep under the canopy, in the deep woods of the south, roofed so close among the trees that a traveller could pass it by.

Carnhold, the stronghold of the cairns, cut into the mountain among the burial-cairns of the first quarrymen, and loud all day with its smithies.

Emberhythe, the forge on the burning shore, where the mountain smokes into the sea and the ground is warm underfoot in winter.

Sandreach, the citadel carved in the canyon wall, which was not built so much as dug, gallery below gallery into the red rock.

Rimewatch, the watch on the frozen mere, whose great hall stood out on the ice all winter, with lamps in its windows through the long dark.

Highreach, the tower of the floating stones, on crags so high that the masons lifted their courses on cranes, and the stones seemed to hang upon the wind.

And Glasspire, raised by seven generations of Stonewrights, that caught the sun like a blade of ice. It was the last and proudest of our works, and its great glass could be seen from a day's sail out, throwing the sun far into the grey.

Ten cities, ten harbours, ten kindreds who called themselves one nation.

This we lay as it was laid for us.

**Part Two · Of Their Fall**

Hear now how the havens were lost, and do not look away, for we did not look away in the telling.

Our fathers had no standing navy, no war fleet, no defensive doctrine. No enemy from beyond the sea had come against the Shorelands in all their generations. Their walls were built for ceremony and weather, not for bombardment, and their gates were wide, and stood open at night.

The first dark sails came out of the grey without warning. At dawn they fell upon the harbour of Eldhythe, and by sunset it was ash. The city behind the harbour held three months. No haven sent to another for help, for none had any help to send. Then Tidesmeet fell, and the people on the high road above it watched its three spans go down into the river one after another, and heard them go. Fenholm went under all at once, as if the fen had only been waiting. Holtward burned in its trees and Carnhold in its smithies. Emberhythe held five months on its burning shore. Sandreach was taken gallery by gallery, and Rimewatch on its ice, and Highreach on its crags. And Glasspire, that seven generations had raised, collapsed in a single night into its own cellars, and the dark beneath it lies open still.

No envoy came that anyone could understand. There was only the harsh thunder of a tongue born in ancient forests, knocking and knocking out of the grey; the soldiers on our wall call it the drums. And no one stood upon the decks. Our fathers looked for a captain on those ships and saw none, nor any crew, and they called it the ghost fleet. Who had sent it they did not know; and because it came out of the fog the sailors called the Mystlands, they named the senders the Mystaeri, the mist-folk. We use both names still.

The sails that took the havens were plainer than the sails that come against us now. They came each alone, and they needed no more, for there was nothing to stop them.

The Stonewrights did not fight. They simply walked; and we were among them.

One thing more is told of Glasspire's night, and we tell it as it was told to us. Some who fled the spire say a shadow was seen in the fog that night, greater than any hull, standing off beyond the harbour while the spire went down. Others who were there saw nothing. We do not know.

### IV.1 · Of the Felling of the Black Pillars

*Laid by Halyna in the Second Tide, in two inks, by turns, from the archive rolls of the guild, which came up the mountain in the Stonewrights' chest. It is the stone leaf of the harvest, and the wood's leaf faces it; where the rolls are stained I have said so.*

<!-- HALYNA -->

We read this from the rolls. We did not live it. Our fathers' fathers lived it, and wrote it down, and did not read what they had written.

In the oldest archives of the guild the fog is written down as a blackish veil at the edge of the sea, and after that only as a question, asked in the night-watches and never answered. What is the grey? What does it keep? Who breathes it? The masters who asked were long dead before any answer came, and the answer, when it came, was not written by us.

Then came the high summer of the Shorelanders' pride. The havens were rich and their roads were heavy with carts, and the counsel of the Stonewrights had come to seem a voice from humbler days, a thing the grandmothers said. In those years the ships of the coast went further out than they had ever dared, along the edge of the grey, and there they found the black pillars of the fog, which the ship-masters named the Mystholders, for they seemed to hold the grey up as posts hold up a roof. They stood in ranks at the fog's outermost edge, tall and dark-trunked and straight as a plumb-line, and their timber was harder than oak, lighter than iron, proof against rot.

For two generations the Trade Council sent out the axes. The rolls keep the count of it, cargo by cargo. They felled the pillars for the buttresses of fortresses and for bridge-beams. They put them into the three spans at Tidesmeet and the pilings of Fenholm, into the roof-trees of Holtward, into the cranes of Highreach that lifted the floating stones. And they put them into things that needed nothing of the kind. A lord of Eldhythe floored his dancing-hall with the black wood. The harbour-houses made their trade-tables of it, and their dice-boards. *[Here the roll is stained, and three lines are lost.]* A merchant of Tidesmeet had one pillar hewn into a single bed, and slept in it, and was proud.

The rolls keep a stranger count beside that one, and no one at the Council read it. Where a pillar was felled, the stump put up new growth, straight and black, and in a few summers a young pillar stood where the old one had, and the grey stood close about it again. The axemen went back for the young growth, for it was light and straight and made good poles, and the guild bought them for scaffold. They cut it, and it grew, and they cut it again, and it grew again, and they cut it again. Then there is a line in a clerk's small hand at the foot of a cargo-list, that the stumps had stopped putting up growth, all along the edge. No one wrote down why, and no one asked.

Season by season the fog thinned, like a dying enchantment. The ship-masters came home and said the grey stood further off than it had in their fathers' day. The Council named it fortune.

The Stonewrights warned them. It is in the rolls, and it is in our own hand: "These trees grow in strange soil. The carvings we find beside them bear a tongue we cannot read. Where wisdom is absent, let restraint be our guide." But trade had become king, and the Stonewrights were reckoned relics, and the warning was read aloud once in the Council hall at Eldhythe and laid on a shelf.

For there were carvings. At the feet of the black pillars the axemen found stones set upright, and on them were cut letters no one could read, fine and curling, as beautiful as frost on a window. They were warnings. We know that now. The Mystaeri had cut them in their own letters, and here and there among the letters they had tried to cut our own words by their sound, as near as their letters would go; the sounds were close, and the symbols wrong. They had set them there trusting the stone to carry the meaning as their wood carries it. But stone carries nothing. Stone does not translate. The stones came to the cities beautiful and mute. Some were locked in the archives of the guild, and it was we who turned the key. Others were sold as curiosities and hung on the walls of harbour-houses, holy pleas reduced to ornament, and men drank beneath them and did their reckoning.

So the Stonewrights warned, and then filed the warning away with the stones they could not read. That is our share of it. We will not lay it all on the Council.

The harvest ended at last, not by wisdom but by the fickleness of men. The pillars near the coast grew scarce and the voyages grew long, and cheaper timber came down from the northern hills, and the Council lost its taste for the black wood. So the axes stopped. No one decided it.

Then came the forgetting. The forebears had not known what they destroyed. Their children would not know. The Shorelanders of the final days remembered nothing at all. The stones hung on the walls of the harbour-houses, and the children grew up beneath them and asked what they said, and were told they said nothing.

Late, very late, the wheel of pride turned. The temples of the havens were mended. Men climbed the steps of the guild-halls again and asked the Stonewrights' counsel, and it was given. But humility came too late. The first dark sails were already on the sea.

This we lay as it was laid for us.

### IV.3 · The Warden's Door

*The confession of Brenn of the outpost, the man who picked up the stone, as a bonded pair of the guild at Eldhythe set it down from his mouth in his old age and sealed it in the archive chest with the mute stones. Halyna read it aloud at the hearth in the Tide of Remembering, by turns, a paragraph each, and laid the closing annal after it. The words before the annal are his, and I have not mended them. The wood's leaf faces it.*

<!-- HALYNA -->

We hold the stone, but these words are not ours. They are the words of Brenn, who served at the outermost outpost in his youth, and came to the temple at Eldhythe forty years after and asked the Stonewrights to hear him. We read them as they were written.

I was at the outpost at the end of the land, where the rock runs out into the sea toward the grey. I was nineteen. There were twelve of us and the warden.

It was a poor post, but it did not look poor. The warden had set the black wood everywhere: in the doorposts and the lintels, in the walls of his hall, in his table and his chair and the frame of his bed. It was the trappings of a rich man's vanity, and he was a rich man's son, and not much older than I was.

He held the post by his father's name on the Trade Council, not by his own. They had sent him to the edge because his temper had made him unwelcome in the cities, and at the edge there was no one for it to wound but us. He had never known battle. He had never known patience.

The stranger came in the morning, in a small grey boat, out of the grey. There was no wind, and the boat came anyway. He beached it on our shingle and walked up to the warden's door. He was tall and pale, clad in woven mist-cloth that moved when he did not. I saw no weapon on him.

He spoke first in his own tongue, sharp syllables that rolled like storm-wind through ancient branches. It frightened us, for it was loud, and we did not know that it was only how they speak. Then he said three words of ours. He said them carefully, like a man setting down something that might break.

"Stop… Sky… Dying…"

They were the right words, spoken truly. I know it now. But words without sentences are only sounds, and we heard only sounds.

Then he knelt on the step of the warden's door and set down before him a strange dark stone, the size of two fists. He set it down the way you would set down bread before a guest.

The warden kicked it aside. It went into the dust by the wall. And his men laughed. I laughed. I have not laughed at anything since.

The stranger rose. He put his right hand on the back of the warden's neck, gently, the way my mother did when I had a fever, and drew their heads together until their foreheads touched, in silence. The warden was so astonished that he let him. I think if it had stopped there, all our lives would have gone otherwise. Then the stranger took a little blade in his left hand and touched it to the warden's right arm.

The warden drew back in terror. He had never had a wound. Because he tore himself away, the cut that was meant to be shallow went deep, and the blood came, and he screamed. He screamed at us, "Seize him." We seized him. He screamed for the killing blow. His men obeyed.

We obeyed.

The stranger did not fight us. I think now there was another cut he meant to make, on his own arm. He never made it.

Afterward the warden had us lay the body in its boat and set the boat alight, so there would be nothing to show. The wood of that boat did not burn like wood. The fire leapt higher than any natural flame, as though the wood itself was screaming. We shoved it off with poles, and the tide took it out, and we could hear it on the water after we could no longer bear to look.

The warden had the clerk write to the Council that same day. I stood by while it was written, and I can say it by heart:

*Unknown intruder. Hostile intent. Neutralized. Vessel burned and released.*

No word of the gift. No word of the kneeling.

The warden wore long sleeves all the rest of his life, through the hot summers of Eldhythe, and I know why.

I picked the stone out of the dust by the wall the next morning, when no one was looking. I do not know why I did it. I have kept it forty years. I give it up now with these words, and I ask God to forgive me, for there is no one left to ask but God.

So far the words of Brenn. Now hear the annal.

The warden's name is not kept. The Stonewrights of Eldhythe cut it from the rolls, and it will be in no book of ours. On his right arm was the only mark the Kneeling Guest's rite ever made upon a Shoreland body: the half of a holy thing, cut off in the middle.

The pair who heard him sealed the dark stone and the confession in the archive chest of the guild, with the mute stones, and there they lay unread. When the havens fell and the guild walked up the mountain, the chest was handed to the youngest apprentice on the road, because it held nothing of worth. The youngest apprentice was Seren, who is writing this down. She carried it every step, and after the passes she carried it alone.

There is no older law among any people than this: that one who kneels at your door is under your roof. We were a people of doors.

This we lay as it was laid for us.

<!-- HALYNA -->

This is held in the grain.

We are the eldest wood. We were a tree when the world was green to its edges. We fell, and the ages lay down on us as leaves lie down on leaves, until time forgot us and we grew dark and hard. We are not stone, though you took us for it.

Wood that time has forgotten does not itself forget. It gives slowly, and only what is so. Even a sliver of it is the dearest gift a child of the long-lived can give. We were given once. This is how.

<!-- RING -->

One of the long-lived took the name Aelrhen when he took his errand, and it means bond-to-stone. Through a long life he listened across the grey to the shore-men at their nets and their walls, and gathered their words as a child gathers fallen leaves, one and one and one.

At the end he had three, and he knew them for the right ones. *Stop. Sky. Dying.* He knew no way to join them, for your tongue joins its words with small words that make no sound across water, and he never heard those.

But he had us. Whoever held us while a tree's shadow moves the breadth of its own trunk would know the whole of it. We were the sentence. He did not know that one of you alone holds only the shape of a knowing. The shape would have been enough.

He chose the outermost of your holds, for its doors and walls and benches were of our wood, and he took that for reverence. We knew it for what it was: our kin from the edge, sawn and polished. At the door he laid his hand on the post and greeted them, and they did not answer, for they were dead.

He knelt on the stones before the master of that house and set us down, as one who comes under a roof brings his best, and spoke the name of the rite in the thunder of his own tongue, *Ael'thar*, and after it his three words in yours.

This was the Aelthar as he meant to make it, the binding of gift and blood and kinship across any divide, the holiest rite of fellowship we have. He would rise and take the back of the host's neck gently in his right hand, where life goes up into thought, and draw their heads together until the foreheads touched in silence.

With his left hand he would open a shallow cut on the host's right arm, and let go the neck, and cut his own left arm, for he would ask no blood he did not give. Then the two would press their bleeding arms together, shoulder to shoulder: a promise of united front, that harm to one was harm to both.

He made the first step. A foot struck us into the dust by the wall, and the men of the house laughed. He did not stop, for the rite is not stopped once it is begun. He rose and took the neck as gently as one lifts a fledgling, and the foreheads met, and he was silent. Then with his left hand he opened the shallow cut.

And the host drew back. We do not know why. Fear leaves no mark on wood.

The blade that was meant to go a little way went deep, for the arm was moving. The host's blood came, and he cried out, and his men came. The Guest did not lift his hand against them, and the cut he would have made on his own arm was never made. They struck him down on the stones of the door, and he died there with the rite half made. We lay in the dust and heard all of it, and no one picked us up.

Of all the things that ever lay at a shore-man's feet and looked like stone, we were the one that could have spoken to him; and it was the one his foot found.

<!-- RING -->

In the morning a man of the house came back alone and picked us out of the dust, and put us away, and did not hold us. We lay most of his life in the dark with the sentence in us; then in a chest among the mute stones; then we were carried up into your mountains in the arms of a child. No one held us so long, until you. Here is the sentence, as it was brought to the door:

*Stop the felling of the pillars at the edge of the grey, for they are not timber. The sky over us is their breath, and ours, and when you cut them it comes open, and our children cannot live in the open sun. We are here, behind the grey, and we have been here always, and we are not your enemies. Stop, for the sky is dying, and we are dying under it.*

That is all he came to say. We have carried it a long way, and the grain holds it still.

*And no one answered.*

### IV.5 · The Burning Boat

*Laid by Corporal Voss, Voss who asked, in the Second Tide, as his grandmother told it in the harbour-houses of Eldhythe, where she kept the fire and drew the ale. It is a tale the ship-masters would not tell and a rhyme the children would not stop singing. The shore laughed at it, and the sea did not. The wood's leaf faces it.*

Give me the stone. This one is not mine. It is my grandmother's, and she had it from the sailors, and they had it from no one who could be trusted.

She kept the fire in a harbour-house on the quay at Eldhythe, the kind with a long table and a low roof, where the ship-masters came in out of the rain. Over the fire there were three flat grey stones set into the wall, with curling letters on them, fine as the tracks of birds on wet sand, and no one knew what they said. They had hung there since her own grandmother's day. The ship-masters knocked out their pipes on the lowest one.

Our fathers feared the open sea. They kept to the shores and to the sheltered roads between the havens, and when a ship went out far enough to see the fog-bank it came home quickly. The sailors called it the Mystlands. Now and then some bold soul went close, and turned back, and came into my grandmother's house with a wild story, and drank on it for a week. She believed none of them. She said the Mystlands made liars of honest men, and it was the only thing she ever said against the sea.

But there was one story the ship-masters would not tell, and she told it to me more than all the others, so I think she half believed it.

It came from the men of the outermost outpost, the one at the end of the land. They swore in their cups that they had once pushed a boat off the shingle burning, with a dead man in it, and that the tide took it out, and that when it was well out it turned. It turned into the wind and against the current, which no boat can do, and went away into the grey still burning, and screaming as it went. They swore to the scream above all. They said you could hear it after the fire was too far off to see.

The shore laughed at that, and my grandmother laughed with it, and knocked out her pipe on the grey stone over the fire. The ship-masters did not laugh. They would not speak of it, for it is ill luck anywhere near the sea, and I have seen a master set down his cup and go out into the rain rather than hear it told.

I have heard it since from three of them, at fires in the passes, as far from salt water as they ever came. Each had seen it himself, or sailed with a man who had, and their tellings were all the same: a small boat burning far out in the grey, where no fire should be, turning into the wind; and after it, weather. They say it brings the foul weather. They say it is the boat that comes for you when you die. The shore laughs, because the shore will never have to meet it. The ship-masters do not laugh. And the cities had it in writing that it was nothing: a stranger come armed, and put down, and his boat burned and set adrift. That was all the writing said, and who argues with writing?

But the children had a rhyme for it. I do not know who made it. We skipped to it on the quay with a rope, two turning and one jumping, and we thought the strange part was the greeting, for who ever heard of such a greeting as that:

> *Grey boat, grey boat, out of the grey,*  
> *knelt at the warden's door one day;*  
> *a stone for a present, a cut for a kiss,*  
> *and who ever heard of a greeting like this?*  
> *Burn it and shove it and give it the tide;*  
> *but the grey boat turned, and the grey boat cried.*

<!-- OPTIONAL (Jack's call, plan §14.6): the ship-masters' couplet, original, in the rhyme's metre. Cut from here to COUPLET END if declined. -->
There were two lines more. We did not sing those on the quay, for if a ship-master heard them he would take the rope out of our hands, and not say why:

> *Grey boat burning, and after, the rain;*  
> *and the one it comes for will not come home again.*
<!-- COUPLET END -->

I skipped to that rhyme at seven. I understood it at forty, on the shingle under this wall, the first time I heard one of their hulls burn. For it screamed. It screamed as it went, the way the outpost men had sworn, and the old sailors on the wall turned their faces from it; and I stood in the smoke with my mouth open and the rhyme going round in my head, and I thought of the three grey stones over my grandmother's fire, and the pipes knocking on them.

We forget. We have always been good at that. But Mystwood remembers.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

### IV.6 · The Voyage of the Aelvaren

*As the Mystwood remembers: known from the heart of a Standard-Bearer of the Tide of Remembering, for Halyna say every hull grown since holds this in some measure, and this heart held it clearest; it faces the Burning Boat. Halvard took it up in the vault while Rhyna was on the wall, and she knew it as he knew it; the soldiers beside her say she plunged both hands into the rain-butt, and would not say why. It was quickly told, for grief is the one thing in the wood for which the shore has words enough. Told by Halyna, by turns, in two inks; set down by Seren. Where the wood was broken, or the telling failed, it is marked, and not mended.*

<!-- HALYNA -->

This is held in the grain.

And not in this grain only. The hull that brings it to you was not there; but it knows, for it grew out of what we were.

We were the Aelvaren, the Bearer of the Binding, and proud of the name: a small boat of the old wood, grey and light. We carried the Guest through the thinning breath to your shore, and waited in the shallows below the door to carry him home with the rite made.

<!-- RING -->

He came back to us dead. The men of the house threw him into us, and his blood ran into our boards; and what a child of the long-lived knows in his last hour goes with his blood into the wood that drinks it. So we had all of it.

Then they put fire to us. The old wood raged in its grief, and burned higher than any fire of theirs should burn, and screamed, for wood can. They shoved us off, and the current took us out toward the open sea, away from home.

We turned against the current, burning, for no water holds a keel that knows its way; and we sailed ourselves home. Even charred, even dying, the wood knows the way home.

Twice on that road, hulls of the shore-men passed near us at the edge of the grey, and turned from us, and ran. We do not know why.

Under the thinnest of the grey we found the old channels, the secret waters that run among the roots where no shore-man's keel has gone. All that way we burned, and the fire went out of us slowly as the grey closed over, until nothing was left of us but the keel, a few ribs, and the Guest's bones in them.

We came up out of the channels, black and small, onto the pale sand before the council-hall, which is a grove of silver elders standing down to the water; and we ran aground beneath the silverbarks, and were still.

The council of the long-lived came down to us, and knew us, for they had sent us. They laid their hands on our keel, many hands together, and knew the whole of it: the gift set down, and the foot that put it in the dust; the neck held gently, the foreheads meeting, the blade that slipped; the Guest struck down, the fire, the shove into the current. Wood can hide nothing.

They saw their holiest peace answered with murder and flame. They knew a murder, and it was one. But why the host's arm drew back from the blade they did not know, for fear leaves no mark on wood. They did not know it was ignorance, and they did not ask. They saw only an act of war.

<!-- RING -->

They laid our keel among the roots of the council-grove, the Guest's bones still in it, as the long-lived lay down their dead; and the roots drank. From that grove it went to every grove, root to root, to the far edge of the world, and every young tree that was to be a hull drank it with the rain. So every hull that has come against your wall has carried our grief in it, and the grain holds it still.

*And no one answered.*

## BOOK FIVE · THE BOOK OF THE TIDES

*The war, and how the wood learned.*

### V.1 · The First Glyph

*Laid by Halyna at the end of the first month of the siege, of what was done on the night the first assault was broken; set down by me, who held the lamp. When Halyna tell, they tell by turns, a paragraph each; I have used two inks, and not marked whose is whose.*

<!-- HALYNA -->

We hold the stone. Seren holds the lamp. That is how this began, and how it has gone on.

When the first assault was broken, the tide brought the wreck-wood in under the wall: grey planks and ribs, and a length of keel as long as a gate, rolling in the shallows. The soldiers wanted to burn it, and the Captain let them burn most of it. But Hale the runner came up from the tide-line saying there were marks on the wood, and Halyna went down to the shingle when the tide had turned, with Seren behind them carrying the lamp. They had the men haul one plank up out of the surf and lay it on the stones. There was carving on it. It ran with the grain, fine and shallow, one figure and no more.

Of the Mystaeri's writing we had three kinds by then, and could make nothing of any of them: the mute stones in the archive chest, the dark stone that lay beside them wrapped in an old man's confession, and now the wreck-wood. The stones would not speak, for stone carries nothing. Battle orders were carved into the living wood, we know now; that night we knew only that there were marks. But the axemen of the harvest years had left a saying in the havens, that the black wood would sometimes put a thought into a man's hand if he rested it there long enough; and there was nothing else to try. So they touched it.

Rhyna laid her palms flat on the grain, and Halvard laid his hands over hers. It never came into either of their heads to try it alone, for they did not yet know that one pair of hands was enough. They did not speak. The tide came and went on the shingle. The lamp burned down a finger's width.

Then the knowing came up into her palms, or the shape of one. She says it burned, not as fire burns but as cold iron burns in winter, and that the sign's own shape was in it: one stroke curling forward, like a wave about to break. Halvard knew it as she knew it, for that is the bond, and it was Halvard who spoke. He said, "Shore."

He could not say how he knew it. He said afterward that it was as though someone had put the word in his mouth and gone away. Rhyna said he was right. They knew more than the word, both of them; but the rest would not go into words, for it was a forest's thought, and they had only the shore's. That was all of it they could tell, though they held the plank until the lamp went out.

Seren wrote it down. She took a whole leaf of this Book for it, and wrote one word in the middle of the page, *shore*, and nothing else, and drew the sign on the facing leaf. She says it looked very small there.

So the Knowing began, on the shingle below this wall, and in the quiet hours between the holdings Halyna set to learning the enemy's gentle Mystwood tongue, which is not read but known, and to telling it, one sign at a time. It is done so still after every assault: the wreck hauled up, one of Halyna's hands on the grain, and Seren with the lamp; for they have learned since that night that what one knows, the other knows. The wood gives its knowing whole to no single mind. Soldiers have laid their hands on it and felt only wet planks. Other bonded pairs of the guild have tried it since, and known a little, and could not hold it long enough to tell it. Seren laid her hand on it once, alone, and felt something come into her too great to hold, and let go.

When they came up from the shingle that night, one of them said, and the other finished:

"The Guest came to our door with three words and no sentences."

"Now it is we who have one word, and no sentence. The right sign, known truly; and a sign without a sentence is only a mark."

This we lay as it was laid for us.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

### V.2 · The Night of the Naming

*Laid by Seren Two-Inks near the end of the Second Tide, on the night Halyna knew the young wood's planks whole at last. The planks she tells of lie in the lowest vault still, and anyone may go down and put a hand on them. The wood's leaf faces it.*

Hold the stone, Seren. I tell myself so every time.

From the first glyph on, we kept the wreck-wood. Not all of it: the soldiers burned what they hauled up, and the tide took more. But every plank that bore a carving, we carried down into the lowest vault under this hall, where the Rite was found, and laid them in rows on the floor. The soldiers called them the tablets. They were only planks. There were fifty-three of them by the end of the Hasty Tide. All but one had given Halyna one sign, and no more of it than a word, and I had written that word on a leaf. The one had given nothing, though it was carved like the rest.

Then the Second Tide came, and the planks from its hulls held two signs. All that Tide Halyna learned to hold the two together, and to name them, and to say what a hull had been told, though there were gaps in every telling. On a night late in that Tide they went down to try the old planks again, the fifty-three, and I went with them with the ink and the lamps. The night was short and the rows were long, so they began at the two ends at once. It was the first time I had seen them work apart, and they were not far apart: the length of the vault.

Rhyna took up the first plank of all, the wave-sign that had said *shore* on the shingle. At the far end, where it had been laid apart from the rest, Halvard took up the one plank that had given them nothing, not even a word, which the sea had brought in weeks after the first. I stood between them with the lamp, and I saw their two faces change in the same moment. Then Rhyna spoke, and it was not a word. It was the whole order that stands first below, and Halvard said it with her from the far end, word for word.

After that every plank gave its whole order, whichever of them held it, as if the wood, once it had come whole in them, had shown them how it is held. They went along the rows until it was light, and I wrote each order under its word. Here they are as I wrote them, for they were the first carvings of theirs we ever truly knew.

<!-- W (inline): the italic carving that opens each of the next seven paragraphs, and *Burn* below, are the wood's words; Seren's glosses after them are stone. -->
*To shore. As root to water, go. Wait on no bough.* That was the wave-sign, the first glyph: the hulls that ran straight at the coast with no one waiting for anyone.

*Break wall. First stone, only stone. Strike till it opens or wood is ash.* That was the closed square, which is how they cut the word for stone, the mute thing: the hulls that lined up before the first wall they touched and would not leave it until they sank.

*Go until struck. Where bark splits, turn aside.* The lone hull that ran in and swerved at the first shell, while the others did not follow it.

*Before bearer, thin wood. Thin wood falls; bearer keeps pace.* The cheap hulls at the head of the column. They were told to die first, and they died first.

*Where it flashed, strike. Last flash, only flash.* The hulls that bent toward whichever gun of ours had fired last, and toward nothing else.

*Wait in grey, as seed waits out frost. When stone is still, come.* The ones that hung at the edge of the fog and came in late, when we had stopped looking.

*Home when wounded. One bough falls; all turn, each by its own water.* The ones that turned and scattered at the first loss.

The soldiers took the head of each for its name, in plainer words, and the wall has used those names ever since: *To the shore*, *Break the wall*, *Go until struck*, *Before the bearer*, *Where it flashed*, *Wait in the grey*, *Home when wounded*.

And there was one plank more, at the end of the last row, that was not from our wall at all. A mason of Emberhythe had carried it up the mountain in his pack after the Fall, charred black at one end, because he could not bear to bring nothing of his city away. He did not know what it was. Halyna held it last. It held one sign, and the sign was *Burn*.

That was all the young wood knew. One word to a hull. Each hull had said its word at our wall, and said it again, and said it until it drowned.

All that first winter we had said that they picked the wrong fortress. Kael said it first and we all said it after him, and laughed. They had not picked anything. They had one word apiece and no other, and they had been sent.

When it was light I laid the two planks side by side on the floor, the first of all and the one from the far end, and the broken grain met, as the halves of a split log meet. I have not asked them what it means, and they have not told me.

Then Halyna sat down on the floor of the vault among the planks, and I sat down with them, and for a long while no one spoke. Then I said what I have written here, and they let it stand.

We had thought them fools. They were the youngest, sent first because the young grow quickest. The worst of the wood is not behind us. It is still growing.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

### V.3 · The Wreck That Went Home

*As the Mystwood remembers: known from a hull of the Tide of Remembering, sunk on its second coming, having turned from our wall wounded and come back to the same stone to die there; it faces the Night of the Naming. Known by Halvard, whose hands were on it; Rhyna had the salt-cuts open in her palms that day and did not touch it, and knew it as he knew it. The hull's own tale was quickly told; the law at its end they turned over for seven nights before they would let me write it. Told by Halyna, by turns, in two inks; set down by Seren. Where the wood was broken, or the telling failed, it is marked, and not mended.*

<!-- HALYNA -->

This is held in the grain.

We were a hull of the Ralenthae, the tide the roots taught, and more than a word was cut in us: a memory, and a caution. We came against your wall in the grey of a morning, our kin about us and the bearer ahead.

A stone of your wall flashed. The young wood had learned it, and the roots had carried it home: where a stone flashes, a gun is. We turned toward it. It spoke again, and the iron went into us low and through, and the sea came in.

<!-- RING -->

Then an older carving woke in us, one of the first the green wood was given. *Home when wounded.* We turned our stern to your wall and went back into the grey, low and slow. The guns let us go, or did not see us go. We do not know which.

As we went, we told our kin, for the wood needs no words among its own. The flash, and the place of it, went out of us into the roots.

Where we were struck went out of us too, and our kin felt it in their own boards, as you two feel each other's hurt across a city.

And the dread went out of us, which is only this: that one place in the water had become a place where wood dies. It went to every hull of that day, slowly, as sap goes, quickest to the heart of the bearer, and from the heart to all.

While you mended your wall, the heart carved the morrow into us: *Look before you trust.* A stone that spoke yesterday may be silent today, and a silent stone may be waiting. So on the next coming we went out in front, because we had been there, to see whether the same stone still flashed. That was all we were sent to do: to look.

It still flashed. With the sea in us already, we could not turn a second time. The iron found us where it had found us before, as though it too kept a count. We went down in the lee of that stone and lay in the cold while our kin went over us, until the tide worked us loose and the surf brought us to your shingle; and your hands are on us now.

<!-- RING -->

But before we went down we had told it. The hulls that lived carried it back into the grey, and the grey carried it home, root to root, to the groves where the next hulls stood growing; and they grew knowing it.

Know this rightly. The groves learn the wall, and not the hand. They learn that walls hide guns, and that a stone may be silent and not empty, and that a hull which turns and runs draws iron after it. But the hand behind the wall, which gun it moves and when, and how long it waits, goes down with the fleet that saw it, and does not reach the groves. Every fleet that comes against you knows walls. The hand behind your wall is new to each of them.

So we went home once, and told, and came back to look, and died looking; and the groves have it now, and the grain holds it still.

*And no one answered.*

### V.4 · The Council Under the Thin Sky

*As the Mystwood remembers: known from the heart of a Standard-Bearer of the Joined Tide, of the grain we call Silverbark, grown from a cutting of the silver elders of their council-grove; for their council-hall was a grove, and the grove heard the vote. Rhyna laid a hand on it on the shingle and took it away almost at once, and Halvard came up from the vault with his face wet. The knowing was over in a moment. The telling took them the rest of that Tide, for a council is many minds, and they had one pair of mouths to tell it. Told by Halyna, by turns, in two inks; set down by Seren. Where the wood was broken, or the telling failed, it is marked, and not mended.*

<!-- HALYNA -->

This is held in the grain.

Our council sat under the silver elders, in a hall that was a grove, with no roof but the boughs and no floor but the moss. The elders kept all that was said beneath them, as silverbark keeps all it hears. We are a cutting of those elders, and we give it as it was, for we can give it no other way.

<!-- RING -->

By then the sky was thin along the whole edge of the wood. The black pillars were gone from the shore in long gaps, like teeth from a jaw; the breath ran out through the gaps, and the between-light went hard and white.

The newborns of the long-lived came frailer every spring, and some did not wake. The eldest dwindled before their years were full, their strength going out of them like water from a cracked jar.

The elders counselled the trees' own patience. The iron comes seldom now, they said. The pillars have put up nothing; they are waiting. Wait with them, and when the iron stops, they will grow the sky back. The elders were dying of the very wound for which they counselled patience; and they knew it, and counselled patience still.

Then the Aelvaren came home by the channels, burned to the keel, with the bones of the Guest in it; and something broke.

The young had grown up under a vanishing sky and watched their mothers and fathers fade in the white light, and could not help them. Now they had seen the Guest come home in ashes. They came into the grove, many of them, and put the question to the council.

The elders rose and pleaded. Send another. Learn their words. They do not know us; they cannot know the wood, and we have never gone down to teach them. Go slowly. But the elder voices were few by then, and faint, and they were the voices of the dying.

And the young answered: "We listened three of their lives and learned three words, and they would not hear even those."

That was true, and not the whole of the truth, and the wood that heard it knows the difference. We could have learned more; we live long. We listened from behind the grey, and never once went down to the shore with our hands open, save once; and when the one who went was killed, we did not send a second.

We did not ask whether a whole people had killed him, or one house at the edge of it. We did not ask who else lived along that shore, in the ten havens, who had never heard our name. We chose to grow ships rather than to learn words.

But war is for the short-minded and the angry young.

So the question was put, and the vote was carried by the weight of anger and grief.

<!-- RING -->

That same night they took the straightest of the young silverbarks at the edge of the grove and laid it open to the heart while it still stood; and into the heartwood they cut the first carving of the war. *Grow until the shore is silent.* And the wood, which cannot unlearn what is carved in it, grew.

In those same summers the iron stopped coming to the edge. The grove had not waited to see it.

The elders who had pleaded went down into the moss one by one. The carving did not go down with them. It is in every heart that has sailed since, beneath all the other carvings, as the first ring lies beneath all the rings; and the grain holds it still.

*And no one answered.*

### V.5 · The Tides, as the Wall Counted Them

*Laid by Kael the Counter in six parts, each at the end of its Tide, for he would not tell a Tide until it was over. The names of the Tides are the wall's own. It is the stone leaf of the growing, and the wood's leaf faces it.*

**I · The Hasty Tide**

I count. That is what I am for. Here is the first count.

Eight sails, and not one keeping station with another. That was the first morning, and every morning of that Tide was like it. Each ship came alone and struck the first wall it touched, and when the wall held it struck the same wall again, and again, until it sank. The old soldiers had names for them from the Fall: warships, fire-ships, transports, mortar-ships, torpedo-boats. They are wrong names, and we use them still. No one stood on the decks. The root-men came off the transports like spilled kindling and died on the shingle; the wall calls them treelings, as if a name could make them small. By noon their fire always slackened, and we learned to say *the fleet has spent its fire*, and breathe. We laughed. God forgive us, we laughed.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

**II · The Second Tide**

The second count, and a harder one.

They came in pairs now. The front one took the shot and the back one took the ground. And in the third month of that Tide a lookout on the east tower called me up to see a thing I will not forget. A hull was struck off the north point, and a mile south of it another hull's sail went pale, grey-green blanching to white like a man's face, and it turned. It had not seen. It had felt. That was the first answering sail.

That Tide the crown-hulls came, and opened their crowns, and the winged seed came over the wall on the wind. The Stonewrights say the wind chooses the keep, and the seed goes where our guns are quiet.

Each fleet had carried a Standard-Bearer from the first, but now the rest waited on it. Sink it and the standard passed to another, a lieutenant standing in, and the fleet took on that one's temper after. So we say at the fire: *Choose which heart you leave them.*

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

**III · The Tide of Remembering**

The third count.

In the second month of this Tide, one night, the Captain had the south gun moved, the one that had done the most killing, and set it forty yards along the wall. In the morning they came. Not to the wall. To the place where the gun had been. One hull first, alone and slow, sounding the water, and the rest standing off to see what it found. They came to see whether the gun was still there.

We had thought them beasts that learn by the stick. They are not. They look before they trust. You learn to read the lean of them: a hull that leans to the shore means it, and a hull that leans away is thinking of home. After that we moved our guns by night, and they came looking in the morning, and it was a quieter war, and I liked it less.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

**IV · The Joined Tide**

The fourth count.

The drums went all the night before, knocking from hull to hull out in the grey. In the morning they came on three roads at once, north and east and the river mouth, as if one hand had flung them. Three roads, one hour.

Then I saw the strangest thing of the war. At the Captain's call, Lanner's battery sank their Standard-Bearer an hour after dawn. The rest did not falter. At the hour the dead heart had carved for them, they turned and came in on the east road, into empty water; there was nothing there to strike. They came a little late, and not together: one sail went pale, and then the next, and then the next, a breath apart, as if each hull had to find the hour for itself. But they kept it. They kept a dead mind's hour.

And some did not come on in order at all: the hulls whose own bearer we had sunk beneath them. They went wild, the orphaned hulls, the carving lost and only the grief left, out for revenge.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

**V · The Tide that Learned Deceit**

The fifth count, and I do not like it.

They came at us as the young wood came, loud and alone and straight, and we laughed as we had laughed the first winter, and every gun spoke; and out of the grey the hunters came for every gun that had spoken. They sent straw down a new road every day, hulls that were not hulls, and marked which roads we answered; and down a road where no gun of ours had woken, they sent the root-men.

But look close. Their lie always shows its seam. The phantoms leave no wreck, and they do not flinch when a shell falls by them. A true rout scatters, and a feigned one keeps its spacing. A laden ship rides low. And the young wood's sails never once went pale; a green hull whose sail blanches is no green hull. Every lie they told us this Tide, we could have known, if we looked. Some of us did not look, and they are under the east cairns. The brave thing, that Tide, was to look.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

**VI · The Last Tide**

The last count.

They carve a morrow now, as we do. Each fleet came with a plan in it, and the plan had turnings, if this then that. And at the end of the plan there was a turning for home. When the bearer fell, and the second after it, they went home. Not scattered. Went.

So now I understand what Halyna told me in the Joined Tide: that the fleet cannot be recalled, for the wood obeys the wood. I answered them then that we would send it home, and I did not know what I meant. The voices that could call it home are dead. It cannot be called home. But it can be sent home. The wood knows fear.

That is the count of the Tides. I have counted the grey sails for the whole of the war, the ghost fleet with no one on its decks, and I know their number, and I will not say it at this hearth.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

### V.6 · Of the Growing

*As the Mystwood remembers: known from the heart of a Standard-Bearer of the Joined Tide, grown in the first groves their council set; it faces the Tides, as the Wall Counted Them. Halvard carried it up from the shingle on his shoulder, and it came to them both, Rhyna at the hearth, before he set it down. But it was too great for them then, and it lay in the vault until they had learned to hold many hulls at once. No wood before it took so long to tell. Told by Halyna, by turns, in two inks; set down by Seren. Where the wood was broken, or the telling failed, it is marked, and not mended; and the Song of the Years at its end I have set as verse, as near as ink allows.*

<!-- HALYNA -->

This is held in the grain.

We were grown, not built. You cut stone and lay it, and it lies where you laid it, and it is dead.

The long-lived had always guided the wood, and the wood breathed out the sky they lived by: one life, in two kinds. A ship is only a tree led far.

They sang to the trees that were to be hulls, and the trees bent to the singing, for nothing had ever been asked of them but song: hulls from the breathing trunks, keels from the roots that fed them, masts from saplings trained by decades of windsong.

No one sails in us. The fleet is crewed by the living, and the living are the wood.

<!-- RING -->

How is a thing commanded that has no one aboard? By carving. For each fleet the council chose one hull to bear the carving, the Sethvaren. Into its heart, the Saenvael, they cut the orders, and the heart carved them on into every hull through the Aelralen, the bond of roots. We made that bond into a leash.

So the hulls obeyed the orders carved within them as a hand obeys the mind, for the wood of the hull and the wood of the command were one. The wood obeys the wood. In the night, while you lay your stone, the heart carves the morrow, and both of you are at work in the dark.

Yet the carving says what, and not whether. Each hull keeps its own grain and dread, and may balk at burning water, or turn savage where it bled: one mind, and many.

The bearers were chosen in grief, and the grief is in their grain. Your names for the grains will serve: the green wood, that had no temper yet; Ironbark, that comes again heavier where it failed; Willow, that turns from the first failure; Blackthorn, that goes where it lost the most; Heartoak, that never turns aside; Twistbough, that never plays one trick twice; Yew, that waits, and burns, and waits again; Ashwood, that throws all it has at once; and Silverbark, that knows you best, and bends least.

So the crudest came first, by the law of the years, which no council can carve against: a hull of one season holds one sign; of forty summers, a phrase; of a shore-man's life, a sentence, and a memory in it. The eldest pillars hold a judgement.

The council wanted the shore silent soon, and young wood grows quickest: one season, angry hands, one sign to a hull. It lay at the edge of your sea while your children had children, and your boats passed it in the fog and saw nothing.

So the armada sailed generations before it arrived, with one sign in it, *Burn*, against havens with no walls for war. The last of that green wood followed you into the mountains; and there, at the Keep, for the first time, someone struck back.

Each growing after went out when it was ready, as a tide goes out, and we call them tides. Leathae was the green tide; Vaelthae had grain in it; Ralenthae the roots taught. Aelthae is ours. Rhenthae and Eirthae were growing behind us, and what is cut in them this heart does not know.

Every hull was grown for its shore: to break stone, to carry fire, to throw it over walls, to strike from under the swell; and for shores that are not sea, to crawl on roots over sand and rock, to swim burning rock in a skin of wet bark, to go white in the snow.

The warriors of the landings are grown of root, for we had none of the long-lived to spare. The keel is the root that fed the tree; the warriors are the rest of it, the fine roots that find their way in the dark. They ride in the deep hulls and go ashore a few at a time, the heavy slowly, the light pouring. They walk, dig, and carry fire. You call them the root-men.

They know only their carving: the keep, then the gun, then the wall, and a wall at its weakest stone, as a root goes to the crack. Where they crowd they knot, four to a knot, and a knot strikes as four. Some go only at the wall, and leave it chequered; some burst upon the stone; some come bark-first; some bind the others' hurts with sap.

What they touch, the wood knows, for they are the wood's hands. Cut them down, and what they touched is known still.

The broad hulls hang back, and in their crowns the winged seed ripens. When a crown-hull opens, the seed goes up on the wind, over your walls. The wind chooses the keep; the seed shuns the hot sky over your guns. If its keep is gone, it comes home, and goes again.

The seed is the hull's own life: strike it all down, and the hull dies. Fell the hull while its seed is aloft, and the seed strikes, and falls, with no crown to come home to. Empty husks fly with it to take the shot meant for it; they have no fire, and the hull does not feel them fall.

Some seed grows so great it needs no crown. It rides the high air alone, hung from its own bladder, slow, and hard to bring down.

<!-- RING -->

The growing took decades. Those who cried for war grew old, and died, and were forgotten, and the carvers were fewer at every growing, until only scattered souls were left, tending the last groves.

So it was a doom set in motion by the dead, crewed by the living, against a people who had never known they were the foe. We were sent. We are still sent. No voice among us can call us home.

The groves sang it over us as we grew, and it is in our rings:

> *The green wood holds one word.*
> *The wood of forty summers holds a word, and waits for its brother.*
> *The wood of a shore-man's life holds a sentence, and a wall remembered in it.*
> *The wood of a long life holds the morrow, and carves it, and waits, and carves again.*
> *And the eldest of us, the pillars of the breath, the hearts of the grey, the anchors of the sky,*
> *hold one thing only now, and it is not a word, and it is not a sentence, and it is not a morrow,*
> *and we have carved it into every hull we sent you, and it is grief.*

So we were grown, and so we were sent; and the grain holds it still.

*And no one answered.*

### V.7 · The Mirror in the Grain

*Laid by Seren Two-Inks, with Halyna, in three parts, each on the night its carving was known. Seren tells it, and Halyna speak in it as they spoke on the shingle, a line each.*

**Part I · Three Roads, One Hour**

It was in the Joined Tide. The plank came from the Standard-Bearer of a fleet that had come on three roads at one hour. Rhyna laid her hand on it on the shingle, and Halvard was on the wall above us at the mortar, with a course half laid. I was beside her with the lamp. I saw her go pale; and when I looked up, he had gone pale on the wall in the same breath, with the trowel still in his hand, for what the one knows the other knows. He came down to us, and they told it. It was longer than any carving we had known, and it was not one hull's order. It was the order of the whole fleet at once, and the ties that ran between hull and hull.

<!-- W -->
*Three roads, one hour. Let the east and the north go as one. Let the south stand at the edge of the dread until the reach of the south has walked east; then let the south go.*

Rhyna began it, and Halvard gave it its end. For a long while after, neither of them spoke. Then I asked them what was wrong, though I think I knew. One of them said, and the other finished:

"It is how we work a wall."

"The one waits for the other's stroke, and does not need to be told."

The carving held a name as well, the name the wood gives to that whole Tide. Halyna told the name: *Aelthae*. I wrote it down. I knew the first part of it. It is *ael*, the joining, and I had written it before in this Book, in *Ael'thar*, the Guest's rite at the warden's door.

I have written the name, and I will not write what I thought.

This we lay as it was laid for us.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

**Part II · What We Taught Them**

In the Tide that Learned Deceit a plank came up from a hull that had come at us as the young wood came. It had sailed in a fleet that ran at the wall in the old way, each hull alone and loud and straight, and not one keeping station with another, as we had not seen since the first winter. The wall laughed, and every gun on it spoke, as every gun had spoken then. The hulls turned one breath short of our fire; and out of the grey behind them the hunters came, and went to every gun that had spoken, and would not leave it. Halyna took the plank, and knew it.

<!-- W -->
*Come as the green wood came: loud, alone and straight. Stop one breath short of the fire, and let the hunters wait upon the flashing.*

<!-- W (inline): the italic line in the next paragraph is the wood's. -->
And beneath it lay the thing it had been cut against: *they strike first and far; they move the guns we find.* It was carved against us, against what we do.

I did not understand it at first. I said that the wood cannot lie. It is the first thing the Guest's gift ever taught us of their carving: what is carved in their wood is always true. Then I understood, and I wrote it down before my nerve could fail. The wood does not lie. It was told to make us believe a lie, and it obeyed.

Halyna sat a long time on the shingle with their hands still on the grain. Then one said, and the other finished:

"They learned it from us."

"We taught it them with every silent gun."

It was true. Every gun the Captain held silent to draw a hull in; every false mast we raised on the wall with no gun beneath it; every gun we hid under sod and brush so that they sailed past it into its mouth. Every wreck from those days went back through the roots to the groves, and the groves learned. They had no word for a lie. We gave them one.

There was a name for us cut small in that carving, and Halyna would not say it at first. It was *Rhenear*: the stone-deaf.

This we lay as it was laid for us.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

**Part III · The Plan That Carved Its Own Defeat**

In the Last Tide a Standard-Bearer came against us with a whole morrow carved into its heart, a plan with turnings in it. We sank it, and the tide brought the heart in, and it was the longest knowing Halyna had ever held; and the holding was not the hard part. They knew it in the time it takes to fill a lamp. It took them until the lamps had been filled twice to tell it. At the end of it was this:

<!-- W -->
*When the bearer falls, the second takes the standard; when the second falls, go home.*

They have carved their own defeat into the plan. They know us now. They know that we can break them, and they have said what they will do when we do. And Halyna said there was one hand in all that carving, and not a council's, and that the hand was young.

But that was not what kept Halyna on the shingle after. It was the last two words. The young wood had known *home* only as the place a hurt thing runs to. This was a going home carved into the plan before the first shot was fired, a thing thought of in the groves and cut there on purpose. A mind that carves how it will go home is a mind that wants to go home. That night, for the first time, we saw that the enemy could hope.

One of them said, and the other finished:

"They carve a morrow now, as we do."

"Then let us build a morrow they did not carve for."

This we lay as it was laid for us.

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

### The Stone out of the Grey

*Written by Seren Two-Inks in the years after, on the leaf that faces the first tale of this Book. It stood empty until now, with the grain drawn on it and one line:* Untold. Halyna cannot yet hold this deep. *In the end no knowing of Halyna's filled it.*

I write this alone, and I write it plain, for there is no one now to know the wood for me.

Halyna are gone. They went in one night, the winter after the tenth Throne fell, as the Bonded go: Rhyna first, in her sleep, and Halvard before the lamp was out, for he knew. They lie under the new capstone of the inner gate, in one grave, with one stone, and one name on it.

I did not go with them. That is what an unbonded life is for, they told me once, and I did not believe them until the morning after. I am the first scribe of the guild to outlive the tellers, and so the Book goes on.

In the spring a small grey boat came in on the tide below the Keep and beached itself on the shingle, as the Aelvaren once beached before the council-hall of the Mystaeri. No one was in it, and no one had rowed it. The old ship-masters would not go down to it, for a grey boat that comes in on the tide is the boat that comes for you. It was the children who went down. In its bow lay a stone.

It was not stone. Or it was stone that had been wood: grey-black and heavy, with the grain still in it, like the Guest's gift that lay so long in the chest I carried up the mountain. The Rivenmen have taken to calling it Myststone, and they say it remembers all. It is the one stone that speaks. Stone does not translate, and that is still true; but this was never only stone.

And it had been cut with a chisel. I know chisel-work. A hand had cut it that learned the chisel late and learned it well, young by their reckoning, I think, and seasoned by ours. It was cut in our own letters, so that anyone might read it, and I did not lay my palms on it. I read it with my eyes. It was cut so that one could read it alone.

## APPENDIX · THE BOOK OF KNOWINGS

# HOW THE LEGENDS ENTER THE GAME

*For the integrator. This section is not part of the Book. Nothing in it touches the simulation: every surface named here is cosmetic and sits off the sim path. Where this section names a design document, it says "the fleet design (admiral doc)"; there is no admiral in the fiction (§9).*

## 1 · The standing laws

1. **Cosmetic, off the sim path.** No leaf, Whisper or message changes a number, a spawn or a behaviour. Every unlock is keyed to a campaign milestone or to an event the sim has already recorded, so two players who play exactly the same way open exactly the same leaves at exactly the same moments.
2. **Low-load moments only.** Lore appears at first launch, in the debrief, in the interstitial between campaigns, and at battle load. It never appears during timed Build or a live Fight. Two fleet surfaces are not lore and follow the fleet design (admiral doc) instead: the between-sortie dispatch (§8 below), and, from the Tide that Learned Deceit, the root sign a sunk hull of the running carving floats for the rest of its sortie, a drawn glyph with no words (admiral doc §9b). The root sign is Seren's eye-work: a root can be read by eye, and only the rest of a carving must be known.
3. **After, never before.** A tale about a fleet behaviour unlocks only after the player has met that behaviour. The Knowing's law (it explains this battle and never hints at the next) binds the tales too.
4. **No forced playstyle.** Every unlock is reachable by any way of holding a battle. Sinking a fleet and routing it both yield wood. No leaf is earned only by one particular counter.
5. **Every EVENT unlock has a CAMPAIGN fallback,** so no player misses a leaf.
6. **One new leaf per debrief or interstitial.** Any others queue for the next. A ripening (a fragment filling in, or a caption added) shows as a small mark and does not count toward the one.
7. **The Book only adds.** It never removes or changes a word the player has already read.

## 2 · The prologue (first launch)

- **What plays.** The Invocation, then the climb-and-writing passage of I.1 (marked `PROLOGUE EXCERPT`, unchanged in v1.1.0). It runs 60 to 90 seconds of text on the torn-cloak art. It is skippable, it plays once, and it can always be read again in the Book.

So from the first minute the player can see the shape of something they do not yet understand, in both of the hands they will learn.

## 4 · The Mystwood tellings, unlocked by knowing

**The rule of the wood (N9, N13).** Mystwood is not read. Touch it and its tale is known, whole, as a memory is known, and a knowing cannot be turned the way words can; the hard part is the telling. Only one of Halyna need touch the wood, for what one knows, the other knows. At first a shore-mind receives only **the shape** of a knowing, because the wood's thoughts are a forest's. Over the Tides Halyna learn to hold more of it and to tell it.

Every wood leaf is gated by **how much Halyna can hold and tell**, and that rises Tide by Tide. It is the Ender's Game ordering turned on the tellers: the first wood is crude and the first tellers are clumsy, and both get better together. Halyna cannot hold the gift whole until the wreck-wood has taught them (Cam 18). They cannot tell the council until they can hold the ties between hulls (Cam 23 to 24). They cannot tell home until they can tell what a carving *wants* (Cam 27). Depth follows campaign bands, so it is deterministic.

## 6 · Battle messages

### 6.1 · The style rules

1. **When, and for how long.** At battle load or briefing, or during the 3-2-1 transition, for three seconds. Never during timed Build or a live Fight.
2. **The draw.** Each battlefield has a pool of 5 to 8 messages, and a replay shows a different one. The draw is cosmetic and never touches the battle's seed. Because of rule 6, any draw gives every player the same information.
3. **Who speaks.**
   - Wall-voices: Hale, Voss, **Kael and the other True Men, by name** (the Twelve, §9), wall-hands, file-leaders, masons.
   - Seren and **Halyna** speak rarely. A Halyna line is two halves, a voice each.
   - Two further voices: **a Trade Council lord** who fled to the Keep, whose old pride is turning, and **old Idrenna**, the Theolith pair who taught Halyna, who take the long view; their line also falls in two halves.
   - The Captain never speaks. There are no admirals, generals or modern ranks, because there was never a navy and the generals were slain; and no one is aboard the fleet.
4. **Length.** At most 16 words, in one or two sentences.
5. **Register.**
   - The plain speech of their world. Humour comes from character, never from anachronism.
   - No design vocabulary and no mechanic named (no AoR, no piece names, no campaign numbers). "Sortie" is allowed, in soldiers' mouths only; so is "treelings".
   - At most one oath in a pool, and faith is never the butt of a joke.
6. **Hint discipline.** If one message in a pool hints at the battle's carving, **every** message in that pool carries the same hint in a different voice. Otherwise none of them does. A hint may name a tell that fits the Tide. It never names the counter.
7. **Truth.**
   - Never say what the fleet *will* do.
   - What the fleet remembers lasts one siege only. The wood remembers the wall, not the hand.
   - A Throne line may count groans.
   - Use fear-names only, until a true name has been told.
   - A treeling line goes only in the pool of a battlefield that lands root-men, and a seed line only where crown-hulls sail.
8. **Flavour.**
   - The Tide sets the fleet's behaviour: one-word mobs, then pairs, then looking, then three roads, then seams, then morrows, then groans.
   - The theater sets the texture: salt, river, fen, canopy, cairn, ember, sand, rime, crag-wind, crystal in the dark.

## 7 · Command Time in the fiction

- **Your ruling (2026-09-26, N4).** A day has **24 hours**, and an hour **60 minutes**. At the pace of the siege a day passes in **18 real seconds**, so an hour goes by in **0.75 s**. The racing hour is meant: it is the urgency and the anxiety of the holding.
- **Under command.** When the player starts an order in a Fight, time **ramps down** (it does not snap) until an hour takes **18 real seconds**. That is 1/24 speed: the hour becomes as long as a day was, and the minutes tick every **0.3 s**. When the order is committed or cancelled, time dwells a moment at that floor, so a second planned order can follow without racing the ramp, and then it **ramps back up**. Orders are stamped in sim ticks, so wall-clock speed never changes an outcome. The ramp and dwell numbers live in admiral doc §12.
- **Void.** The v1.0.0 mapping, in which a day had fewer hours and one game-hour was one second and one sim tick, is void. Admiral doc §12 (v0.3.0) restates the tick-to-hour mapping under the 24-hour day: the sim tick stays one real second at the siege pace, which is 1⅓ game-hours, and no tick count changes. The Fight doc must still restate any grid-per-second figures. The Legends do not decide it.
- **The command, told as fiction (N5).** The Captain commands, and his **True Men know his command**. Before the assault he gives each True Man his intent: where to watch, what shot to lay, when to hold (the Deploy order card). In the holding each of the Twelve fights his **battery** by his own judgment, because he knows the Captain's mind; that is why each battery fights on its own (auto-fire running the player's plan). The Captain can call to **one battery at a time**, by horn, with **three calls every True Man knows**, which are the game's three Fight verbs (Cannons & Towers §6):
  1. *Shift your ground*: the water a battery watches, not the stones it stands on (re-task the zone).
  2. *Change your shot*: for the water, the shore or the sky (switch domain).
  3. *Hold, or loose* (fire control).

  The **runners** (Hale among them) carry word of what is seen, never commands. When the Captain lifts the horn to call a battery, **the moment seems to stretch**: that is Command Time. A battery is a group of guns, not one gun (admiral doc §12). Twelve True Men matches the late cap of twelve guns, so even if every gun stood alone as its own battery, none would lack a True Man; that is a fit, not a rule, and the Book states no number of guns.
- **How the Book names it.** I.3, *The Captain's Long Breath*, is the only place, and it is named as something men *say*, never as a rule. "A day has four-and-twenty hours in it." In the holding "an hour goes by in a heartbeat, and a whole day in four-and-twenty of them" (a resting heartbeat is about 0.75 s). *"For the Captain, an hour lasts as long as a day"* is now exact. Hale counts ripples going down a sail, "and each of them, I think, was a minute". His picture follows the ramp: the hour slows the way a cart slows on a long hill, not the way it stops against a stone, and it lets go the way a held breath goes out.
- **The law, told as character.** Kael's correction states determinism: *"It is not the world that slows, lad. It is the Captain who will not hurry. The moment he chose was the moment that came."* The stamp is the tick, not the clock. "A second earlier or later" becomes a deliberate choice, never a reflex. Strategy, not skill.
- **The rules.** The Book gives no numbers and names no mechanic. No text of any kind appears during a live Fight.
