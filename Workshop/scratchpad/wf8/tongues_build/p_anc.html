2The First Tongue: The Ancestor of Both

Jack’s note: “Imagine that all languages come from an ultimate base language.” Orrowen and Seilrhass are made to sound like opposites, and they are. Under them lies the First Tongue, Tumaʔ, “what is held in common”: one tongue that both descend from, reverse-engineered as a philologist would build the primitive roots under two daughter languages. It has a sound system, 624 roots, the ordered sound laws that turn them into both living tongues, and 45 first marks from which the course-hand’s letters, the dry cut’s word-signs and the grain’s signs all descend. Every form in this section is computed by a sound-law engine from its ancestral form and checked against both lexicons; nothing in a table of forms is typed by hand.

The check

356 derivations from 253 inherited roots, 0 mismatches. Every one of the 310 Orrowen entries and 215 Seilrhass entries published in v0.1 falls out of the laws, directly or as a formation whose every part does, with a short list of named irregularities (§2.3). Every Hal form the Shoreland spec gave comes out exactly (three are emended first), and so does every Eldest form. The canon Hal texts, the Stonwryt and the proverb, are reproduced word for word. The 371 reserve roots derive in both tongues by the same laws and collide with no existing word. What the check cannot do is judge beauty or originality: the reserve forms passed a blacklist and the whole English dictionary, and a dictionary pass against Welsh, Irish and the Tolkien lexicons is still owed before any of them ships (§13.8).

Notation. An asterisk marks a First Tongue form: *tolm-. In ancestral forms th is /θ/, dh is /ð/, x is /x/ (as in Scots loch), ŋ is the ng of sing, and ʔ is the catch, a stop in the throat, the grandfather of the Mystaeri knock. A hyphen marks a suffix seam and + a compound seam. “O” is Orrowen, “S” is Seilrhass. The name is Jack’s to confirm Jack: Tumaʔ in the First Tongue, Tuma in Orrowen, Theinae in Seilrhass, and in English the First Tongue.

2.1 · What the First Tongue was, and why no one knows it

  • One people, one tongue, one hand. Before there was a grey or a wall, one people spoke Tumaʔ. They cut their marks on whatever stood: a stone set on end, or the trunk of a living tree. Their word for both was *tolm-, “a standing thing”.
  • A mark was a word and a sound at once. Each of the first marks stood for a thing, and it could also be read for the first sound of its name. A line of marks said what was meant and how it was said, both at once. Neither living hand can do that.
  • A word held. The tongue’s name for itself comes from *tum-, “hold, keep, in the hand and in the mind”. In Orrowen the root means “remember” (tum); in Seilrhass, “hold, know by touch” (thein). So the hearth’s yes and the wood’s Knowing are one verb.
  • The parting. No tale tells it, and nothing here needs one Jack. The Book fixes only the two ends: the Mystaeri “were old” before the Shorelanders cut their first stone (II.2), and the first Stonewrights “came up onto the high ridges” in the age before the havens (II.1). The two kinds of standing thing became the two peoples’ matter. Those who stayed with the trees cut their marks into living wood, and every ring the tree grew carried them outward: what grows keeps its meaning and loses its sound. Those who went up to the rock cut their marks in stone, which does not grow: the chisel wore the marks straight, and the builders read them for their sounds.
  • The tongues drifted, as tongues do. The wood’s speech lost every stop and every back vowel, and its catch became the knock. The stone’s speech kept its stops, lost its endings, and turned the lost endings into mutations. By the time the grey sails came, no word of one could be heard in the other.
{{tab a_kept}}

Why the Knowing is the nearest survivor. Only the wood kept what the first marks meant, and “a knowing cannot be turned” (the foreword). The stone kept how the First Tongue sounded, and sound is what time changes: the Rivenmen cannot sound their own Hal. So the one part of the First Tongue anyone alive can still take in whole is the wood’s. This doc says so; the Book never does (§2.8).

Why no one knows. The Hal is unreadable even to the Rivenmen (§3.4). The Mystaeri “never once went down to the shore with our hands open, save once” (V.4). The grain has no sound, so the one hand that kept the First Tongue’s meanings cannot tell anyone how it sounded. And the kin words look nothing alike: tresk and thaess, tolm and thael, ol and ael, molt and nael. Only the laws show that they are one word.

2.2 · How it sounded, and its grammar in brief

Stops and back vowels, like the stone’s tongue, with the wood’s breathy clusters (xw-, xr-) and a catch in one root in eight. It sounds heavier than Seilrhass and less worn than Orrowen: *trosk-i, *xreun-aʔ, *waʔr-ai, *swe-reŋ-o-s.

{{tab a_cons}}
  • Clusters. At the start of a word: stop + r or l; s + stop; xr, xw, xwr, xwl, gw, sw. After a vowel, most sonorant + consonant pairs, st, sk, and doubled ll, nn, mm, rr, ss. Common: t, k, s, n, l, r, m; rarer: b, ŋ, h, y.
  • Vowels. Five short vowels, a e i o u, and the diphthongs ai ei oi au eu ui. The back vowels o and u are frequent, and the wood lost both. There are no long vowels: length in the Hal comes from the catch.
  • Syllables and stress. A syllable is (C)(C)(C)V(C)(C). Stress falls on the first syllable, as in both daughters. A catch carries a falling pitch on its syllable, and that pitch survives in the knock’s “high falling pitch”.

The catch, ʔ, and its three fates. The catch is the First Tongue’s own colour, and it is where the knock began. In the wood’s speech a stop at the head of the second syllable left a catch before it, and the catch then spread to every word of two syllables or more: the Mystaeri knock is the First Tongue’s catch, moved. A root with the catch after its vowel is often the solemn form of a plainer root: *sol- “lie still” beside *soʔl- “the deep rest” (O sollan, peace).

{{tab a_catch}}

Roots and ablaut. Most roots are one syllable, CVC or CVCC (*tolm-, *krask-, *senn-). A root may show e, a or o in different words: *tol-/*tal- gives tolm a stone, tald rise, and the wood’s thal the neck; *krenn-/*krann- gives crenn a captain and rhann heavy; *bro-/*bra-/*bre- gives brod a word, ranth a word, renn a mouth. Extensions make new stems: *-d- “made, brought to” (*tal-d- rise up; *gal-d- build; *xal-d- a wall), *-t-, and *-n- and *-l- (*xwre-n- frost in stone, *xwre-l- frost in wood).

The grammar the daughters inherited.

  • Nouns end in a thematic vowel, *-o- (broad) or *-i- (slender). The nominative adds *-s: *tolm-o-s. Orrowen built on this form, which is why the Hal ends in -os and -is. The bare stem was used for calling and naming, *tolm-o, and Seilrhass built on that, which is why it has no case endings. The broad and slender classes of Orrowen are these two vowels, remembered.
  • Other endings: *-aʔ “a whole of two or more” (O dual -a, and the feminine); *-uʔ “the place of” (O -ow); *-aθi plural (O -Ath); *-en “belonging to” (O -en; S plural -en); *-eʔ “one of” (S -ea); *-aʔr “all of a kind” (S -aer, later -ear); *-el “one, a small one” (O -el).
  • Verbs take the person endings *-om, -iθ, -os, -ana, -aʔ, -ar, -us, -ant. Orrowen kept them. The 3sg *-os fell with the other endings, which is why the living 3sg is the bare stem (Hal STONOS > ston). Tumar, “we remember”, is *tum-ar, “we hold”.
  • Particles could stand before or after the word they governed. Orrowen, which put its verb first, fixed them before the word, and that froze their final -n and final vowels into the mutations. Seilrhass, which put its verb last, fixed them after the word: its verb suffixes -e, -es and -ar are the particles *e “now”, *es “at another time” and *ar “toward”, fused to the verb after the final vowels had fallen. So *es became a future in the stone and a past in the wood: the stone looked forward with it, and the wood looked back.
  • Pronouns and pointing words were many, and each tongue kept a different set: *en “this one here, I”; *θo “you”; *o “he, it”; *ey “she”; *ol “together, we”; *sa/*se “that one by you”; *la/*le “that living one”; *xa/*xe “that mute thing”; *ra “this”; *re “that”. The wood’s split between living and mute pronouns is the First Tongue’s.

2.5 · The kinship: the words that are one word in both tongues

38 words are one word in both tongues, and they fall where the Book already cares most. The meaning in the First Tongue is the one both senses grew from.

{{tab a_kin}}

Two crossings worth seeing. Death and night trade places across the two tongues. The stone says “die” with *senn- “go down” (O senn, “go out, as a fire in peace”); the wood kept *senn- for “beneath”. The wood says “die” with *feʔ- “fade” (S veas); the stone kept *feʔ- for “night” (O vess). In the same way, the stone’s word for stone is the wood’s word for the tree, and the wood’s word for stone is the stone’s word for mortar setting fast.

2.6 · The first marks: the sign-set both hands came from

The First Tongue wrote with 45 marks, cut on whatever stood. Each was a small picture, and each was named by a First Tongue word. Stone took the marks to the chisel: they were worn straight and set on a bed, then read for the first sounds of their names, and a reform of the first builders laid them out as a system of uprights and laid stones, which is the course-hand. Some marks the chisel kept whole as words, and those are the dry cut’s word-signs (§5). Wood took the same marks into the growing tree: they were set on files and bowed by the knife, read for what they mean, and grown into new signs. That is the grain. Two marks were kept whole by both peoples: the Fore (a stroke crossed near its head: the Latin cross, the Bar Before) and the Door (two posts and a lintel: the gate mark, and DOOR).

{{tab a_marks}}

Where the table says a mark was lost on stone, read lost from the letters: many of those marks were kept by the chisel whole, as the heads and crowns of the dry cut’s word-signs (the Cup is TUM, the Drop LORR, the Star ORL, the Scar VORR, the Knot TOVV, the Mouth BROD, the Hand GARL, the Eye VESK, the Kneel LUMM, and so on: §5.4). And the stone kept the marks as words, not as meanings: a word-sign is read as one Orrowen word, whose meaning drifts with the word.

Every course-hand letter, from the marks

The reform’s rule (C6). A letter is the upright of its place + the laid stones of its manner. The three uprights come from the three oldest letters: the Bearer for the lips (p), the Course for the tongue-tip (t) and the Split for the back of the mouth (k). Each manner’s stones come from the mark whose own name began with that kind of sound: the voicing pin from the Post, *gann-; the nasal’s middle stone from the Breath Within, *molt-; the fricative’s low stone from the Breathing, *hoss-; and so on. So the reform is itself an act of reading by first sound: it asked what each mark sounded, and kept only the strokes that told it.

The two marks kept whole

  • The Fore (*re-il, “the one before; first”). In wood it is the Bar Before, the root of the Screen line; its soft reading, reil, is the Fore’s own name, worn. On stone it is the first builders’ mark, “first, before all”, and in the dry cut it is the word-sign hosast, the foremark (§5.5). Its right arm and foot also gave the letter r. It is a Latin cross, and that is fine (note 8): it is older than both peoples, and each kept it for “first”. Whether the Shorelanders also keep it as a holy sign is Jack’s call; the design does not need it, and the Book’s faith needs no emblem Jack.
  • The Door (*gann-aʔ, “the two posts”). On stone it is the gate mark that closes an oath, capped as the coping that closes a tale; it is also the word ganna and the pair-name custom, “a pair is a gate”, and the dry cut’s word-sign GANNA. In wood it is DOOR, its posts battered in. “We were a people of doors” (IV.3). So, it turns out, were both.

They are no one’s alphabet. The first marks are proto-writing, so some are the world’s commonest strokes: the Stem (|), the Bar (—), the Fore (+, by design), the End (T), the Door (Π), the Cut (V), the Three (|||). None is a Futhark rune (no stave with straight branches; the Roots’ three feet are uneven), an Ogham letter (the Tally cuts notches, not strokes), a Cirth letter, a Tengwa, or an Arrival logogram.

2.7 · The roots

624 roots. The {{n roots_inh}} inherited roots are the roots behind every existing word of both tongues, with their small words, affixes and names. The {{n roots_res}} reserve roots are First Tongue words that neither living tongue had used, each with its regular form in both tongues, laid by for the whole Book’s translation (§11): a reserve word enters Orrowen as a Hal word (so its Hal form and its mutations are known), and Seilrhass as a root like any other. ★ marks a root that both tongues kept. Where a tongue did not keep a root, the cell gives the form it would have, and says “falls together with” where that form is already another word, and “a lift” where it is on the originality blacklist and must not be used. The reserve roots were drawn from the First Tongue’s own sound pattern, seeded so the list can be remade exactly, and screened: no clash with any existing word, no blacklisted form, and no form in either tongue that is an English word. Any reserve word can be swapped for another draw without touching the laws Jack.

2.8 · The lore: letting the kinship show, without editing a tale

Call-out for Jack Jack

The Book should never say that the two tongues are kin. No one at the hearth knows it. It can let the player find it, in the places the Book already keeps for native hands and their translations (Legends map §4).

  1. The mark on the black chest (I.4) is the Fore. The tale says only “a chest of black stone, with the mark of the first builders cut in its lid”, and that surface is still owed. Draw it as the Fore in the Hal’s cut style: a stroke crossed near its head, square-footed on its bed. From One Word (Cam 1) the player has the Bar Before in Seren’s sign-list, so the two are the same sign, and nothing in the Book says so. Its translation, at The Bedrock Hand (Cam 16): The first builders’ mark: “first; before all”.
  2. The head of the lintel column (owed; Read Down the Lintel, Cam 32). Above the topmost Hal name there is one line older than the Hal, cut in the first marks. It stays untranslated until item 4.
  3. Seren’s last line in the Book of Knowings (optional: it adds to the appendix, not to a tale), added with item 4: The Bar Before is the mark on the lid of the chest I carried. I looked at it every night of the war, and did not see it.
  4. A last achievement, The First Tongue (native Tuma), at the launch after The Last Carver, when nothing else is left to earn. It changes no word; it adds three translation lines: under the lintel’s first line, What is held in common.; under the proverb, wherever it is shown in both tongues, In both tongues the last word is one word: both.; under the Stone out of the Grey, Stone writes what is said, and wood what is meant. Here, for the first time since the first tongue, both are cut on one face. It keeps the Legends’ laws: cosmetic, reached by every player, after and never before, and it only adds.
  5. “The Knowing is the nearest survivor” needs no new line. The foreword already says “Words can be turned, and a knowing cannot.” This doc gives the reason (§2.1).
  6. Echoes the tales already hold, for players who dig, and nothing to add: Tumar is “we hold”; the Guest’s last word and the gift’s last clause are one root; Rhyna’s name is the wood’s word for stone, and the Guest’s errand-name Aelrhen, “bond-to-stone”, is at the root “together, set fast”; Halvard’s -vard is the Aelvaren’s -varen.
  7. Never: in a tale, in store art, before the Finale, or as a mechanic.

Faith, reverently. If “an ultimate base language” is, for Jack, the one tongue before the scattering, the design leaves exactly that room and never claims it. The Book names no cause for the parting. The First Tongue’s word for God, *marð- “the Maker, the one knelt to”, survives only in stone (Mardh); the wood kept no name for Him, only the kneeling. The Stone’s grain says “our God” as the one they kneel to.

Recommended: adopt 1 and 4 (the drawing, and one late achievement), with 3 if the Book itself should notice at the very end.
