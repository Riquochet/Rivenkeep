3Orrowen: The Shoreland Tongue

Orrowen, “of the Shore”, is what the Rivenmen speak, what Seren writes, and what the hearth answers in. It is built to feel ancient and Celtic without a Celtic word in it: the likeness lies in how the tongue works (verb first, the mutations, the dual, the conjugated prepositions), never in its vocabulary. It is also built to be the opposite of the thunder-tongue in every way a listener would notice, and yet it descends from the same First Tongue (§2): every word in it is derived by the sound laws, with no mismatches. Version 2 keeps the whole grammar of v0.1 and adds what Jack’s notes asked for: a stone grammar for the economical chisel register (§5), a running ink hand for the whole tongue (§6), the Hal placed as the most economical hand of all (§3.4), and Seren’s name given its meaning (§3.6). The script, Garl Dhrenn, is written three ways, from the most economical to the fullest:

{{tab s_hands}}

A vow is always cut whole, in letters, with every word: “a sign holds what is meant; a vow must hold what was said.” That is why the tale-stone’s Stonwryt can be read word for word.

3.1 · What things are called

{{tab s_names}}

The names were chosen so that the words for writing are the words for masonry. A scribe of the guild lays a line (cadh trenn) as a mason lays a course, and a letter is a stone. A Stonewright cutting a line dry lays it as a dry-stone waller lays a wall: no mortar, the stones chosen to fit by their shapes alone, and the small words, the mortar words, left for the reader to lay back in.

3.2 · The sound of it

Eighteen consonants. Three more lived in the Hal and have merged: /ŋ/ into n, /ʍ/ (hw) into f, /x/ into h. The glide /j/ exists only as softened g.

{{tab s_cons}}

Six vowels, and two long nuclei: ae /ɛː/, only from old long ē (as in Kael), and ow /ou/, from old long ū and in the place-suffix -ow. Unstressed e is often /ə/.

{{tab s_vow}}
  • Broad and slender. a, o, u, ow are broad; e, i, y, ae are slender. A word’s class is the class of its last full vowel, and its suffixes follow it. Root vowels are free, so Corlen and Pellow are good words.
  • Stress falls on the first syllable of every content word. Particles and pronouns are unstressed and lean on the next word.
  • The music comes from few long vowels, many sonorant codas (-rn, -ld, -lm, -rl, -nn, -mm) and a frequent rolled r. Nothing in it has the thunder-break. To a Shoreland ear the Mystaeri “knock”.
  • Frequency. Common: l, r, n, t, d, s, th, m. Rarer: b, g, dh, v, h. Rare: f, rh, w.

How the Book spells it

The Book spells Orrowen as Seren would hear it, not letter for letter from the course-hand (§3.2 gives the mapping). These rules keep every existing Shoreland name valid.

{{tab s_rom}}

The Book never uses Welsh ll, dd, ff, ch or Irish bh, mh, gc, dt. Orrowen must not look Welsh or Irish on the page.

Syllables

A syllable is (C)(C)V(C)(C). z, x, q, j never occur, and nor do the endings -ion, -iel, -dor. Where a mutation would make an illegal cluster, a softened g before a consonant drops, a softened rh after a consonant drops, and a nasalised d before r stays d.

{{tab s_phon}}

3.3 · Grammar

Glosses in this doc use S = softening, N = nasalising, VN = verbal noun, DU = dual, PST = past, FUT = future, NEG = negative, Q = question, REL = relative. The romanised line shows the mutated sound; the course-hand writes the base letter and a bite.

Word order: verb first

  • Verb, subject, object, then the rest. Keth et tolm “hold the stone”; Carm et lodh hy et trun “cries the mortar from the ground”.
  • The noun comes first in its phrase. Adjectives, demonstratives and possessors follow it: et gunn sy “the deep this”, that is, “this deep”; hosk et ganna “lintel [of] the gate”.
  • Numerals come before a singular noun: sull carm “three call”; delv vennuld “twelve True Man”.
  • Particles come before the verb and mutate it: re (PST), es (FUT), nath (NEG), ho (Q), sa (REL).
  • Emphasis is a cleft with the copula: El et tolm sa heth “It is the stone that holds”.

Nouns: three numbers, one article, and the construct

There is no grammatical gender; only the pronouns o (he, it) and ey (she) tell sex. That keeps the soft mutation off the article, which is the Welsh and Sindarin habit this tongue avoids.

{{tab s_num}}

A few old duals are words of their own ending in -a: ganna “gate”, literally “the two posts”; pana “both”; and every pair-name. Some nouns name a whole kind, and one member takes -el: Stonwrytan “the guild” gives stonwrytel “one Stonewright”.

Harmony. A suffix vowel takes the class of the stem’s last full vowel. The script writes these two vowels with their own harmonic letters, so the class is written once per word, by its stem.

{{tab s_harm}}

The article is et, the same for every number. It never mutates and never causes a mutation. A proper name takes none, and neither does a noun with a possessive. A noun followed by a definite possessor takes no article: this is the construct, and its possessor is softened. Hosk et ganna is “the lintel of the gate”; *Et hosk et ganna is wrong (a star marks a wrong form). Putting an article on the head of a construct is the classic foreigner’s error, and it is exactly the Last Carver’s (§6.19).

{{tab s_constr}}

The two mutations

Both change only the first consonant of the next word, and only once. The course-hand always writes the base letter with a bite under it, so the dictionary form can always be found. The outcomes and triggers are Orrowen’s own: neither table is Welsh’s or Irish’s, though both are the same kind of thing.

Softening (S, “the wearing”). In the old tongue these consonants wore soft between vowels. The vowels at the ends of words are gone; the wearing stayed. f, v, w, th, dh, s, h, l, r, n and all vowels are unchanged.

{{tab s_soft}}

Nasalising (N, “the bedding”). These words once ended in -n, and the n bedded into the word that followed. f, v, w, th, dh, s, h, l, r, rh, m, n are unchanged.

{{tab s_nasal}}

What triggers which. Each trigger once ended in a vowel (S) or in -n (N); §2.4 shows the old forms. Adjectives do not mutate, except after a dual: pa yarl dhevel “two young hands”.

{{tab s_trig}}

Verbs

A verb is a stem and a person ending. Tense is a particle before the verb. There is no second-person dual: a pair is spoken to as many and answers as two.

{{tab s_pers}} {{tab s_forms}}
  • Yes and no. Orrowen has no word for either. You answer by repeating the verb: Ho dhumos? “Do you remember?” is answered Tumar “We remember”, or Nath dumar “We do not”. So the hearth’s answer is, grammatically, a yes. Every stone leaf asks, and the hearth says yes to it.
  • The progressive is the state verb with ul “in” and a verbal noun: Doss et hald ul glennol “the wall is in mending”.
  • The imperative plural in -a sounds like the broad 3du (tuma). A dual verb always has a subject, and the script tells them apart (§3.7).

Two verbs “to be”, the most irregular words in the tongue, as the oldest words usually are. The copula puts the predicate first: El tolm et hald “[It] is stone, the wall”; somm el tolm tolm “while stone is stone”.

{{tab s_be}}

Pronouns and prepositions

One set of forms is both pronoun and possessive; the mutation after it marks the possessive. The dual pronouns are the plural plus -na. A pronoun after a verb is emphatic (Tumar ol “We remember”); an object pronoun follows the subject (Re hadhan o “we two laid it”).

{{tab s_pron}}

When the mutation cannot show, nothing marks the possessive. Before a word the softening leaves unchanged (f, v, w, th, dh, s, h, l, r, n, a vowel), o saed after a verb reads “he, half”, not “its half”, and o hosen reads “he … like”, not “his equal”. Say the possessor as a noun (saed et covv, “the half of the cloak”) or recast (uld hosen et Crenn, “a man like the Captain”). The same holds for en, ol, va before a letter the bedding leaves unchanged. back-translation

Prepositions take the person endings directly; there is no “to me” or “on him”. ul (in, +N), hy (from, +S) and dem (until, +N) follow the same pattern.

{{tab s_prep}}

Hy is also “than” (“greater from X”): hos strom hyo, “one greater than he”. After a verb of going the “from” reading wins (orr hy, “leave”), so a comparison is not set after orr. The most is sost, “self, very”: et strom sost, the greatest. An adverb is the bare quality after the verb. back-translation

Having, being able, feeling, knowing

Orrowen has no verb “to have”, no “can”, and no verb for most feelings. A thing is at you; a feeling is laid upon you, as a course is laid. And the Shoreland word for knowing the wood is the canon’s own: to know it is to hold it.

{{tab s_idiom}}

Negation, questions, relatives

  • Not: nath + N before a verb (nath geth “does not hold”); nel for the copula; the prefix na- + S for “un-”: nawrodhat “untold”, nadhum “forget”, nayald “destroy” (un-build), nalodhat “unbonded” (un-mortared).
  • “Only” of a clause is nath … veth, “not … but”: sa nath re yorrar veth amm ew venn et dask, “who fought only when the cause was just”. Hosel “only, alone” set after a verb reads “alone”. Nath … tul is “not yet” (Seren’s own line), never “no longer”; the tongue has no “no longer”, and says it another way (re vammant so hrenneth, “they had lost their captains”). The negative past is nath re + S (nath re yal, “was not”). back-translation
  • Questions: ho + S. The question words are cedh who, vodh what, ul vodh where (“in what”), hy vodh why (“from what”), amm vodh when.
  • Relatives: sa + S with a gap: et tolm sa heth “the stone that holds”; Voss sa re vess “Voss who asked” (from pess).

The formal and oath register

  1. The dual for pairs. The Bonded speak of themselves as “we two” and are spoken of by their pair-name with 3du verbs: Tuma Halyna “Halyna remember”. Where the Book gives Halyna a plural verb, the Orrowen original is dual. Common speech in the havens let the dual slip; the guild never did.
  2. Swearing. An oath begins Rytom um … “I cut [my word] upon …”, from ryt, a cut vow. It closes with the one-word vow Ston. “It stands.”, followed in writing by the gate mark. Liturgy keeps the Hal form Stonos. A vow is cut whole, in letters, even in stone (§5.2).
  3. Respect. Elders and bonded pairs are addressed in the 2pl. The Captain calls a True Man tho, in the singular, as a comrade.
  4. Antiphony. A line said “one voice to the comma and the other the rest” is split at the wedge mark. In Seren’s two inks, one ink runs to the wedge and the other takes the rest.
  5. The Hal in liturgy. The Rite of the Cornerstone is recited in the Hal. The guild pronounces it by the living tongue’s rules as best it can, and knows the words sound wrong.

Names, and the pair-name

X of the Y is a construct: Rhyna Yanna Ulvenn “Rhyna of the Inner Gate”. X the Y is apposition: Kael Nydherd “Kael the Counter”. A compound epithet is modifier and softened head: Halvard Tolmvard “Halvard Stone-Warden”, which translates his own name. At the sealing (hoskol, “the laying of the lintel”) the guild names a pair as a gate is made:

{{tab s_pair}}

The three canon pair-names come out of one rule by three different softenings, and every pair-name ends in -a because the lintel is the dual. A pair-name takes a dual verb and, in writing, carries a sealing lintel. The unbonded keep one name. Seren has no lintel and never will; “one name is a mourning”.

3.4 · History: from the first marks to the leaf-hand

{{tab s_stages}}

The lintel of the western stair shows the whole history in one column, “one beneath another, for as many generations as the quarry was worked”: Hal names at the top, cut right to left; haven spellings in the middle; Halvard’s line at the foot in the living hand. Names are always letters, so the column reads by letter, and a player who has earned the old hand can read it downward and watch the tongue change (§12.2, Read Down the Lintel).

The four hands on the economy scale

Writing on the Shore began with meanings and ended with sounds, and every step toward sound was also a step toward ink.

{{tab s_economy}}

So the Hal is the most economical hand of all that can still be sounded. The first builders had just made the letters (the reform, §2.6), but they cut with the old marks wherever a mark would do, spelled only names and the words no mark held, and left every ending and every small word to the reader, who spoke the Hal and could not mistake them. The living dry cut is a little fuller: the living tongue has lost the Hal’s endings and turned their loss into mutations and harmonic suffixes, so the endings that still carry meaning are laid on in letters. The leaf-hand writes everything. Two things keep this true to the First Tongue: a word-sign is a word, not a meaning (it is read as one Orrowen word, aloud, and when the word’s meaning drifted, the sign followed the word: the Cup is read tum, “remember”, though the mark meant “hold”), and the tale-stone’s Stonwryt is cut in full, in letters, word for word, because it is a vow. It is the one Hal text anyone can still sound.

The sound changes, in order

The exact laws, stage by stage from the First Tongue, are in §2.3, and they derive every Orrowen word with no mismatches. In brief, from the Hal to the living tongue:

  1. The wearing. Inside the Hal, between vowels, at compound seams and after a word ending in a vowel, a single p b m t d k g hr was said soft: v w v dh r h y h. The Hal hand never wrote it.
  2. The bedding. After a word ending in -n, p t k were voiced and b d g became nasals.
  3. The fall of the endings. Unstressed final syllables were lost: -os, -is, short vowels, the -n of the particles. With the endings gone, the wearing and the bedding were no longer explained by any sound, and they became grammar, which is how real Celtic mutations arose. Old tolmos > tolm; xaldos > hald; ulan > ul, still nasalising.
  4. Long vowels shifted: ā > o, ē > ae, ī > i, ō > u, ū > ow. Old kēlos > Kael; old orrū > orrow “shore”. Final long -ā survived as -a: the dual ending, the lintel of every pair-name.
  5. Three mergers: x into h (xal > hal), ŋ into n (soŋmas > somm), hw into f (hwlennā > flenn “leaf”). The Hal hand still has a letter for each.
  6. Old wr- lost its w (wryta > ryt). The guild still spells its greatest word the old way, Stonwryt, and says it /ˈstonryt/.
  7. Harmony. Unstressed suffix vowels reduced, then took the colour of the stem: old kethola > kethyl, but tumola > tumol.

Where each trigger came from

Every trigger is explained by its old ending, which the First Tongue explains in turn: the particles were fixed before the word, and their final -n and final vowels froze into the mutations (§2.2). Two rows are emended from v0.1: Hal re (not rea), and Hal van (not vanan).

{{tab s_origin}}

Why a Rivenman cannot read the Hal

He can see every letter of it. What he cannot do is sound it. It runs right to left and every letter faces the way it runs, so an old p leans like a living k. It has no word-gaps. It spells the endings the living tongue has lost (TOLMOS for tolm). It writes no bites, so the pair the Rivenmen call Aldwena is cut ALD · BEN. Three of its letters stand for sounds no one has said in twelve generations. And some of its words are simply gone. This is why “the first fathers of the guild had known better, and we had forgotten them”: the forgetting is written into the stone. Halyna read the slate leaves by lamplight because the Bonded keep the Hal as a liturgy; earning the old hand is learning what they already knew.

And most of it is not in letters at all. Outside the one vow, the Hal is cut in word-signs, mirrored, with no endings and no small words. A Rivenman knows perhaps half of those signs from the living dry cut (the same heads and crowns, turned the other way and cut taller), but he cannot supply the Hal’s endings or its small words, because he does not speak the Hal. So the Bonded keep the slate leaves of the Rite as a liturgy, learned by heart with their Hal readings, and read the signs as reminders of words they already know.

3.6 · Seren: “a single sorrow that overcomes”

Jack’s note 6: “Sometimes a name is just a name. Having a meaning isn’t always necessary. Seren should mean ‘a single sorrow that overcomes’.” Her name is the First Tongue’s *Swe-reŋ-o-s.

{{tab a_seren_parts}} {{tab a_seren_der}}
  • Stress is on the first syllable: SE-ren. Both vowels are slender, so her name takes slender suffixes.
  • In the course-hand. The living hand writes s e r e n, spelled as said, with no lintel: “one name is a mourning”, and her one name is a single sorrow. The Hal writes SEREŊOS, with the long ending and the shore-nasal ŋ, one of the three letters for sounds no one has said in twelve generations. The sorrow in her name is, in the old hand, a letter no one can sound.
  • In the wood’s tongue the same First Tongue word would also come out Seren (spoken Se’ren). It is the one name in the Book that is the same in both tongues: fitting for the one who writes both halves of the Book.
  • The guild’s own reading. The Rivenmen hear ser, “go on, carry forward”, in her name. That is a folk etymology and a true thing about her (“she can go where we cannot, alone, and carry this Book on after us”, II.3), but it is not where the name comes from; ser stays a verb of its own. Welsh seren “star” remains a coincidence of sound only.
  • Names are names. The names given no meaning keep none (Corlen, Voss, Hale, Marl, Tamm, Aske, Harl, Ulden, Hesk, Lanner, Aldun, Bena, Idlan, Denna). The laws account for their sounds; the real-world senses (Estonian tamm, Old Norse askr, English marl, Scots harl) are never borrowed.

3.7 · The canon lexicon ({{n lex_s}} entries)

The fields are built from the Shorelanders’ own metaphors: to tell a tale is to lay a course (cadh trenn); a promise is a cut stone (ryt); a pair is a gate (ganna, the dual of “post”); to know the wood is to hold it (keth); freedom is loosing (lusk, lunn). So one Orrowen word often carries several English ones: trenn is course, line and tale; keth is hold, keep and know; vesk is see and read; tresk is cleft, riven and torn. Columns: the living form; the Hal form where it is known (most nouns are the stem + -os broad or -is slender); the harmony class (B broad, S slender); the sense; notes; and, new in v2, how the dry cut writes it: its word-sign (HEAD+crown, with its number in the inventory, §5.6), its sign with a complement, or letters. The First Tongue derives every entry (§2.4). This is the canon; the whole tier-3 lexicon of {{n olex}} entries, which contains it, is in §11.3.

4The Course-Hand (Garl Dhrenn)

An alphabet of straight chisel strokes that stand on a mortar line. It records sound, and it is cut in stone, so it has no curves. It is Ogham-like in spirit (strokes set on a line, made to be cut) and its own in every shape and rule: its letters stand on one side of the line only, and their shapes say how the sound is made. The letters are unchanged in v2. The dry cut uses them for names, rare words and complements (§5); a vow is cut in them whole; and the leaf-hand is them, written (§6). Every letter descends from a first mark by the first builders’ reform (§2.6).

4.1 · The picture in one paragraph

Every letter stands on the mortar line (lodh), as a stone stands on its bed. Consonants are ashlars: tall, joined, cut whole. Each has an upright rising from the bed and one or more laid stones running to its right. The upright tells where the sound is made: a forward-leaning prop (the lips), a stepped offset (the tongue-tip) or a back-leaning shore (the back of the mouth). The laid stones tell how. Vowels are pinnings: small loose stones set between the ashlars, their parts never touching. A mutation is a bite cut into the mortar under a letter’s foot, so the letter itself stays whole. Each word is a stone with its own bed, parted from the next by a head-joint, and successive lines break the joint as a mason staggers courses. A pair-name carries a sealing lintel. A page of it looks like a wall.

4.2 · From the tongue to the letters

The course-hand writes the underlying form, not the Book’s spelling. Each base consonant is one letter, with its mutation as a bite. Each root vowel is a full vowel letter. Each suffix vowel is a harmonic letter.

The other way always works, because the hand shows every base and every mutation. Worked example: Seren’s first word, Nawrodhat “untold”, is the prefix na- (which softens) + brodhat “told”. The hand writes n a bS r o dh A t: the b stands whole with a V-bite under it, and the harmonic A takes its class from the o before it, so it is read a.

4.3 · How a letter is built

The three uprights (place). Coordinates are in cell units, origin at the letter’s left foot on the bed, y up.

{{tab s_upr}}

The laid stones (manner). They lie at three heights: top (y = 3), middle (2) and low (1). A joined stone starts on the upright; a free stone stands apart. The pin is a short second upright, a bedded stroke from (3.6, 0) to (3.6, 1.3), and it marks voicing.

{{tab s_manner}}

The families answer the mutations. p b m share the prop, and so do the soft forms they wear to, v w; t d n th dh share the offset; k g h share the shore. Both mutations keep a sound on its own upright, with two exceptions that history explains: softened rh became h, a bare breath; and nasalised g was once /ŋ/, the shore-nasal the Hal still writes. So the living hand’s one empty slot, the shore nasal, is the gap the merger left.

Vowels (pinnings). The broad vowels carry a capstone over short pins; the slender ones a tall pin with a low stone beside it. y is the leaning pin: e’s tall pin riven from its bed, leaning over its low stone as the riven ridge leans over the Keep.

{{tab s_vowels}}

The harmony mark: once per word. A harmonic letter has no value of its own. It takes the class of the nearest full vowel before it in the same stone. Theldeth and lunnath end in the same two letters, A th, read -eth after e and -ath after u. The Title teaches this in its fourth and eighth words.

The end rule, which gives every stroke its “up”

A stroke end is square where it lies on the bed or on another stroke of the same letter. Otherwise it tapers over 0.4 u, where the chisel lifts. A stroke with no square end at all (a free stone, a capstone, a lintel) is dressed square at both ends. The rule is computed, not stored, and the grain’s knife-cuts, which taper at both ends, can never be mistaken for it.

4.4 · Bites, stones, marks, lintels

A bite is a notch cut down through the bed band, under the letter’s foot.

{{tab s_bites}} {{tab s_feet}}
  • A bite goes under the first letter of a word, or under the first letter of a root after na-. It is written only where the mutation changes the sound: es ston has none.
  • Names and set compounds are spelled as said, with no bite: Halvard, Lurrvard, gorndholm, vennuld, and pair-names in running text. The bite marks grammar, not etymology.
  • An N bite under a vowel reads m- before a broad vowel and n- before a slender one. A softened rh or g that falls silent keeps its letter and its bite: the hand never drops a letter the tongue has dropped.

Stones. (v0.1 called a word with its own bed a “word-stone”; v2 calls it simply a stone, to keep it apart from the dry cut’s word-signs.) Each word has its own bed band, broken at the head-joint, so a line looks like a course of stones. A word is never split across lines. Breaking the joint: where a head-joint on the lower line falls within 1.0 u of one on the line above, the lower line’s joints widen by 0.25 u until they clear (or, if that would overflow the measure, narrow by 0.2 u steps to a floor of 1.4 u). A joint is a word break whatever its width, so reading is untouched.

Punctuation. Every mark is a stone in the line, in its own cell. Questions and cries have none: the particle ho and the imperative carry them. Direct speech is not marked; the Book’s quotation marks are Seren’s, added in translation.

{{tab s_punct}}

The sealing lintel. A pair-name in running text is spelled as it is said (h a l y n a), with one unbroken free stone at y = 3.75 over the whole word. That is how a reader knows a pair-name: it has a gate over it. The Stonwryt ligature is how a master-pair signs a vow under a capstone: his post (the first syllable of his cradle-name), a half head-joint, her post (her cradle-name stem, in base letters, with no bite), and one lintel over both on one shared bed. The final -a is not cut, because the lintel is the -a, and the lintel softens the second post. So Halyna’s Stonwryt reads HAL | RHYN and the old capstone’s ALD | BEN. A reader who knows the rule says Halyna and Aldwena. One who does not sees two unknown names.

4.5 · Numerals

Speech counts in twenties; the guild also counts in twelves, “twelvefold, as the old masters counted it”. A number is a run of numeral letters, each capped by its own short laid stone at y = 3.75; the caps break letter by letter, where a sealing lintel is unbroken. The numeral letters are the initials of the number words. Values add, except that a unit letter standing directly before d, m or b multiplies it.

4.6 · Reading it back

For each stone, read the letters left to right (right to left and un-mirrored in the Hal):

  1. Classify each letter. Height 3 (or 4 in the Hal) is an ashlar; height 2 (or 2.5) is a pinning. An ashlar’s first stroke gives its upright: a forward lean is the prop, a step is the offset, a back lean is the shore. Its laid stones and pins give the manner. A pinning’s parts give the vowel.
  2. Resolve the harmonic letters from the nearest full vowel before them.
  3. Read any bite under the first letter, apply the mutation to get the spoken form, and keep the base for the lexicon.
  4. Read the marks: perpend, wedge, gate, coping. A sealing lintel means a pair-name; caps mean the letters are numbers.
  5. Undo a Stonwryt ligature: first post + softened second post + a.

Four look-alikes a reader must not trip on

  • A letter’s free low stone is not a wedge. l and w end in a free stone at y = 1 inside their own cell. A wedge is the same stone in a cell of its own, a letter gap after the word.
  • A middle stone meets the offset’s riser partway; a joined top stone leaves from its top corner. On n, r, rh the riser shows above the stone; on t, d, l, dh nothing shows above it. In the coal hand, read this and not the height.
  • Full a against harmonic A. The imperative plural (Talda), the collective -an, the old duals and every pair-name’s -a have an unbroken capstone. The 3du -A (Tuma Halyna) is broken. The capstone tells an order to many from two who do a thing.
  • The Last Carver’s bites are tapered strokes: his N bite is three short strokes, a cup; an S bite would be two, a V.
Proved from the pictures alone

In the blind decode test (2026-09-27) a reader who had only the rules of the Orrowen and course-hand chapters, and screenshots of the drawings with every title and description stripped out, read ten course-hand images back exactly, letter for letter, bite for bite and mark for mark: the cut, coal and Carver forms, both bites, harmony, the sealing lintel, the wedge, the perpend and the gate. The English came out word for word from the lexicon. Nothing in the renderer needed changing; the four look-alikes above are what slowed the reading. The Hal texts and the charts were not part of that test.

Reading a dry-cut line adds one step before step 1: sort each stone’s signs. A sign 4.6 u tall on a footing is a word-sign (look it up by its head and its crown, §5.6); a sign 2 u tall standing before a word-sign, with its one laid stone running left from the head of a pin, is the turned stone (not); everything else is a letter. Letters after a word-sign on its bed are its complement. Then lay the mortar back in (§5.2).

4.7 · Four ways to make the letters and the signs

{{tab s_forms4}}

Cut rendering. Each stroke is two flat V-cut facets, lit from the top left, in one ink: the drawings in this doc are stroke-only SVG in currentColor under the class stone-ink, with the shade facet, the mortar and the bites at fixed strengths. No filters, no gradients. The Title is 43 letters and five marks; a whole leaf of Seren’s hand fits well inside a page. The leaf-hand is no longer a flat-pen sketch: it is drawn, and built as a font (§6).

The Last Carver’s hand is the fourth, and it is a mistake made four ways. He learned the letters only from the coal Title, so: every letter he cut occurs in the Title; he cut no bed, because cloth has no mortar and he never knew letters stand on one; every stroke tapers at both ends, as a knife-cut in living wood does, because the rule that a stroke is squared where it is bedded is the one thing the Title could not teach him; and the cutting stops with an open joint, no mark, “the chisel’s last stroke still sharp at the end of the line”. He copied the Title’s nasal bite faithfully. So his only error of language is the extra word (§10.19). And a fifth tell, new in v2: he cut the mortar. Every mortar word of the Title’s first line is cut, ul and five times ol, and one more the Title never had. No Stonewright cuts a small word in stone, least of all et. He cut stone as the Captain wrote on cloth: in letters, every word, as it is said.

4.8 · The old register: Garl Hal, the first builders’ hand

The same letters, transformed, and the same word-signs. The top names on the lintel of the western stair, the slate leaves of the Rite, the mark on the black chest and the old capstone’s Stonwryt are all in this hand.

{{tab s_hal}}

The Hal’s word-signs. The Hal cuts the same heads and crowns as the dry cut (§5), under its own rules 1 to 5: mirrored in their cells, cut at 4/3 of their height (a word-sign stands 6.13 u, a letter 4), on the one continuous bed, with no footing (height alone tells a sign from a letter), no complements and no bites. Only names, the words no mark held, and a vow are cut in letters, and the letters carry their full Hal endings. The misreading is designed. Mirrored, the Hal vowels swap: an old a looks like a living o, e like i, A like O; u is symmetric, and an old y mirrors into a shape the living hand does not have. No mirrored Hal consonant looks like any living letter, because every mirrored laid stone runs leftward. So a living reader who ignores the direction reads the consonants as nonsense and the vowels wrong. The hand tells him it is the Hal only by its height, its missing joints and its leftward stones.
