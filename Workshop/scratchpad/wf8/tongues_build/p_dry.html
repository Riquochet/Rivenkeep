5The Dry Cut (Ryt Broc): Orrowen’s Chisel Register

Jack’s note 1: “Because Orrowen is a chisel language, we need an economy of words. It seems like the lettering should be like the reformed Egyptian of the Book of Mormon in its economy.” The dry cut is that economy. The stone is cut; the mortar is the reader’s. It cuts only the stones of a sentence (its nouns, verbs, qualities and numbers), as word-signs where a word has one, and leaves out the small words, the mortar words, for the reader to lay back in. The reason is the chisel’s: a word cut in granite costs a mason an hour, and a small word costs him as much as a large one. Across the eight sample texts the dry cut uses 117 signs where the leaf-hand uses 339 letters and marks: 35 per cent. A granite Book would be about a third as long as the ink one.

5.1 · The picture in one paragraph

A word-sign is one of the First Tongue’s marks, worn straight by the chisel and stood on the bed, and read as one Orrowen word. 27 marks serve as heads: each alone is a root sign, read as the word the stone kept for it (the Cup is tum, “remember”; the Course is trenn; the Door is ganna). Any head may carry a crown: one of 20 other marks, cut small and set over the head like a capstone on a pillar, which turns it into a neighbouring word (the Hearth under the Still is varn, home; the Hearth with the Drop is theld, family). The head gives the field and the crown the word. A word-sign stands 4.6 u tall, half again as tall as a letter, “proud of the course”, and it sits on a footing, a second mortar line under its own, so a reader always knows a sign from a letter. Endings that matter are laid on after the sign in letters; names, rare words and loans are cut in letters; not keeps one mark of its own, the turned stone. There are 328 word-signs: 134 write words of the v0.1 lexicon, and 194 write reserve words of the First Tongue, for the whole Book’s translation.

It is the “reformed” hand in Jack’s sense, and not a copy of one. Logograms with phonetic complements are an idea shared by Egyptian, Maya and Chinese writing, and ideas are free. Nothing in the dry cut copies the characters of any of them. Its signs are the First Tongue’s own marks, and its economy is the First Tongue’s: the first marks wrote no small words, as the grain still writes none.

5.2 · The stone grammar

Eleven rules. The notation below writes [WORD] for the word-sign read word, HEAD+crown for a sign by its parts, and bold letters after a sign, as [THELD]A th, for its complement.

  1. D1 · Only the stones are cut. Nouns, verbs, qualities, numbers, and the few adverbs and conjunctions that carry weight (tul “yet”, somm “while”, amm “when”). A word with a word-sign is cut as its word-sign; any other word in letters.
  2. D2 · The mortar words are left out. They are never cut, except in a vow (D9): {{tab s_d2}}
  3. D3 · The one mortar word that keeps a mark is not. Nath, nel, new and the prefix na- are cut as the turned stone, set against the stone they turn, on its bed. A thing cut not-so must never be read as so.
  4. D4 · Order is the order of speech. The verb first, then its subject, its object, the rest, exactly as spoken. A line that begins with a noun has no verb and is a naming: a heading, a memorial, a label, a proverb. Two nouns side by side are a construct, the second the possessor (hosk ganna, “the lintel of the gate”), unless the line is a proverb, when they are predicate and subject.
  5. D5 · Endings are laid on in letters (phonetic complements). A word-sign alone is the bare stem: a singular noun, the 3rd-person or imperative singular of a verb, an adjective. An ending that says something the order cannot is cut after it, in letters, on the same bed. The harmonic letters A and O take their class from the sign’s own word. An irregular ending is cut in full vowels, as a name is. A person ending is cut only when no subject stone follows: Tuma Halyna is [TUM] =Halyna. {{tab s_d5}}
  6. D6 · No mutations are cut. A mutation belongs to the mortar word that caused it, and the reader lays both back in together.
  7. D7 · Letters, always: the names of persons, and pair-names with their sealing lintel (“a name is said, not meant”); a word with no word-sign; loan words and Mystaeri names, spelled by sound, with the knock as a wedge inside the word; numbers, as capped numeral letters. Place-names that are plain words are cut as their words: Cemm Hyll (Tidesmeet) is [CEMM] [HYLL].
  8. D8 · Compounds are their stones, side by side on one bed: Stonwryt is [STON][RYT]; Stonwrytan is [STON][RYT]a n; vennuld is [VENN][ULD].
  9. D9 · A vow is cut whole. A Stonwryt, an oath (Rytom um …), the Covenant: every word, every mortar word, every ending and every bite, in letters, then the gate. “A sign holds what is meant; a vow must hold what was said”, for a vow is sworn aloud, and whoever raises the stone must be able to say it again word for word. When a vow is only cited (on the Stonwryt coin, on the lintel of a finished work, in the rolls), it is cut dry, and the gate alone is read Ston.
  10. D10 · The marks. The perpend ends a sentence; the wedge is a pause, and stands between the things eth, ell and veth would have joined; the gate ends a vow and is read Ston; the coping ends a tale; the foremark may stand at the head of a work. A cry, a question and direct speech are not marked: “a stone does not ask”.
  11. D11 · Beds and lines. Each word (the turned stone if any, its sign or signs, its complement; or a word in letters) is one stone on its own bed, parted from the next by a head-joint, and lines break the joint as in the letters. Every word-sign also stands on its footing. The line pitch is 6.4 u.

Stone has no tense. A work’s inscription speaks of what stands, a memorial of what was, a vow of what shall be. Where the time must be cut, a time is cut: a day, a year, a count of generations. And the Guest spoke the dry cut: Hess… Helv… Sennyl… are the three stones exactly, for he had already left out every mortar word, “your tongue joins its words with small words that make no sound across water” (§10.8).

5.3 · The word-signs and their laws

The ancestor’s laws of form made the letters out of the marks (§2.6). The word-signs are the marks the chisel kept whole, and they obey the first two of those laws and five of their own.

{{tab s_wslaws}}

The geometry. A crowned sign’s strokes are exactly the head’s strokes with every y multiplied by 0.7024, then the crown’s strokes with each point (x, y) moved to (2.2 + 1.2 (x − 2.2), 3.4 + 1.2 (y − 3.4)). Every crown lies wholly above y = 3.4 and every pressed head wholly below 2.95, so a crown can never touch or crowd a head.

5.8 · What the word-signs give the Book

  • Every inscription can now be economical and true. A lintel, a capstone, a marker, the coin: each can carry a real dry-cut line of a few signs.
  • The Stonwryt coin gets a legend at last: the vow’s last clause and its gate, cut dry, round the rim, [SOMM] [TOLM] [TOLM] ⟨gate⟩, “while stone is stone. It stands.” Jack
  • The granite Book that Halyna mean to cut “when there is peace enough” would be cut dry: a third of the ink.
  • The Creed and the guild’s word, as the guild would cut them over the door of its halls: Build, not un-build. Guard, not attack. (nayald is the turned stone and [GALD]; nath before it is a second turned stone) and Not sword, stone. Not killing, keeping. (veth “but” is the wedge).
  • Kinship a decoder can find. The stone’s word-signs are the same first marks as the grain’s signs, worn the other way: the dry cut’s TUM (the Cup, “remember”) is the grain’s HOLD; its SCETH (the Bough, “a hull”) is the grain’s HULL; its LUNN (the Gap) is the grain’s BREATH. Nothing in the Book says so (§2.8), and the grain’s forms of those marks stay exactly as they are.

6The Leaf-Hand (Garl Flenn): The Running Ink

Jack’s note 1, its second half: “The later written language would have to be able to be written quickly like English if the smaller words were included in the language.” The leaf-hand is that later hand. It is the course-hand written, not cut: the same letters, each made in one movement of the pen, the whole hand slanted and joined. It writes the whole tongue, every mortar word and every mutation, as fast as a hand can move. Seren writes the Book in it, and it is built as a working font, Garl Flenn, embedded in this page.

{{ink title}}

The Title of Liberty in the leaf-hand: 48 letters and marks in 20 movements of the pen.

6.1 · The picture in one paragraph

The pen goes up the letter’s upright from the line and lays its stones rightward. The voicing pin, which the chisel cut as a separate short post, becomes a second leg that brings the pen back to the line. A free stone is a quick touch set after. A hairline carries the pen along the line from each letter to the next. A reader of the course-hand can read the leaf-hand at sight: every letter keeps its upright (forward, stepped, back), its stones at their heights, its pin. The bites hang small under the line, the sealing lintel rides over a pair-name, and the harmonic letters dip their capstones where the cut breaks them.

Every letter can be told from every other. Place is the upright’s lean (after the slant: hard forward, gently forward with a jog, a little back); manner is the height of the stone and whether it is full, short or a touch; voicing is the second leg. The closest pairs on the look-alike screen are a/A and o/O, told apart by the dip, and b/d, told apart by the lean and the jog. The two inks: Halyna’s tellings alternate their two inks by paragraph, and in antiphony (the Cry, the guild’s word) the ink changes at the wedge. Seren’s own notes, and every leaf that is not Halyna’s, are in her one ink.

6.4 · How fast it is

Speed is how often the pen is lifted: one movement for every stroke that does not begin where the pen already is, and one for every bite, lintel and mark.

{{tab s_speed}}

A word whose letters all end on the line is written without lifting the pen at all: Tumar is one movement, t-u-m-A-r, and the perpend is the second. Written whole, small words and all, the ink runs about as long as English: the I.1 pilot is 1,020 words of Orrowen against about 1,080 of the Book’s English for the same text (§11.4).

6.5 · The font, Garl Flenn

The leaf-hand’s table is precise enough to build a font from, and it has been built: Garl Flenn 1.000, 15.3 KB as woff2, 1,347 glyphs, each letter swept by a broad nib (0.40 u at 25°) and a round hairline pen, with every letter’s four forms (alone, first, inside, last) chosen by the font itself. Type the Book’s spelling and the hand appears. It is embedded in this page, so everything below is live text, not a picture.
