- **IV.1 ¶4, a lexicon gap.** The Book's epithet "the floating stones" (of Highreach) had no set-phrase row. >
> **Headnote.** Laid by Corporal Voss, Voss who asked, in the Second Tide, as Voss's grandmother told it in the harbour-houses of Eldhythe, where she kept the fire and drew the ale. It is a tale the ship-masters do not tell, and a song the children do not stop singing. The Shore laughed at it, and the sea did not laugh. The wood's leaf faces it.

>
> **¶2.** She kept the fire in a harbour-house at Eldhythe, on the quay, the kind that has a long table and a low roof, where the ship-masters came in out of the rain. Above the fire three wide grey stones were laid in the wall, with curling signs on them, fine as the tracks of birds on wet sand, and no one knew what they said. They had hung there since her own grandmother's days. The ship-masters knocked their pipes on the lowest stone.
>
> **¶3.** Our fathers feared the open sea. They kept to the Shore, and to the coasting lanes between the havens, and when a ship went far out and saw a fog-bank, it came home quick. The sailors called it the Mystlands. Sometimes a bold one went near, and turned [? *re hyl o*: "turned back" or "was changed"], and came into my grandmother's house with a wild tale, and drank on it for a week. She believed none of them. She said: The Mystlands turn true men into liars. And that was the only thing she ever said against the sea.
>
> **¶4.** But there was one tale the ship-masters did not tell, and she told it to me more than the others; so I think she believed half of it.
>
> **¶5.** It came from the men of the outermost outpost, the one at the end of the dry land. They swore to it in their cups: We pushed a boat off the shingle once, on fire, with a dead man in it; and the tide carried it out; and when it was far out, it turned. It turned into the wind and against the current; a boat cannot turn like that. And it went away into the grey, still burning, and screaming as it went. They swore by the scream more than by anything. They said: You can hear the scream when the fire is past seeing.
>
> **¶6.** The Shore laughed at that, and my grandmother laughed too, and knocked her pipe on the wide grey stone above the fire. The ship-masters did not laugh. They would not speak of it, because it is bad luck when the sea is near; and I saw a ship-master who set down his cup [? *o nunth*, the possessive unmarked before n] and went out into the rain, because he did not want to hear the tale.
>
> **¶7.** Then I heard it from three ship-masters, at fires in the mountain passes, the farthest from salt water they had ever come. Each had seen it himself, or had sailed beside a man who saw it; and their tellings were the same: a small boat on fire, far out in the grey, where there is never any fire, turning into the wind; and after it, weather. They say: It brings the bad weather. They say: It is a boat that comes for you when you die. The Shore laughs, because it will never come to the Shore. The ship-masters do not laugh. And the towns wrote it down: It was nothing. A stranger, armed, killed. A boat, burnt, cast adrift. That was the whole of the writing [? *Ew gor yalat et rytyl ull*, "that writing was everything"]; and who argues with what is written?
>
> **¶8.** But the children had a rhyme about it. I do not know who made it. We skipped on the quay with a rope while we sang it, two children turning and one skipping; and we thought: The greeting is the strange thing; for who ever heard a greeting like that?
>
> **Rhyme** *(not blind)*. Grey boat, grey boat, out of the grey, / knelt at the warden's door one morning; / a cut for a kiss, a stone for a gift, / and a greeting like this, unheard-of! / Burn it, push it, give it to the tide; / but the grey boat cried out, and turned.
>
> **¶9.** There were two more lines. We did not sing them on the quay, because when a ship-master heard them he caught the rope out of our hands, and would not say why:
>
> **Couplet** *(not blind)*. Grey boat on fire, and rain behind; / and the one it comes to does not come home.
>
> **¶10.** I skipped to that rhyme when I was seven. I understood it when I was forty, on the shingle under this wall, the first time I heard a ship of the fleet burning. For it screamed. It screamed as it went, as the men of the outpost had sworn, and the old sailors on the wall turned away from it; and I stood in the smoke with my mouth open and the song turning in me, and I thought of three wide grey stones above my grandmother's fire, and of the pipes knocking on them.
>
> **¶11.** We forget. We have always forgotten well. But the Mystwood remembers.
>
> **¶12.** This I lay as it was laid for me.
>
> **Hearth.** And the hearth said: We remember.

   - "our own words" said as *brodath orrowen*;
   - "rather than hear it told" said as "because he did not want to hear the tale".
2. **Polysemy the context mostly carries.** Each costs at most a nuance: *stykil* wonder/enchantment (¶6), *genth* table/shelf (¶7), *saed* half/share and *cadh* lay/file away (¶9), *desir* "late, after the time" (¶12), *ryss* wide/flat (IV.5 ¶2). For *genth*, the lexicon has no other word for "shelf". If Jack wants "shelved" to be plain, *eth cadhat lo sest*, "and laid aside", would carry it without *genth*. That is a style call, so it is left to him.
3. **Two more unmarked possessives, left standing.** *El ol saed ull* (IV.1 ¶9) and *hessyth ol lestir* (IV.1 ¶7 and addition S06-11) put *ol* before a letter the bedding leaves alone, the §3.5 case. Both were read right. In each, the word order already marks the possessive:
   - after the copula, the next phrase is the predicate, so *ol saed* can only be "our share";
   - in a verbless saying, *hessyth* and *ol lestir* are its two halves.
   
   Unlike *o nunth*, neither follows a verb. **Recommend** the integrator add one line to `orrowen_v2` §3.5: "The rule bites after a verb. After the copula *el/ew*, and in a verbless line, a pronoun before a noun is read as its possessor." Otherwise, recast both: *Doss saed ull lor*, "that share is ours", and *hessyth et lestir*.
4. **The reader's own slip in ¶7** (the carvings as object, not subject) is not the text's fault. The Orrowen follows the unit's S8 agreement rule (a 3pl verb with a plural noun subject), and the gloss supports the Book's reading.
5. **A process finding for the next blind readers and the integrator.** In the verse blocks (the rhyme and the couplet), `build_s06.py` writes the Book's English and the word-for-word lines as `>` blockquotes too. So the native lines cannot be taken by `>` alone, and this test lost its blind reading of the verse that way. Either the builder should mark the English verse differently (no `>`, or a `<!-- en -->` tag), or blind extraction should take only the first blockquote under each verse heading. The I.1 pilot's verse block is laid out the same way; its back-translation took lines by line number to get round this. Every unit with verse built from the pilots' layout will have the same trap.
