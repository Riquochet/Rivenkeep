## IV.1 · OF THE FELLING OF THE BLACK PILLARS (*GOSTOL ET GANNATH DOMM*)

*A stone leaf, laid by Halyna in two inks, a paragraph each. The inks alternate from the first body paragraph; Halyna "would not have me mark which is whose", so they are named only first and second. Seren's headnote, her stained-roll note in ¶4 and the hearth's answer are in her one ink.*

### Title

> ## *Gostol et Gannath Domm*

**IV.1 · Of the Felling of the Black Pillars**

Word for word: *The felling of the black posts.*

- The lexicon's title (O0783), unchanged. *Gannath* is the canon's word for the pillars (*Gannath Myst*, the Mystholders, is "the posts of the grey"); *gostol* is the felling of a tree.

### Seren's headnote (her one ink)

> *Re hadha Halyna ul Hyll Pawast, ul ba luth, hos eth ullen, hy scaevureleth rytow et Stonwrytan, sa re rarrant dem susk et brunn ul vestul et Stonwrytan. El flenn et tolm um et derryl; eth doss flenn et mesk ul o lern. Ul vodh doss et scaevureleth luthat, re vyskym o.*

*Laid by Halyna in the Second Tide, in two inks, by turns, from the archive rolls of the guild, which came up the mountain in the Stonewrights' chest. It is the stone leaf of the harvest, and the wood's leaf faces it; where the rolls are stained I have said so.*

Word for word: *Halyna laid [it] in the Second Tide, in two inks, the one and the other, from the rolls of the archive of the guild, which came up the mountain in the chest of the guild. It is the leaf of the stone about the harvest; and the leaf of the wood is in its face. Where the rolls are stained, I said it.*

- **Halyna are two, and take the dual**: *re hadha* (S·*cadh* + 3du *-A*), as IV.4's *Re wrodha Halyna*. *ul ba luth* and *hos eth ullen* are IV.4's own words for "in two inks" and "by turns" (O1894, O2858), reused exactly.
- *scaevureleth rytow*, "archive rolls": *scaevurel* is a roll ("a small wheel"), *rytow* the archive; the phrase is an addition (S06-09).
- *flenn et tolm* / *flenn et mesk*, "the stone's leaf" / "the wood's leaf": the Book's two kinds of leaf, as a pair (additions S06-05, S06-06). *um et derryl*, "about the harvest": *um* is "upon, concerning", what a thing is told upon. *El flenn et tolm um et X* is the headnotes' one formula for "It is the stone leaf of X" (III.1, IV.1, V.5; II.1 with its own second half). *(Merge fix: the first draft had* El flenn et tolm o, um et derryl*; the merge keeps one rendering of the recurring English, `wf8/merge_report.md`.)* *doss … ul o lern*, "is in its face", is IV.4's *doss o ul lern Yebb et Pard* with the pronoun.
- *re vyskym o*, "I said it": Seren's own first person; the stained places are marked in her ink (¶4).

### ¶1 · first ink

> Re vesken et trenn sy hy et scaevureleth. Nath re hossan o. Re hossant gedheth ol nedheth o, eth re rytent o, eth nath re veskent galat sa re rytent.

We read this from the rolls. We did not live it. Our fathers' fathers lived it, and wrote it down, and did not read what they had written.

Word for word: *We two read this tale from the rolls. We two did not live it. The fathers of our fathers lived it, and they cut it (wrote it), and they did not read the thing that they had written.*

- **The dual for the pair, the plural for the people.** Where Halyna speak of their own acts (reading, telling, judging) the verb is dual, as `orrowen_v2` §3.9 item 1 asks: *re vesken*, *nath re hossan* (1du *-An*). Where they speak of the guild or the Shore, it is plural: *ol nedheth*, *re ryter ol* (¶2), *ew ol sa re hyler* (¶8). The English "we" carries both; the Orrowen tells them apart.
- *gedheth ol nedheth*, "the fathers of our fathers": *ol* (+N) beds *gedheth* into *nedheth* (an addition, S06-10).
- *ryt* is "cut letters, write": the forefathers *cut* the rolls, and did not *read* (*vesk*) them.

### ¶2 · second ink

> Ul rytowath hemm sost et Stonwrytan doss et myst rytet hosen esket dommel ul gless et wadh, eth hy ull hosen nayalat veth pessyl ul vessvarath, sa re dhess nahos fedh. Vodh el et myst? Vodh doss ulo? Cedh hoss o? Re sennent et prassan sa re vessent, eth re yal begess ul lodh, eth hy ull re rarr et tessyl; eth amm re rarr o, nath re ryter ol o.

In the oldest archives of the guild the fog is written down as a blackish veil at the edge of the sea, and after that only as a question, asked in the night-watches and never answered. What is the grey? What does it keep? Who breathes it? The masters who asked were long dead before any answer came, and the answer, when it came, was not written by us.

Word for word: *In the oldest archives of the guild the grey is written as a blackish veil in the edge of the sea, and after that as nothing but a question in the night-watches, which no one ever answered. What is the grey? What is in it? Who breathes it? The masters who asked died, and an age lay between, and then the answer came; and when it came, we did not write it.*

- *hosen nayalat veth pessyl*, "as nothing but a question": "only" said as *nayalat veth*, the shape of I.1's *lo nayalat veth osk*.
- *sa re dhess nahos fedh*, "which no one ever answered", is the frame of the wood leaves' close, *Eth re dhess nahos* (O0563): the question the rolls asked is answered the way the wood's leaves are, by no one.
- **The questions.** The question word stands first and the verb follows unmutated: *Vodh el et myst?* "What is the grey?" "What does it keep?" is *Vodh doss ulo?*, "What is in it?": a question with *keth* ("what does it hold" / "what holds it") reads both ways.
- *eth re yal begess ul lodh*, "and an age lay between" (*ul lodh*, "in the mortar", is "between"): the English "were long dead before any answer came".
- *nath re ryter ol o*, "we did not write it": *ol* after the verb is the emphatic "we" (the Shore's, plural).

### ¶3 · first ink

> Hy ull re rarr molt syst, amm re yal lyrness um et Orrowan. Re yalant et cummath dyll, eth re yalant saevarath mest um so orrolath, eth re vesk niltenn et Stonwrytan hosen carm hy orlath nestorn, galat sa re vyskent et scaelurath. Ul surrath ull re orrant scetheth Orrow dem neld, hosen nath re werdent fedh, lo sest et myst; eth lo ull re weskent gannath domm et myst, sa re vollant et higileth Gannath Myst, hy vodh re hethent et myst dem susk, hosen re veskent, hosen kethent gannath crisur. Re stonant ul drenneth ul gless hyvenn sost et myst, susk, eth gann domm, eth venn hosen venndhrenn; eth re yal et mesk domm duss hy russvesk, eth lelv hy russtyvil, eth re vemess o lilvyl.

Then came the high summer of the Shorelanders' pride. The havens were rich and their roads were heavy with carts, and the counsel of the Stonewrights had come to seem a voice from humbler days, a thing the grandmothers said. In those years the ships of the coast went further out than they had ever dared, along the edge of the grey, and there they found the black pillars of the fog, which the ship-masters named the Mystholders, for they seemed to hold the grey up as posts hold up a roof. They stood in ranks at the fog's outermost edge, tall and dark-trunked and straight as a plumb-line, and their timber was harder than oak, lighter than iron, proof against rot.

Word for word: *Then came the heart of summer, when pride was upon the Shorelanders. The havens were rich, and carts were many upon their roads, and the counsel of the guild looked like a voice from humble days, a thing the grandmothers said. In those years the ships of the Shore went far out, as they had never dared, beside the grey; and there they found the black posts of the grey, which the ship-masters named the Posts of the Grey, for they held the grey up, as it seemed, as posts hold a roof. They stood in courses in the outermost edge of the grey, tall, and dark-trunked, and plumb as a plumb-line; and the black wood was harder than the hard-tree (oak), and lighter than the hard-metal (iron), and it warded off rot.*

- *molt syst*, "the heart of summer", for "the high summer" (an addition, S06-04), and pride said with the lexicon's idiom: *amm re yal lyrness um et Orrowan*, "when pride was upon the Shorelanders" (O0487).
- *Gannath Myst*, the Mystholders, is the canon's (`orrowen_v2` §4.7: "as posts hold up a roof"). In Orrowen the ship-masters' name and the axemen's word are one word, *gannath*, posts.
- *ul drenneth*, "in courses": the ranks are laid as a mason lays courses; *venn hosen venndhrenn*, "plumb as a plumb-line".
- *duss hy russvesk … lelv hy russtyvil*: oak is *dussvesk* "hard-tree" and iron *dusstyvil* "hard-metal", so the Orrowen says "harder than the hard-tree, lighter than the hard-metal". *hy* "than" softens *d* to *r*.
- *hosen nath re werdent fedh*, "as they had never dared" (*berd* softened: *b > w*).

### ¶4 · second ink

> Lo dhrenn pa dhesk re lusk Redul Serdul et dannwresketh dem neld. Kethent et scaevureleth et nydhyl, havul eth havul. Re yostant et gannath dem homarath et kethowath eth dem heskalstomornath. Re hadhant so ul sull heskal ul Gemm Hyll eth ul nannath Sedhnell, ul grisurvesketh Lurrvard, ul memmerdeth Dhaldow Helv sa re wemment et tolmath sa haemerent. Eth re hadhant so ul nalatath sa nath re vaverent so. Re yal vilvhellur lo vithul Dhavow Hemm, eth re hadh o et mesk domm ul o vressir. Re yaldant et tavowlesteth serdulyentheth hyo, eth nulesshrynireth. *[Lo sy doss et scaevurel luthat, eth doss sull trenn mammat.]* Re rann vaskorn ul Gemm Hyll hos gann ul hos lodull, eth re aedh o ulo, eth re yal lyrness umo.

For two generations the Trade Council sent out the axes. The rolls keep the count of it, cargo by cargo. They felled the pillars for the buttresses of fortresses and for bridge-beams. They put them into the three spans at Tidesmeet and the pilings of Fenholm, into the roof-trees of Holtward, into the cranes of Highreach that lifted the floating stones. And they put them into things that needed nothing of the kind. A lord of Eldhythe floored his dancing-hall with the black wood. The harbour-houses made their trade-tables of it, and their dice-boards. *[Here the roll is stained, and three lines are lost.]* A merchant of Tidesmeet had one pillar hewn into a single bed, and slept in it, and was proud.

Word for word: *Along two generations the Trade Council loosed the axes away. The rolls hold the count, cargo and cargo. They felled the posts for the buttresses of the keeps and for bridge-beams. They laid them in the three spans in Tidesmeet and in the pilings of Fenholm, in the roof-trees of Holtward, in the lifters of Highreach that lifted the stones that float. And they laid them in things that did not need them. A lord of Eldhythe had a dancing-hall, and he laid the black wood in its floor. The harbour-houses made trade-tables of it, and dice-boards. [Here the roll is stained, and three lines are lost.] A merchant in Tidesmeet hewed one post into one bed, and he slept in it, and pride was upon him.*

- *havul eth havul*, "cargo by cargo", is built as I.1's *hos eth hos* (an addition, S06-03).
- **The havens.** A haven's name softens as a possessor, as the pilot's *Corlen Dhavow*: *Dhaldow Helv* (< *Taldow Helv*, Highreach), *Dhavow Hemm* (< *Tavow Hemm*, Eldhythe); *Sedhnell* and *Lurrvard* do not change. Tidesmeet alone is said with *ul*, "in": *ul Gemm Hyll* (< *Cemm Hyll*), because its softened form *Hemm Hyll* is read as "old tide" and shares a word with Eldhythe (note 4).
- *ul nannath*, *ul grisurvesketh*, *ul memmerdeth*: *ul* beds *gannath*, *crisurvesketh* and *bemmerdeth* (the lexicon's crane is *bemmerd*, "lifter"; *stendor* ends in the banned *-dor*). *sa re wemment et tolmath sa haemerent*, "that lifted the stones that float". *Tolmath sa haemerent* is the Book's own epithet for Highreach's stones: S05 says "the tower of the floating stones" as *taldow et tolmath sa haemerent*. It now has a set-phrase row (addition S06-15). **(Back-translation fix.)** *Haemer* is "float" and also "hang", and this leaf uses it for "hang" three times (¶8, ¶11, IV.5 ¶2; ¶11's *Re haemerent et tolmath* is "the stones hung"). Without the row, the blind reader read "the cranes of Highreach that lifted the stonework that hangs there" and lost the floating stones.
- *Re yal vilvhellur lo vithul Dhavow Hemm, eth re hadh o et mesk domm ul o vressir*: "a dancing-hall was at a lord of Eldhythe, and he laid the black wood in its floor": the tongue has no "have" (§3.7) and no verb "to floor".
- **The stained place** is Seren's, in her one ink inside Halyna's two: *Lo sy doss et scaevurel luthat, eth doss sull trenn mammat*, "Here the roll is stained, and three lines are lost" (*luth* is ink and stain at once).
- *eth re yal lyrness umo*, "and pride was upon him": the merchant's pride in the same idiom as the Shore's in ¶3.

### ¶5 · first ink

> Kethent et scaevureleth nydhyl ullen lo sest ull, dyss hyo; eth re vesk nahos o lo et Redul. Ul vodh re yal gann gostat, re dhald gendan hy et brynt, venn eth domm; eth re orrant systeth merr, eth re ston gann tevel ul vodh re ston et gann hemm, eth re ston et myst pawast, virr, um gor lern et gann. Re vymment et gostardath dem et gendan, hy vodh re yal o lelv eth venn, eth gannath gell hyo; eth re lenull et Stonwrytan so dem brynur. Re stinent o, eth re dhev o; eth pawast re stinent o, eth pawast re dhev o; eth pawast re stinent o. Hy ull doss trenn lo yarl lyss saevul, lo visk nydhyl havul: Re hessent et brynteth tevyl, lo dhrenn et cless tunn. Re ryt nahos hy vodh, eth re vess nahos.

The rolls keep a stranger count beside that one, and no one at the Council read it. Where a pillar was felled, the stump put up new growth, straight and black, and in a few summers a young pillar stood where the old one had, and the grey stood close about it again. The axemen went back for the young growth, for it was light and straight and made good poles, and the guild bought them for scaffold. They cut it, and it grew, and they cut it again, and it grew again, and they cut it again. Then there is a line in a clerk's small hand at the foot of a cargo-list, that the stumps had stopped putting up growth, all along the edge. No one wrote down why, and no one asked.

Word for word: *The rolls hold another count beside that, stranger than it; and no one at the Council read it. Where a post was felled, new growth rose from the stump, plumb and black; and a few summers went by, and a young post stood where the old post had stood, and the grey stood again, near, on every face of the post. The axemen went back to the new growth, for it was light and plumb, and good poles came of it; and the guild bought them for scaffold. They cut it, and it grew; and again they cut it, and again it grew; and again they cut it. Then there is a line in a clerk's small hand, at the foot of a cargo-list: The stumps stopped growing, along the whole edge. No one wrote why, and no one asked.*

- **[Jack] The optional clause** (the Book's `OPTIONAL` marker, plan §14.5) is *eth re lenull et Stonwrytan so dem brynur*, "and the guild bought them for scaffold"; if it is declined, cut it and the semicolon before it, and nothing else moves.
- *re dhev o*, "it grew": *tev* is "a child" and "grow, sprout" (its root is \**taw-*), so the young pillars grow as children do. The cutting of the poles is *stin* (a stroke), not the felling *gost*.
- **The clerk's line is quoted**, as the pilot quotes the crowd's cries: Orrowen has no "that" before a clause.
- *lo yarl lyss saevul*, "by a clerk's small hand" (*garl* softened, as the canon's *pa yarl*); *lo visk nydhyl havul*, "at the foot of a cargo-list" (*misk* softened; *nydhyl havul* is the lexicon's cargo-list).

### ¶6 · second ink

> Fedh eth fedh re hyl et myst dem myntyth, hosen stykil ul sennyl. Re vymment et higileth dem varn, eth re vyskent: Doss et myst dem neld; ul morlath ol nedheth re yal virr. Re voll et Redul o: Pussal.

Season by season the fog thinned, like a dying enchantment. The ship-masters came home and said the grey stood further off than it had in their fathers' day. The Council named it fortune.

Word for word: *Season by season the grey turned toward thinness, like an enchantment in dying. The ship-masters came back home, and said: The grey is far off; in the days of our fathers it was near. The Council named it: Fortune.*

- *fedh eth fedh*, "season by season" (an addition, S06-02).
- *dem myntyth*, "toward thinness": *byntyth* ("narrowness, thinness", from *bynt* "narrow; fine, thin") bedded by *dem*. Unbedded, *dem byntyth* is read as the bedded *pyntyth*, "speed"; the analyzer took it so, and the bite was put right.
- *hosen stykil ul sennyl*, "like an enchantment in dying": *sennyl*, dying, is the Guest's third word.
- **The ship-masters' words are quoted**, so "their fathers' day" becomes *ul morlath ol nedheth*, "in the days of our fathers" (*ul* beds the vowel of *orlath*: *m-* before a broad vowel, as the pilot's *ul morrol*).
- *Re voll et Redul o: Pussal.* The name is given after a colon: *o pussal* would read "its fortune" (*o* softens, and *p* would have to show it).

### ¶7 · first ink

> Re veltir et Stonwrytan so. Doss o ul et scaevureleth, eth doss o ul ol narl sost: "Tevent et mesketh sy ul drun dyss. Lymment et ryteteth sa wesker lo so sest brodhen sa nath noss veskyl lor. Ul vodh naross poduloth, hessyth ol lestir." Veth re dhald serdul dem bithuloth, eth re yal et Stonwrytan nydhet hosen hemmeleth, eth re yal et peltir vesket ul garm hos fedh ul redulhellur Dhavow Hemm, eth cadhat um yenth.

The Stonewrights warned them. It is in the rolls, and it is in our own hand: "These trees grow in strange soil. The carvings we find beside them bear a tongue we cannot read. Where wisdom is absent, let restraint be our guide." But trade had become king, and the Stonewrights were reckoned relics, and the warning was read aloud once in the Council hall at Eldhythe and laid on a shelf.

Word for word: *The guild warned them. It is in the rolls, and it is in our own hand: "These trees grow in strange ground. The carvings that we find at their shoulder bear a tongue that we cannot read. Where wisdom [is] absent, restraint [is] our guide." But trade rose to kingship, and the guild was counted as relics, and the warning was read aloud once in the council-hall of Eldhythe, and laid on a shelf.*

- **The warning is a guild saying, verbless**: *Ul vodh naross poduloth, hessyth ol lestir*, "where wisdom absent, restraint our guide", in the shape of *Grest um hos, grest um vana* (an addition, S06-11). *hessyth*, restraint, is *hess* "stop" made abstract: the Guest's first word, which the guild wrote down before it was ever said at their door.
- *sa nath noss veskyl lor*, "that we cannot read": the "at" idiom of Seren's own line (*Nath noss tul kethyl … lo Halyna*).
- *ul ol narl sost*, "in our own hand" (*ol* beds *garl*); *re dhald serdul dem bithuloth*, "trade rose to kingship" (*pithul* "king, lord" + *-Oth*); *nydhet hosen hemmeleth*, "counted as relics"; *cadhat um yenth*, "laid on a shelf" (*genth* "table; shelf", softened: *g > y* before a vowel).

### ¶8 · second ink

Lo visketh et gannath domm re weskent et gostardath tolmath cadhat venn, eth umont re yal rellorath rytet sa re yal veskyl lo nahos, bynt eth kylet, rerr hosen frenn um risker. Ew peltireth so. Sesker ull amm sy. Re rytent et Mystaeri so ul so rellorath sost; eth lo sy eth lo ull, ul molt et rellorath, re raevulant rytyl et brodath orrowen hy so hunnat, virr hosen es morr so rellorath. Re yalant et hunnatath virr, eth et rellorath navenn. Re hadhant so lo ull, eth re oskant et tolm dem lymmyl et kaeveret, hosen lymm so vesk o. Veth lymm tolm nayalat. Nath noss brodlymmyl lo dholm. For there were carvings. At the feet of the black pillars the axemen found stones set upright, and on them were cut letters no one could read, fine and curling, as beautiful as frost on a window. They were warnings. We know that now. Word for word: *For there were carvings. At the feet of the black posts the axemen found stones set plumb, and upon them were signs cut that no one could read, fine and curling, fair as frost on a window. They were warnings. We know that now. The Mystaeri cut them in their own signs; and here and there, in the heart of the signs, they tried to cut the words of the Shore by their sound, as near as their signs would go. The sounds were near, and the signs false. They laid them there, and trusted the stone for the carrying of the meaning, as their wood carries it. But stone carries nothing. Stone cannot translate. The stones came to the cities fair and mute. Some were locked in the archives of the guild, and it was we who turned the key. The others were sold as curiosities, and hung in harbour-houses, on the wall: holy pleas, turned into ornament; and men drank under the stones, and reckoned their trade.*

- **Their letters are *rellorath*, "signs"**: the Shore has only its own *tolmath* (stones, letters) and calls what it cannot read signs. "The symbols wrong" is the same word, *et rellorath navenn*.
- *rytyl et brodath orrowen*, "cutting the words of the Shore": "our own words" would be *ol mrodath*, an onset the tongue does not allow (§2.5); this is IV.4's own way round it (*ul et brodath orrowen*).
- *virr hosen es morr so rellorath*, "as near as their signs would go": *es* beds the vowel of *orr* (*m-* before a broad vowel), and *es* in a past frame is "would", as in IV.4.
- *lymm* / *brodlymm*: "Stone carries nothing. Stone does not translate" is *Veth lymm tolm nayalat. Nath noss brodlymmyl lo dholm.* To translate is *brodlymm*, "to word-carry", so the second sentence takes up the first. It is said with the "at" idiom, "translating is not at stone" ("cannot"), because *nath* would bed *brodlymm* into *mrodlymm* (an addition, S06-12).
- *eth ew ol sa re hyler et roskur*, "and it was we who turned the key": the cleft, with the relative agreeing (1pl), as the pilot's *sa re hether ol ryteth*.
- *pessyleth peness kylet dem rerrel*, "holy pleas turned into ornament" (*kyl*, turn, change).

### ¶9 · first ink

> Hy ull re veltir et Stonwrytan, eth hy ull re hadh o et peltir lo sest et tolmath sa nath re yal veskyl lont. El ol saed ull. Nath gadhan o tunn um et Redul.

So the Stonewrights warned, and then filed the warning away with the stones they could not read. That is our share of it. We will not lay it all on the Council.

Word for word: *So the guild warned, and then it laid the warning by the stones that they could not read. That is our share. We two do not lay it whole upon the Council.*

- *re hadh o et peltir*: *cadh* is "lay" and "file away, lay by": the guild lays its warning by as a mason lays a stone by.
- *El ol saed ull*, "That is our share" (the guild's, plural); *Nath gadhan o tunn um et Redul*, "we two do not lay it whole upon the Council": the tellers' own judgement, in the dual. The English "will not" is said as a present, as Kael's *nath gadhom o los*: the negative future is not defined.

### ¶10 · second ink

> Re hess et derryl lo rask, nel hy voduloth, veth hy hylyth uldath. Re yalant gannath lo sest et Orrow merr, eth re yalant et farrolath sell, eth re rarr mesk naryll dem vimm hy wrunneleth et bridlern, eth re vamm et Redul maver dem et mesk domm. Hy ull re hessent et dannwresketh. Re naelur nahos o.

The harvest ended at last, not by wisdom but by the fickleness of men. The pillars near the coast grew scarce and the voyages grew long, and cheaper timber came down from the northern hills, and the Council lost its taste for the black wood. So the axes stopped. No one decided it.

Word for word: *The harvest stopped at the end, not from wisdom, but from the fickleness of men. The posts beside the Shore were few, and the voyages were long, and cheap wood came down from the hills of the north, and the Council lost [its] want for the black wood. So the axes stopped. No one chose it.*

- *nel hy voduloth, veth hy hylyth uldath*, "not from wisdom, but from the fickleness of men": the guild's word *Nel et hesp, veth et tolm* in shape, two abstracts in *-Oth* set against each other. *kylyth* is the shared lexicon's own word for the Book's "fickleness" (O1134, *kyl* + *-Oth*). *(Merge fix, `wf8/merge_report.md`: the first draft had* hylyl uldath*, "the turning of men", addition S06-08, a second form for a sense the lexicon already carried.)*
- *re vamm et Redul maver*, "the Council lost want": the bare noun, because *o vaver* ("its want") after *et Redul* reads as a construct with an article on its head, the Last Carver's error (the analyzer says so).
- *Re naelur nahos o*, "no one chose it": *naelur* "choose, decide".

### ¶11 · first ink

> Hy ull re rarr et nadhumol. Nath re sesk et vadullan galat sa re nayald. Nath re seskent so dhevath. Re dhum Orrowan et orlath dasken nayalat sost. Re haemerent et tolmath ul davowlesteth, um et hald, eth re dhevent et tevath ul vimm et tolmath, eth re vessent: Vodh myskent? Eth re yal mysket lont: Myskent nayalat.

Then came the forgetting. The forebears had not known what they destroyed. Their children would not know. The Shorelanders of the final days remembered nothing at all. The stones hung on the walls of the harbour-houses, and the children grew up beneath them and asked what they said, and were told they said nothing.

Word for word: *Then came the forgetting. The forebears did not know the thing that they destroyed. Their children did not know. The Shorelanders of the final days remembered nothing at all ("nothing itself"). The stones hung in harbour-houses, on the wall, and the children grew up under the stones, and asked: What do they say? And it was said to them: They say nothing.*

- *nadhumol*, "the forgetting", is *nadhum*, "un-remember": the Title's *tumol* with *na-*.
- *re dhevent et tevath*, "the children grew": *tev* twice, child and growing, as in ¶5.
- "would not know" is said as the plain negative past (*Nath re seskent*); the tongue's "would" (*es* in a past frame) is not negated anywhere in the canon.
- **The children's question and its answer are quoted**: *Vodh myskent?* "What do they say?" *Myskent nayalat.* "They say nothing." (*mysk* is used unmutated, as the pilots advise.)

### ¶12 · second ink

> Desir, desir sost, re hyl scaevur lyrness. Re yalant lesteth Vardh ul et cummath clennet. Re yystent uldath lusulath hellurath et Stonwrytan pawast, eth re vessent niltenn et Stonwrytan, eth re yal o hebbet. Veth re rarr nestornoth desir. Re yalant et wemmeth domm hosast tevar um et wadh.

Late, very late, the wheel of pride turned. The temples of the havens were mended. Men climbed the steps of the guild-halls again and asked the Stonewrights' counsel, and it was given. But humility came too late. The first dark sails were already on the sea.

Word for word: *Late, very late, the wheel of pride turned. The temples in the havens were mended. Men climbed the steps of the halls of the guild again, and asked the counsel of the guild, and it was given. But humility came after its time. The first dark sails were already upon the sea.*

- *scaevur lyrness*, "the wheel of pride"; *scaevur* is also the roll's root (*scaevurel*, "a small wheel"): the same wheel that turns is the one the rolls are wound on.
- "too late" is *desir*, which is "late, after the time" (its lexicon sense): humility came after its time.
- *re yystent* (*gyst* "climb" softened, *g > y* before *y*), as the pilot's *re yyst*.

### The close (no ink of its own: the Legends builder sets the laid formula apart, outside the alternation · merge)

> Cadhan o hosen re yal cadhat lona.

This we lay as it was laid for us.

Word for word: *We two lay it as it was laid at us two.*

- **The hearth formula in the dual.** The frame is the lexicon's (*Cadhom o hosen re yal cadhat lom*, O0238, the pilot's), reused word for word; only the person changes, to the pair's 1du (*cadhan … lona*), because §3.9 item 1 says Halyna's plural is a dual in the original. The lexicon's 1pl row (O0234, *Cadhar … lor*) is what common speech would say. **[integrator]** The same line closes I.4, II.1, II.3 and both parts of III.1; see note 1.

### The hearth's answer (Seren's ink)

> *Eth re vysk et odh:* **Tumar.**

*And the hearth said: We remember.*

- The canon formula and the canon yes, unchanged (O0564; *Tumar*, Jack's note 2).


**The facing leaf.** IV.1's wood leaf is IV.2, *The Axe in the Grain* (*Dannwresk ul Meskdhrenn*, O0360), a wood leaf of another unit: its knowing is carved in the grain there, with Seren's headnote in Orrowen.

## IV.5 · THE BURNING BOAT (*SULTER SA VORR*)

A stone leaf, laid by Voss, in Seren's one ink. Voss is wry: he gives his grandmother's tale and turns it on the listeners (*hy nahos sa oskos*, "from no one you trust"; *Gor fedh re nadhumar gell*, "we have always forgotten well"; *cedh brodyorr ul lern ryt?*, "who word-fights against a cutting?").

### Title

> ## *Sulter sa vorr*

**IV.5 · The Burning Boat**

Word for word: *The boat that burns.*

- The lexicon's title (O2425), unchanged; the pilot's *Harl Orrow sa Vorr* ("of the Burning Shore") is built on it.

### Seren's headnote (her one ink)

> *Re hadh Kesterd Voss, Voss sa re vess, ul Hyll Pawast, hosen re wrodh scaelur Voss o ul davowlesteth Dhavow Hemm, ul vodh re heth ey et vorr eth re wamm ey et braemur. El trenn o sa nath gadhant et higileth, eth vell sa nath hessent et tevath vellyl. Re dhyvull et Orrow umo, eth nath re dhyvull et wadh. Doss flenn et mesk ul o lern.*

*Laid by Corporal Voss, Voss who asked, in the Second Tide, as his grandmother told it in the harbour-houses of Eldhythe, where she kept the fire and drew the ale. It is a tale the ship-masters would not tell and a rhyme the children would not stop singing. The shore laughed at it, and the sea did not. The wood's leaf faces it.*

Word for word: *Corporal Voss laid [it], Voss who asked, in the Second Tide, as Voss's grandmother told it in the harbour-houses of Eldhythe, where she kept the fire and drew the ale. It is a tale that the ship-masters do not tell, and a rhyme that the children do not stop singing. The Shore laughed at it, and the sea did not laugh. The leaf of the wood is in its face.*

- *Re hadh Kesterd Voss, Voss sa re vess*: I.1's headnote shape (*Re hadh Crennel Kael, Kael Nydherd*), the rank before the name (*Kesterd Voss*, an addition, S06-13) and the canon's own epithet after it (*Voss sa re vess*, O2790).
- *scaelur Voss*, "Voss's grandmother": after a verb, *o scaelur* would read "he … grandmother" (§3.5: *s* never softens), so the possessor is said as a name.
- *ul davowlesteth Dhavow Hemm*, "in the harbour-houses of Eldhythe": *tavow* twice, once bedded by *ul*, once softened as the possessor. The harbour and the haven are the same word; a Rivenman hears the chime.
- *eth nath re dhyvull et wadh*, "and the sea did not laugh": Orrowen's yes and no; the verb is said again.
- *ul vodh re heth ey et vorr eth re wamm ey et braemur*: she *kept* (*keth*, hold) the fire and *drew* (*bamm*, pull) the ale.

### ¶1

> Hebb et tolm lom. Nel en drenn sy. El trenn en scaelur, eth re yal o loy hy et farrardath, eth re yal o lont hy nahos sa oskos.

Give me the stone. This one is not mine. It is my grandmother's, and she had it from the sailors, and they had it from no one who could be trusted.

Word for word: *Give the stone at me. This is not my tale. [It] is my grandmother's tale, and it was at her from the sailors, and it was at them from no one that you trust.*

- *Hebb et tolm lom* is the pilot's (O0897), word for word.
- *Nel en drenn sy*: *en* beds *trenn*, so "my tale" is plain to hear.
- Having is the "at" idiom: *re yal o loy*, "it was at her" (she had it).
- **Voss's wryness**: *hy nahos sa oskos*, "from no one you trust", turns the English "no one who could be trusted" on the listeners, as Voss does.

### ¶2

> Re heth ey et vorr ul davowlest lo Dhavow Hemm, um et tavow, et lonn sa re yal genth sell eth crisur vimm lo, ul vodh re vellorant et higileth hy et helvnynth. Ul susk et vorr re yal sull tolm ryss myst cadhat ul et hald, eth rellorath kylet umont, bynt hosen miskryteth nasath um rull thenth, eth re sesk nahos vodh re vyskent. Re haemerent lo ull hy orlath ey scaelur sost. Re runnant et higileth mygurath um et tolm vimm sost.

She kept the fire in a harbour-house on the quay at Eldhythe, the kind with a long table and a low roof, where the ship-masters came in out of the rain. Over the fire there were three flat grey stones set into the wall, with curling letters on them, fine as the tracks of birds on wet sand, and no one knew what they said. They had hung there since her own grandmother's day. The ship-masters knocked out their pipes on the lowest one.

Word for word: *She kept the fire in a harbour-house at Eldhythe, on the quay, the kind that had a long table and a low roof at it, where the ship-masters came in from the rain. Above the fire there were three flat grey stones laid in the wall, and curling signs upon them, fine as the tracks of birds upon wet sand, and no one knew what they said. They had hung there from the days of her grandmother herself. The ship-masters knocked pipes on the lowest stone.*

- **Not *tolm myst*.** *Tolm Myst* is the canon's Myststone (`orrowen_v2` §4.7), the wood the Book's reader learns to mistake for stone. The warning stones are true stone (IV.1: "stone carries nothing"), so they are *tolm ryss myst*, "flat grey stone", every time, and never the two words side by side.
- *rellorath kylet*, "curling signs", as in IV.1 ¶8; *um rull thenth*, "upon wet sand" (*grull* softened: the *g* drops before a consonant, §2.5).
- *et lonn sa re yal … lo*, "the kind that had … at it": the relative with the preposition left behind (the pilot's G5).
- *Re runnant*, "they knocked": *drunn* softened, *dr- > r-* (lexicon §4.3). The ship-masters knock *pipes*: "their pipes" (*so vygurath*) after *et higileth* reads as a construct with an article on its head.

### ¶3

> Re yal ludal um ol nedheth hy et wadh lunnat. Re hethent et Orrow, eth et wadhdhrenneth cummen ul lodh et cummath, eth amm re orr sceth dem neld eth re vesk cless vyst, re vymm o dem varn pynt. Re vollant et farrardath o: Mystow. Fedheth re orr berderd virr, eth re hyl o, eth re vellor o ul lest en scaelur, eth trenn dend lo, eth re fimm o umo hos dynd. Re raevar ey nahos hyont. Re vysk ey: Kyl Mystow uldath venn dem nyculardath. Eth ew et galat hosel sa re vysk ey fedh ul lern et wadh.

Our fathers feared the open sea. They kept to the shores and to the sheltered roads between the havens, and when a ship went out far enough to see the fog-bank it came home quickly. The sailors called it the Mystlands. Now and then some bold soul went close, and turned back, and came into my grandmother's house with a wild story, and drank on it for a week. She believed none of them. She said the Mystlands made liars of honest men, and it was the only thing she ever said against the sea.

Word for word: *Fear was upon our fathers from the open sea. They held to the Shore, and to the haven-like sea-lanes between the havens, and when a ship went far out and saw a fog-bank, it came back home quickly. The sailors named it: the Mystlands. Sometimes a bold one went near, and turned back, and came into my grandmother's house, and a wild tale at him, and he drank on it one week. She believed none of them. She said: The Mystlands change true men into liars. And it was the only thing she ever said against the sea.*

- *Re yal ludal um ol nedheth*, "fear was upon our fathers": the lexicon's idiom for fear (O0485), and *ol nedheth* as in IV.1.
- *Mystow* is the Orrowen behind Jack's "the Mystlands" (`orrowen_v2` §4.7), kept.
- *berderd*, "a bold one, a hero" (O0072): "some bold soul", with Voss's shrug in it.
- *eth trenn dend lo*, "and a wild tale at him": a clause with no verb, laid beside the others, as the pilot's *eth so wresketh ul so simmeth*. (*lo dhrenn dend* was tried and read "along a wild road": *lo dhrenn* is "along".)
- *Kyl Mystow uldath venn dem nyculardath*: "The Mystlands change true men into liars", the grandmother's words quoted.

### ¶4

> Veth re yal hos trenn sa nath re hadhant et higileth, eth re wrodh ey o lom mest hy et ullen; hy ull kaeverym re raevar ey o saed.

But there was one story the ship-masters would not tell, and she told it to me more than all the others, so I think she half believed it.

Word for word: *But there was one tale that the ship-masters did not tell, and she told it to me more than the others; so I think she believed it, half (she half believed it).*

- *re raevar ey o saed*, "she believed it, half": *saed* stands after the object as an adverb (the bare quality after the verb, lexicon §5.3), and §3.5 is what makes it work: after a verb, *o saed* reads "it, half", never "its half". "Half believed" is a degree, not a portion, so it takes no possessor. **(Back-translation fix.)** The first draft had *re raevar ey saed et trenn*, "she believed half the tale", which the blind reader read back as "she believed half of it": a portion of the tale, where Voss means she was half convinced.
- *mest hy et ullen*, "more than the others": *gor ullen* was tried and read *gor* + S·*rhullen*.

### ¶5

> Re rarr o hy uldath et deldvar hyvenn sost, et hos lo rask et gaed. Re rytent lo so nunthath: Re hastar sulter hy et dhysal hos fedh, ul vorr, eth uld sennet ulo; eth re lymm et hyll o dem neld; eth amm re yal o ul neld, re hyl o. Re hyl o ul lern et helvhoss eth ul lern et kess; nath noss kylyl hosen sy lo sulter. Eth re orr o dem neld ul et myst, ul vorr tul, eth ul scyndalol somm re orr o. Re rytent um et scyndal mest hy gor yalat. Re vyskent: Doss hunnol et scyndal los, amm doss et vorr ul neld veskyl.

It came from the men of the outermost outpost, the one at the end of the land. They swore in their cups that they had once pushed a boat off the shingle burning, with a dead man in it, and that the tide took it out, and that when it was well out it turned. It turned into the wind and against the current, which no boat can do, and went away into the grey still burning, and screaming as it went. They swore to the scream above all. They said you could hear it after the fire was too far off to see.

Word for word: *It came from the men of the outermost outpost, the one at the end of the dry land. They swore in their cups: We pushed a boat from the shingle once, alight, and a dead man in it; and the tide carried it away; and when it was beyond, it turned. It turned against the wind and against the current; no boat can turn so. And it went away into the grey, still alight, and screaming while it went. They swore upon the scream more than upon anything. They said: You can hear the scream when the fire is beyond seeing.*

- **The oath is quoted**, in the outpost men's own "we" (*Re hastar*); they swear *lo so nunthath*, "at their cups" (an addition, S06-14). A drunken oath has no *Ston.*: the pilot's rule closes an oath that is sworn, not one that is retold.
- *ul lern et helvhoss eth ul lern et kess*, "against the wind and against the current": *ul lern* is "in the face of".
- *nath noss kylyl hosen sy lo sulter*, "turning so is not at a boat": "which no boat can do", in the "at" idiom.
- *Re rytent um et scyndal*, "they swore upon the scream": the oath's own verb and preposition (*Rytom um …*).
- *ul neld veskyl*, "beyond seeing" (an addition, S06-07), for "too far off to see".

### ¶6

> Re dhyvull et Orrow um ull, eth re dhyvull en scaelur eth, eth re runn ey mygur um et tolm ryss myst ul susk et vorr. Nath re dhyvullant et higileth. Nath re wrodhant um ull, hy vodh el pussal dath o, amm doss et wadh virr; eth re veskym higil sa re hadh et nunth, eth re orr hyo ul helvnynth, hy vodh nath re vaver o hunnol et trenn.

The shore laughed at that, and my grandmother laughed with it, and knocked out her pipe on the grey stone over the fire. The ship-masters did not laugh. They would not speak of it, for it is ill luck anywhere near the sea, and I have seen a master set down his cup and go out into the rain rather than hear it told.

Word for word: *The Shore laughed at that, and my grandmother laughed too, and knocked her pipe on the flat grey stone above the fire. The ship-masters did not laugh. They did not speak of it, for it is ill luck when the sea is near; and I have seen a ship-master who set down the cup and went out into the rain, because he did not want to hear the tale.*

- *Nath re dhyvullant et higileth*: the yes-and-no rule again, the verb said back against the Shore's laughing.
- *el pussal dath o*, "it is ill luck": the copula puts the predicate first; *o* stands last, where it cannot soften *pussal*.
- "rather than hear it told" is *hy vodh nath re vaver o hunnol et trenn*, "because he did not want to hear the tale": the pilot's *Nath re vaver o neskyl* shape.
- *sa re hadh et nunth*, "who set down the cup". **(Back-translation fix; a rule fix, not a misreading.)** The first draft had *sa re hadh o nunth*, "his cup". But *n* does not soften, so after a verb §3.5 reads *o nunth* as "it … cup", not "his cup". The blind reader got "his cup" only by overriding the rule, and ¶4's *o saed* depends on that rule holding.

### ¶7

> Hy ull re hunnom o hy sull higil, lo vorrath ul hiskenneth, et deld sost hy dhantnynth sa re rarrant fedh. Re vesk gor hos sost o, ell re farr o lo sest uld sa re vesk o; eth re yalant so wrodholath hosen: sulter lyss ul vorr ul neld ul et myst, ul vodh nath noss vorr fedh, ul gylyl ul lern et helvhoss; eth hy ull, helv. Myskent: Lymm o et helv dath. Myskent: El sulter o sa rarr demoth amm sennith. Dhyvull et Orrow, hy vodh nafedh es narr o dem et Orrow. Nath dhyvullant et higileth. Eth re yal o rytet lo et hinnarath: Ew nayalat o. Besk hespet, hurrat. Sulter vorrat, luskat. Ew gor yalat et rytyl ull; eth cedh brodyorr ul lern ryt?

I have heard it since from three of them, at fires in the passes, as far from salt water as they ever came. Each had seen it himself, or sailed with a man who had, and their tellings were all the same: a small boat burning far out in the grey, where no fire should be, turning into the wind; and after it, weather. They say it brings the foul weather. They say it is the boat that comes for you when you die. The shore laughs, because the shore will never have to meet it. The ship-masters do not laugh. And the cities had it in writing that it was nothing: a stranger come armed, and put down, and his boat burned and set adrift. That was all the writing said, and who argues with writing?

Word for word: *Afterward I heard it from three ship-masters, at fires in the passes, the farthest from salt-water that they ever came. Each one himself saw it, or sailed at the shoulder of a man who saw it; and their tellings were alike: a small boat alight, beyond, in the grey, where fire never is, turning against the wind; and then, weather. They say: It brings the foul weather. They say: It is a boat that comes to you when you die. The Shore laughs, for it will never come to the Shore. The ship-masters do not laugh. And it was written at the cities: It was nothing. A stranger, armed; killed. A boat burned; set adrift. That was the whole of the writing; and who word-fights against a cutting?*

- **Weather is *helv*, the sky**: "and after it, weather" is *eth hy ull, helv*, and the foul weather is *et helv dath*.
- *Dhyvull et Orrow, hy vodh nafedh es narr o dem et Orrow*, "the Shore laughs, for it will never come to the Shore": the English "because the shore will never have to meet it", turned so that *o* is the boat. (*es gemm et Orrow o* was tried; *et Orrow o* reads as a construct.)
- **The cities' writing is cut dry.** *Besk hespet, hurrat. Sulter vorrat, luskat.* "A stranger, armed; killed. A boat burned; set adrift.": no verbs, no small words, as a record is cut (the stone grammar, `orrowen_v2` §3.11). The English "put down" is *hurrat*, "killed"; the record is plain where Voss's English is soft.
- *cedh brodyorr ul lern ryt?*: "who argues with writing?" is "who word-fights (*brodyorr*) against a cut word (*ryt*)?": Voss's joke, word against stone.

### ¶8

> Veth re yal tevvell umo lo et tevath. Nath seskym cedh re hadh o. Re hunnerer um et tavow lo wunth somm veller o, pa dhev ul gylyl eth hos ul hunneryl; eth re haeverer: El et cemmyl et galat dyss; hy vodh cedh re hunn fedh cemmyl hosen ull?

But the children had a rhyme for it. I do not know who made it. We skipped to it on the quay with a rope, two turning and one jumping, and we thought the strange part was the greeting, for who ever heard of such a greeting as that:

Word for word: *But the children had a rhyme about it. I do not know who laid it. We skipped on the quay with a rope while we sang it, two children turning and one skipping; and we thought: The strange thing is the greeting; for who ever heard a greeting like that?*

- *tevvell*, "a children's rhyme" (O2529); *cedh re hadh o*, "who laid it": a rhyme, too, is laid.
- *pa dhev ul gylyl eth hos ul hunneryl*, "two children in turning and one in skipping": the dual softens *tev*; the progressive is *ul* + the verbal noun.
- *cemmyl* is "a meeting, a greeting" (O0261).

### The rhyme (*Sulter Myst*, the Grey Boat)

> *Sulter myst, sulter myst, hy et myst,*  
> *re lumm lo yebb et pard hos ryst;*  
> *stinel dem ninth, tolm dem hebbet,*  
> *eth cemmyl hosen sy, nahunnat!*  
> *Vorra o, hasta o, hebba o dem hyll;*  
> *veth re harm sulter myst, eth re hyl.*  

> *Grey boat, grey boat, out of the grey,*  
> *knelt at the warden's door one day;*  
> *a stone for a present, a cut for a kiss,*  
> *and who ever heard of a greeting like this?*  
> *Burn it and shove it and give it the tide;*  
> *but the grey boat turned, and the grey boat cried.*

Word for word:

> *Grey boat, grey boat, out of the grey,*  
> *knelt at the warden's door one morning;*  
> *a cut for a kiss, a stone for a gift,*  
> *and a greeting like this, unheard-of!*  
> *Burn it, shove it, give it to the tide;*  
> *but the grey boat cried out, and turned.*  

- **The rhyme's name is the lexicon's** (*Sulter Myst*, O2424). It rhymes in couplets, as the English does, each couplet in its own way:
- *myst · ryst*: a true rhyme. **[Jack]** "one day" is *hos ryst*, "one morning". The Guest came "in the morning, in a small grey boat, out of the grey" (IV.3), so the rhyme is true to the tale where the English is vague; *hos orl*, "one day", would not rhyme.
- *hebbet · nahunnat*: a **harmony rhyme**. The two words end in the same suffix, *-At*, in its slender and its broad shape; the leaf-hand writes both with the same two letters (A t), so the rhyme is exact to the eye and near to the ear. To get it the halves of line 3 change places ("a cut for a kiss, a stone for a gift"), and line 4's question becomes an exclamation: *eth cemmyl hosen sy, nahunnat!*, "and a greeting like this, unheard-of!" (*nahunnat*, O1543). Voss's own prose keeps the question (¶8).
- *hyll · re hyl*: "tide" and "turned" differ only by the long *l*: the boat turns against the tide in the rhyme's own sound. To end on it, the last line says "cried out, and turned" where the English says "turned, and cried".
- *stinel dem ninth*, "a cut for a kiss": *dem* beds the slender vowel of *inth* into *n-* (§3.3), as *ul* beds *orrol* into *morrol*.
- *Vorra o, hasta o, hebba o*: the plural imperative, the shore-folk shouting to each other; *re harm*, "cried out" (*carm* softened).
- The metre is the skipping rope's: four beats to a line, first-syllable stress; the refrain *sulter myst* stands where the English has "grey boat".

### ¶9 (optional, with the couplet)

> Re yal pa dhrenn ullen. Nath re veller so um et tavow, hy vodh amm re hunn higil so, re rik o et bunth hy ol narlath, eth nath re vysk o hy vodh:

Word for word: *There were two other lines. We did not sing them on the quay, for if a ship-master heard them, he took the rope out of our hands, and did not say why:*

- **[Jack] The optional couplet** (the Book's `OPTIONAL` marker, plan §14.6): this paragraph and the two lines below it go together; if the couplet is declined, cut both, and nothing else moves.
- *pa dhrenn ullen*, "two other lines" (the dual softens *trenn*; the adjective after a dual is softened too, and *ullen* begins with a vowel). "he would take the rope" is the plain past, *re rik o*: *es* + *grik* would bed to *nrik*.

### The ship-masters' couplet (optional)

> *Sulter myst ul vorr, eth helvnynth ul sestow;*  
> *eth nath mymm dem varn et hos sa rarr o demo.*  

> *Grey boat burning, and after, the rain;*  
> *and the one it comes for will not come home again.*

Word for word:

> *Grey boat alight, and the rain behind;*  
> *and the one it comes to does not come back home.*  

- *sestow · demo*: a vowel rhyme on two-syllable endings (*SES-tow*, *DE-mo*), in the rhyme's metre. *ul sestow*, "behind, after" ("in the back"), is the lexicon's; *helvnynth*, rain, is "sky-water".
- *et hos sa rarr o demo*, "the one that it comes to": the relative with *demo* ("to him") left behind, as G5. *nath mymm dem varn*, "does not return home": *mymm* is "return, come back", and carries the English "again".

### ¶10

> Re hunnerym lo dhevvell ull amm re yal dhom surr lom. Re hyrrilym o amm re yal pa vurr surr lom, um et dhysal ul vimm et hald sy, et fedh hosast sa re hunnom scethel ul vorr. Hy vodh re scyndal o. Re scyndal o somm re orr o, hosen re rytent uldath et deldvar, eth re hylent et farrardath hemm um et hald hyo; eth re stonom ul dhidh, eth brodow lunnat lom, eth et vell ul gylyl ulom, eth re haeverym um sull tolm ryss myst ul susk vorr en scaelur, eth um et mygurath ul drunnol umont.

I skipped to that rhyme at seven. I understood it at forty, on the shingle under this wall, the first time I heard one of their hulls burn. For it screamed. It screamed as it went, the way the outpost men had sworn, and the old sailors on the wall turned their faces from it; and I stood in the smoke with my mouth open and the rhyme going round in my head, and I thought of the three grey stones over my grandmother's fire, and the pipes knocking on them.

Word for word: *I skipped to that rhyme when seven years were at me. I understood it when forty years were at me, on the shingle under this wall, the first time that I heard a hull alight. For it screamed. It screamed while it went, as the men of the outpost swore, and the old sailors on the wall turned from it; and I stood in the smoke, and a mouth open at me, and the rhyme turning in me, and I thought of the three flat grey stones above my grandmother's fire, and of the pipes knocking upon them.*

- **Age is the "at" idiom**: *amm re yal dhom surr lom*, "when seven years were at me"; forty is *pa vurr*, "two twenties" (the Shore counts in twenties).
- *lo dhevvell ull*, "to that rhyme": *lo vell* was read as S·*pell*, "spire" (the pilot's *Pellow Vell* homophone), so the children's word *tevvell* is used, softened.

- *re hylent … hyo*, "they turned from it": "their faces" is left in the turning (*so lerneth* after *et hald* reads as a construct).

### 4. Tidesmeet in construct [Jack]

*Cemm Hyll* (Tidesmeet), softened as a possessor, is *Hemm Hyll*. That reads "old tide" (the analyzer glosses it so, and shows no name), and its first word is the second word of Eldhythe, *Tavow Hemm*, which sits two sentences away in IV.1 ¶4. The leaf therefore says Tidesmeet with *ul*: *sull heskal ul Gemm Hyll*, "the three spans in Tidesmeet", and *vaskorn ul Gemm Hyll*, "a merchant in Tidesmeet". The other havens keep the construct (*Dhaldow Helv*, *Dhavow Hemm*, *Sedhnell*, *Lurrvard*). If Jack would rather keep "of Tidesmeet" as a construct, it is *Hemm Hyll* in both places, and nothing else moves.
