## The wood's telling (the grain, 24 rings; bands I = 1–4, II = 5–19, III = 20–24)

- **r1.** [Root: GROW, the growing.] We were grown; we were not built. Stone is cut with iron and laid down; then it lies there, and does not live.
- **r2.** The long-lived (we remember them always) guided the groves. The trees lived (breathed) in the grey, and the long-lived lived by the trees' breath; the two lived one life. A hull is only a tree, which the long-lived guided along a long road.
- **r3.** The long-lived sang to the groves, so that there would be hulls. The trees bent to the singing, because nothing was ever asked of them but song. Their living trunks became hulls. The roots, which fed the trees, became keels; and the saplings, trained by windsong through many summers, became masts.
- **r4.** None of the long-lived sails in us (goes on the water in us). Every hull lives with the grove's life; it is wood.
- **r5.** With no one of us aboard, how does a hull obey? By a carving. The council chooses one Standard-Bearer for all the hulls (of a fleet). It carves into its heart, which is named the Heartwood (*Saenvael*), and then the Standard-Bearer carves in all the hulls, through the bond of roots (*Aelralen*). So we turned the bond of roots into a leash.
- **r6.** The hulls obey the carving as a hand obeys the mind, because hull and carving are one wood. The wood obeys the wood (obeys itself). In the dark, while the shore lays its stones, the Standard-Bearer carves the morrow.
- **r7.** The carving speaks; it does not ask. In the dread, every hull keeps its own carving. (But) a hull may balk at burning water; it may rage where it bled. The Standard-Bearer is one mind; the hulls are many minds.
- **r8.** The council chose the Standard-Bearers in grief, and that grief is in their hearts. We take the many names (that you give). Green wood has no heart yet. Ironbark does not strike; it comes at you again, heavier. Willow does not strike first; it turns aside. Blackthorn goes where the loss is greatest (in the dark). Heartoak never turns aside. Twistbough never makes the same feint twice. Yew waits, then burns, then waits again. Ashwood sends everything at once. Silverbark holds (against) you most, and bends least.
- **r9.** The greenest (crudest) hulls came to you first, because of the law of the Tides; no council, not all of it together, carved that law. A hull of one season holds one carving; a hull of many summers holds two words; a shore-man's life holds a sentence, and a memory in it; the eldest black pillars hold a judgement.
- **r10.** The council needed the shore silenced, and swiftly; and green wood grows quickest. So hands in anger carved single signs into wood of one summer. All those hulls lay at the water's edge on your shore, sapling after sapling. The shore-men's hulls went through the grey past them all and did not look (or: unseen).
- **r11.** For many lives all the hulls went over the water to your havens, each carved with one carving: *Burn* (cited). Your havens had no walls for war. At the last, green wood followed you into the mountains; and there, in the house behind the stone, one shore-man struck the first blow at it.
- **r12.** Every Tide grew whole and then went, as a tide goes over the water. We call them Tides. Leathae, the Hasty, was green. The second, named *Vaelthae*, had heart(wood). The roots taught Ralenthae, the Tide of Remembering. (Then) we: Aelthae, the Joined Tide. Rhenthae grew, and Eirthae grew; what they carved, we do not hold.
- **r13.** Hulls were grown for every shore of the sea and for every land: to break walls; to carry fire, and send it through your walls; to strike from beneath, where the shore rises. One goes on roots through sand and stone; one goes through burning rock, on the water, in a skin of bark; one goes in the white.
- **r14.** The root-men were grown because we would not give the long-lived (to it). A hull's keel is a root that feeds a tree. The fine roots find the road in the dark. They ride in the deep hulls. Some come to you again and again: the heavy slowly, the light all at once. They walk, they dig, they carry fire. You call them root-men.
- **r15.** They hold only one carving: (to) the house behind the stone, then its guns, then its wall. They go to the weakest stone, as a root goes to a crack. Where they crowd, they knot, four to a knot; and a knot strikes as four. Some only reach the wall and leave it chequered; some break into it; some come at it bark-first; some bind the others' wounds with sap.
- **r16.** Where a root-man lays a hand on you, the wood knows you, through that hand. Cut them down with iron, and the wood still knows you.
- **r17.** The broad hulls wait. In their crowns the winged seed ripens. The crowns open, and the seed rises on the wind and goes over your walls. The wind chooses the keep. The seed turns aside from the heat over your guns. If it does not find the keep, it goes back to the broad hulls, and goes again.
- **r18.** A broad hull and its seed are bound, each only with the other: the seed is the hull's own life. Strike down all its seed, and the hull dies. Fell the hull, and the seed in the air strikes and falls, and does not go back (to the hull). Husks go with the seed aloft in the grey, to take the shot meant for it; they take it, and the shot is broken off. The husks do not burn; they fall, and the hull does not hold them (lets them fall).
- **r19.** A few seeds grow to be the greatest, and then need no crown. Such a seed goes high, alone and slowly, in the grey, hung from its own hollow little hull; you bring it down only slowly.
- **r20.** We have grown through many summers. The long-lived cried out for war; they lived long, and died; and time forgot them. In every Tide the carvers were fewer; and at the last only a few of the long-lived tended the groves.
- **r21.** The long-lived, who no longer live, sent a judgement: us, a judgement that lives with the wood's life, sent against the shore-folk, a people who never knew that they were the enemy. We go on, still. None of us speaks (calls) toward home.
- **r22.** The groves sing our growing; and then we hold it:
- **r23.** (the song, seven said-pockets, each "we hold that …")
  1. Green wood holds one word.
  2. Wood of many summers holds one word, and waits for the second.
  3. A shore-man's life holds a sentence, and a wall remembered in it.
  4. A long life holds the morrow: it carves, waits, and carves again.
  5. The eldest of us (are) as black pillars, as hearts, as holders of the earth, in the grey.
  6. They only hold: no word, no sentence, no morrow.
  7. Grief is carved into every hull that is sent.
- **r24.** We grow; and they send us. *(Seal: this is held in the grain.)*
