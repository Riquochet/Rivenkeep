1What This Doc Is

The Build phase is half the core loop and the most decision-dense minute in the game — the place you repair, extend, and re-shape a fortress you have been raising for sorties, under a soft clock, off a two-piece draw. This doc is the Build design pulled out of the GDD into one home and elevated where there was room: triage made geometric, the wall given its full set of jobs, and the reason a fleet chews your walls made explicit.

The shape in one breath

Each sortie opens with a cleanup wipe (spackle gone, walls repaired or removed, castles healed if they held, fire zones cycled), then a timed build where you place pieces to keep the required castles enclosed, protect your guns, and choose which castles are worth the wall. Win the instant the required rings close at the end of any Build; the rest of the phase is spent buying safety, coverage, and Battle Score with a finite hand. Nothing here is reflex — it is a low-pressure spatial puzzle against a mind that will read what you built.

The "how" (numbers, the flood-fill, the state machine) lives in spec boxes; the "why" is measured against The Why throughout, and §14 gives the honest north-star read. Where this doc and a sibling disagree on a shared concept, the owning doc wins (enclosure-as-win → Victory; cannon veterancy → Cannon & Tower §8; the reading fleet → Fleet Memory; the requirement ladder → Campaign).

2Where Build Sits, and the Timer That Governs It

Build is the first phase of every sortie: Cleanup → Build → Deploy → Fight, repeated until the ship pool empties into the Last Stand. It is where the fortress is authored; Deploy places the guns on top of it; the Fight tests it.

THE BUILD CLOCK — 54 s, flat, whole campaign DERIVED · v1.9.0, tuned to the display

Build runs on a fixed 54-second countdown at every stage of the campaign. No campaign taper, no Sortie-1 bonus, no Boss special value — all three of those rules are deleted, and why they are not needed is the whole design of this clock. It is still extended by Stonwryt Alter Time, and it is still server-tunable.

Where 54 comes from — the floor is set from below by the tax. §11 prices the rebuild tax at 2 / 3 / 3–4 pieces, and §5 converts that into time spoken for before the player makes one discretionary choice: ~17 s early · ~25 s mid · ~30 s late, rising to ~45 s on the worst Build in the game — a late repair sortie carrying a breakthrough re-plug. That is a mandatory floor, not a target. A clock set at the floor is a phase with no game left in it, so the clock is the floor plus the smallest discretionary remainder that still contains a decision: a choice and the piece that expresses it — one piece, at the harsh end of 9 s (including the beat to decide it).

45 s (worst mandatory Build, §5)  +  9 s (one discretionary piece, and the decision behind it)  =  54 s

⚠ v1.9.0 — moved from 55 to 54 s, deliberately, for the display below. 54 s is exactly 18 s × 3, which is what lets the on-screen clock read a clean 3 days rather than 3-point-something. The cost is 1 second of discretionary time, uniformly, at every stage — see the freedom table, which barely moves. The tax floor (45 s) is untouched; it is derived from combat, not from the display.

The clock does not scale — the tax does. That is the law. Fixing the clock and letting the tax grow inside it means what escalates across the campaign is how little freedom you have, not how fast you must move:

StageTax (§11)Discretionary time leftFreedom

Cam 7–22~25 s29 s54%

These are the nominal figures the clock was set against — barely moved from the 55 s pass (69/55/45/18% → 69/54/44/17%). §5 realizes them a few seconds better once the wall-watching mechanic (Fight §3b) is in — the clock does not change to chase that; it is margin, not a re-tune.

Three rules already in this doc set forbid the alternative. A tapering clock on top of a growing tax is escalation on two axes — exactly what §11 refused when it rejected a perimeter-proportional tax. It is also a third screw against the choreography law (piece-pressure and cognition never turn together — one screw per campaign). And it would be invisible difficulty: a clock shortening by 5 s across 32 campaigns is not something a player can read, whereas "three holes to plug and time for one idea" is legible in the moment. So — one number, whole campaign, and the campaign is felt in what fills it.

Why the deleted rules were redundant. The +10 s Sortie-1 bonus existed because sortie 1 builds from scratch — but sortie 1 carries no tax, so under a flat clock it is already the loosest Build in the battle (7–9 fresh pieces plus recognition ≈ 33–40 s, leaving 14–21 s of discretion). The fixed 22 s Boss value existed to tighten the finale — but the Boss demands 4/4 castles, so every hole is mandatory and the tax is already at its maximum share there. Both rules were compensating for a clock that scaled; neither survives a clock that does not.

The band the old text described is unchanged and now has arithmetic behind it: generous enough to be thinking-space, tight enough that a long ring, awkward pieces and wind together create real pressure — and the pressure must always read as "plan carefully," never "move fast."

How the clock is DISPLAYED — days, not seconds v1.9.0, Jack's ruling, refined further

The mechanic stays in seconds; only the readout changes. Every number above — 54 s, the tax bands, the discretionary margin — is the real design and does not move. What the player sees on screen is a different unit: the countdown displays in days, because a fortress rebuild that takes 54 seconds and reads as 54 seconds feels like a game clock, and Jack wants it to feel like a siege.

The conversion is now exact: 1 day = 18 s, so 54 s = 3 days, no rounding. That exactness is why the clock moved from 55 to 54 above — a clean 3.0 rather than a fuzzy 3.06 is worth one second of discretionary time.

The readout — two numbers, two speeds, and the fast one is deliberate v1.9.0, Jack's ruling. A large day number counts down 3 → 2 → 1 → 0 — one change per 18 real seconds, the clock's main beat. Beside it, a smaller hour number sweeps 23 → 0 within each day, resetting to 23 every time the day ticks down. Since a day is 18 s and holds 24 hour-values, the hour figure changes every 18 ÷ 24 = 0.75 s — faster than a real second, and that mismatch is the point: a normal clock's fastest hand ticks once a second; this one ticks roughly 1.3× that. It reads as hurried without being unreadable (24 values in 18 s is still a clean digit-per-glance), which is exactly the "little feeling of anxiety" wanted — the small number is visibly racing while the big number holds its dignity.

Implementation note, not a design question: the exact frame at which the day ticks and the hour wraps (23→0 then reset, vs. 0→23 then decrement the day) is a fencepost detail for whoever builds the widget, not a derived figure — either reads correctly at a glance.

The genuinely good part: the SAME conversion turns the tax into a sentence the fiction already wants to say. "Workers need N days" (the tax) against "the fleet returns in 3 days" (the clock) is the exact siege framing Jack described — and it's the existing math, relabeled:

StageTax, in daysWhat it reads as

Whether Deploy and Fight get a matching frame (dusk for Deploy, the night's siege for Fight) is an open extension, not decided here — flag it if a consistent whole-siege fiction is wanted; Build's display works standalone either way.

The timer is load-bearing — and it is the one place Build can slip to arcade

The soft clock is the only thing stopping Build from becoming an optimizer's paradise — an untimed board would let a player grind out the single best packing every sortie, which is executing a memorised optimisation, the exact north-star failure (The Why §2). But turned too far the other way — a clock parked at the tax floor, a heptomino draw, high wind, a breached ring to re-plug — it tips into fast-fingers-win, the arcade failure. The red line for tuning: if a board is only winnable by placing quickly, the timer is too tight for that board. Difficulty is supposed to come from the problem, not the clock. The 54 s clock is what puts arithmetic under that red line: the tax was priced at unhurried per-piece times (6–8 s for a re-plug, which already includes the orientation search, the fit validation and the drift read), so even the worst Build in the game is 45 s of deliberate work inside a 54 s clock. A board that cannot be held at that is a bad board — not a bad player, and not a bad clock. (This reconciles The Why §2, which now reads "low-pressure timed thinking" rather than "untimed" — the GDD's timed Build was correct.)

Time rollover — one destination, and it is the Last Stand RESTRUCTURED · v1.7.0

The chain is no longer three phases deep, because two of its links turned out to be inert once the clocks were derived. Unused Build time rolls to Deploy at 100% — same sortie, immediate, and Deploy always has a use for it (the seam read is explicitly unbounded). Whatever is still unspent when Deploy closes, plus 50% of whatever is unspent when the Fight closes, goes into one visible Last Stand bank that grows all battle. Unused Deploy no longer extends the Fight, and unused Fight no longer extends the next Build — the derivation, and why both links died, is Victory §5's. What matters here: Build is where the saving starts, and the bank is the only place saved time turns into score. It is also what makes the visibly-growing bank a real Zeigarnik hook (The Why §7) instead of a number that only exists on the final sortie.

The Ready button ends Build early — but it is disabled if ending now would be an immediate loss (you cannot skip your way into an un-enclosed defeat). Ready is what makes a flat clock safe at the low end: an early-campaign Build has 38 s of slack, and slack is only dead air if you are made to sit in it. The clock is a ceiling on deliberation, never a floor on phase duration — so the early game runs fast because the player ends it, and the late game runs long because the tax fills it.

3The Six Steps — the Cross-Sortie State Machine

Build is not a blank canvas each time — it is the same fortress, one sortie older, carrying its damage. Six sub-steps run in fixed order every Build, even if the player places nothing. This is where "damage carryover" lives, and it is what makes the walls feel yours (endowment, The Why §7) rather than disposable.

1 · Cleanup wipe — four things at once

A right-to-left wipe (the mirror of the Deploy→Fight transition, so the phase change is unmistakable) resolves the previous sortie in one animation: spackle removed (temporary patches gone); walls repaired — any block below 25 % HP is removed, every block at ≥ 25 % restored to full; castles healed — each castle that ended the last Fight enclosed recovers 1/3 of its total HP; and the area-denial cycle advances one step, fire → holes → cleared (a burn zone becomes an impassable hole, a hole clears to open ground — the only way area denial resolves). You start each Build looking at clean walls and a board that has visibly moved on.

2 · Pre-Build cannon bonuses (Lock-In 1)

Before you touch anything, surviving guns take their rebuild + enclosure bonuses off the end-of-Fight snapshot — the reward for having protected them. Numbers and the veteran curve are owned by Cannon & Tower §8; Build's job is to show the projected gains live as you wall guns in.

3 · The player builds (timed)

The core minute. Place wall pieces and spackle; use trash and storage. Fight debris — craters, holes, burn — is still on the board and must be built around. Walls need not attach to anything (isolated segments are legal placements) — but see step 4. A fresh two-piece draw fills the Wall Rack at the start of every Build, so a favourable piece cannot be hoarded across sorties: use it, store it, or lose it.

4 · Isolation cleanup — and the interior harvest

After build time ends, a second right-to-left wipe removes every wall block touching 0 or 1 other blocks, castles, or cliffs (4-way only — diagonals do not count as touching). This clears dangling fragments from Fight damage and any new walls you failed to connect. Single-pass, no cascade — a block made isolated by the removal of its neighbour is not itself removed this pass. Then the interior harvest: wall blocks sealed inside an enclosure but touching nothing structural pulse blue with an ω and convert to Stonwryts (4 blocks = 10, rounded down). Exterior isolated blocks (red ✕) are lost; interior floating blocks (blue ω) pay out — same rule, different location. During step 3 both previews run live, teaching the rule by play.

5 · Destroyed-cannon rebuild — the one save window

A gun at 0 % that is enclosed after cleanup is restored to 50 %. This is the only window to save a destroyed cannon — the Build immediately after its death. Miss it (and fail to trash it during the next Deploy) and it becomes a permanent ruin: a 2×2 obstacle you can never reclaim, treated like rock. Trash budget is ⌈sorties / 2⌉. Full model: Cannon & Tower §8.

6 · Enclosure check — win, continue, or lose

The flood-fill (§4) runs live during step 3 for the tiled-floor preview, and formally at end-of-Build and end-of-Deploy. If the required castles are enclosed at the end of a Last Stand Build → the battle is won; in a normal sortie → on to Deploy. If they are not → defeat. The steps all fire even on a zero-piece Build, so doing nothing is a losing move whenever a ring has been breached.

The health rules the carryover runs on

Wall block HP — three hits, and the DOOMED state at two derived ·

Every wall block absorbs 3 hits from a baseline shell — the same toughness as a baseline hull (Victory §3), which is a coincidence of derivation rather than a symmetry that was designed. Inverted from the rebuild tax (§11): a Bombard core carries 5–6 shells per sortie and must cost the player one gap, so a block has to swallow two hits before it is lost and three before it falls. The bar shows only on blocks that have been hit; the sprite cracks in three stages.

  • 1 hit — repaired to full, free, in the next cleanup wipe. Chipping is not damage; it is noise.
  • 2 hits — DOOMED. It still blocks, still holds the ring, still counts for enclosure this Fight, and it is gone in the next cleanup. This is the state the repair line was written to create, and it is the entirety of the deferred tax.
  • 3 hits — destroyed on the spot: an instant hole in the perimeter and a live breakthrough risk.

Two thresholds, two threats — and that is the point. The fleet chooses between breaking the ring now (three hits, concentrated, an event) and taxing the rebuild (two hits, spread, no event but a bill next phase). Those are exactly the two things §11 says the fleet is doing to your wall, and they now have different prices.

Supersedes the inherited “below 25 % is unrepairable” line

The 25 % figure was set under voided numbers and had nothing left to bite on: with three HP states (100 / 67 / 33) a 25 % line never fires, so the doomed-but-standing state it existed to produce could not occur. The rule is now stated in hits, not percent — unrepairable at two — because percent is a display artefact and hits are what the player counts off the crack sprite. Restated as a fraction for the renderer it is a 34 % line, i.e. anywhere strictly above 33 % and at or below 67 %, taken at the harsh end.

Castle degradation — the 3-strike clock, and how enclosure heals it

Degradation only begins once a castle has been enclosed at least once — a never-enclosed castle sits grey and ignored. After that, each consecutive sortie it is left exposed ticks it down: 3/3 → 2/3 (cracks) → 1/3 (heavy) → 0/3 (destroyed, removed permanently). Enclosure reverses it: each sortie the castle is enclosed it recovers 1/3 of total HP (a castle at 55 % → 88 %; 1/3 → 3/3 in two enclosed sorties). Troop and bomber hits during the Fight partially drain a segment; the halo drains visually as it takes damage. This is the loss-aversion engine (The Why §7): loss approaches visibly, converting dread into urgent action rather than despair — and it is the compounding half of a breakthrough's cost (a defeat only arrives when a 3/3 destruction drops you below the required count).

4Enclosure — the Win, Made Geometric

Enclosure is the win condition (Victory §2), so its rules must be exact and legible — a false enclosure is an unfair loss, a denied-but-closed ring is an unfair defeat. Both are game-breaking. The model is a single, simple idea: a castle is safe when it sits inside a closed loop of strong wall.

Barriers, and the one diagonal exception

Four things form perimeters: wall blocks, cliffs, spackle, and castle footprints. Cannons do not — a gun in a gap does not seal it. All perimeter joins are 4-way side-to-side (two blocks touching only at a corner leave a gap) with a single exception: two cliffs touching diagonally close the gap — the only diagonal closure in the game, because cliffs are large irregular terrain that overlaps. Map edges never enclose; only closed loops do.

Why the castle counts as wall

A castle's 3×3 footprint is a barrier — strong wall in its own right. This does three things at once: it keeps the drag-and-place language of the Build phase consistent (a castle edge behaves like any other wall for enclosure and for isolation cleanup), it lets you anchor a ring to the castle instead of walling all the way around it, and it makes castles cheaper to enclose — the castle simultaneously encloses and is enclosed. Cliffs work the same way, which is why a cliff-backed castle is the cheapest of all to seal.

The spec — exterior flood fill

Search only the friendly zone (everything on or beyond the DMZ is "outside"). Mark every wall/cliff/spackle/castle grid BARRIER. Seed a 4-way BFS from every non-barrier friendly grid on the map edge and every non-barrier grid adjacent to the DMZ. Flood; block a passage when a pair of diagonal cliffs straddles it. After the flood: unflooded, non-barrier friendly grids are ENCLOSED; flooded grids are OPEN. A castle is enclosed if ≥ 1 of the 16 grids ringing its footprint is ENCLOSED (two separate enclosures both touching it are both valid). ~1,500 grids worst case, sub-millisecond; it must be perfect, not fast. (Runs on every piece move during Build for the live preview.)

The minimum enclosure — 2×2, or it's dead space

An enclosed region only counts as an enclosure if it holds at least a 2×2 open area. A single sealed grid — or a one-wide slot — is dead space, not a courtyard: sealing it is just building a thicker wall, and it does not enclose a castle. The reason is exact — a cannon occupies a 2×2 footprint, so anything smaller cannot seat even one gun; it is a gap, not a courtyard of coverage (§5). Mechanically the flood-fill gates its ENCLOSED regions by this floor: a region too small to contain a 2×2 square is reclassified open/dead, never enclosure. This closes the degenerate micro-enclosure exploit — a 1×1 pocket next to a castle "technically" sealing it — and keeps enclosure meaning real defended space, which is what the win condition and the Area lever are meant to reward.

The preview is the legibility

Any space that becomes enclosed immediately shows a tiled floor — dark for a permanent ring, gray for a ring that leans on spackle (temporary, lost next wipe). Breach a wall during the Fight and the tiling reverts instantly. This live feedback is what lets a player reason about enclosure instead of guessing — the fairness promise (The Why §3) depends on it.

5Triage Geometry — the Strategic Spine

The oldest question in the game is "which castles do you protect?" — and because you almost never have to protect them all (only the Boss Finale demands 4/4), it is a real decision with interesting answers, not a pass/fail. This section elevates it from an accident of where castles landed into a designed geometry: the map's castle layout is a puzzle, and the puzzle is which set to seal for the least wall and the best coverage.

Triage is a set problem, not a point problem

The requirement ladder asks for a count — 1 of 4, then 3 of 6, up to 9 of 10 (owned by Campaign). But which set of castles is cheapest and safest to enclose is a spatial question shaped by four designed levers per board:

  • Cost-to-enclose — a castle backed by cliffs or map geography needs little new wall; a castle in open ground is dear. Free perimeter is the currency.
  • Keystones / shared perimeters — castles close enough that one ring encloses several. Sealing the keystone set is wall-efficient; it is the geometry behind "efficient enclosure."
  • Coverage overlap — a tight cluster lets a few enclosed guns cover many approaches; scattered castles force thin, stretched coverage.
  • Exposure — some castles sit on the fleet's shortest approach or a troop lane; sealing them is cheap in wall but expensive in the Fight.
The law that keeps it strategic — ≥ 2 near-equal solutions per board

A deterministic board with a single optimal triage is a lock with one key: solved once, dead forever (The Why §4, wide solution space). So every board must ship at least two near-equal triage answers — e.g. "the cheap east cluster: three castles, one ring" versus "the cliff-backed north pair for the least wall, plus a lone south castle." Neither strictly dominates; they trade wall-economy against coverage against exposure. A board that funnels to one best set has failed the north star even if it plays "hard," and board design must actively protect that width.

Difficulty here is spatial constraint, not bigger enemies

As the requirement tightens across the campaign, the freedom shrinks — more of the map's castles become mandatory, forcing longer rings with more seams to defend and fewer sacrifices available. The board gets harder because the spatial problem is more constrained, which is difficulty-as-cognition (The Why §4), not a stat treadmill. This is the escalating-commitment curve (early: three spares and freedom; late: one spare and none of it forgiving) delivered with no new mechanic — only tighter margins on a decision the player already understands.

Courtyard of coverage — the interior is not optional

Every valid enclosure needs a courtyard: an interior open enough to seat the cannons that defend its own ring. Its concrete floor is a 2×2 (§4) — the smallest space that can hold a single gun; anything less is dead space, not a courtyard. This is a hard spatial constraint (a ring pulled so tight there is no room for guns is not a real fortress) and it is where the enclosure tradeoff bites — a minimal ring is cheap and safe but seats few guns and scores little Area; a generous courtyard seats more coverage and earns more Area stars but is more perimeter to hold. Big-or-tight is a live decision every sortie, never a dominant answer.

The cannon slate — the widget that makes the courtyard decision possible new v1.2.0

Courtyard-of-coverage asks you to build interiors big enough to seat the guns you are about to place — which is unanswerable unless you know how many guns, and how many separate seats they should form. So Build carries a persistent cannon slate widget, on screen for the whole phase.

  • Sortie 1 — a forecast. No guns are standing yet, so the slate reads what you will have to place in the coming Deploy: the slot count, and which archetypes are available to fill it. You are building for a fort that does not exist yet, and the widget is the only thing that tells you its size.
  • Sortie 2 and on — current holdings plus what's new. The slate reads what you currently have standing — those guns are already on the board and already seated — alongside any slots that opened from last Fight's performance. The decision shifts from "how big a courtyard do I need" to "do my existing seats still fit, and where does the new gun go."

Both readings answer the same question in the two situations Build actually occurs in, which is why it is one widget and not two. Kept deliberately thin — a count and the available types, not a loadout screen; picking and siting guns is Deploy's job and stays there.

Why this is a requirement, not a convenience

Walls are built before cannons are placed, and that ordering is correct — by Build time you already know the DMZ line, the spawn points, the terrain, and therefore where an attacker can come from and where it wants to get to. That is a sufficient input set for a wall plan provided you also know your own gun count, because a wall plan seating four guns is a different plan from one seating seven, and one big courtyard versus two small ones is a decision about command structure, not just coverage (guns sharing a courtyard become one commanded group — see Deploy §4 and Cannon §3). Without the slate on screen, the highest-value decision in Build is being made blind for no reason.

The wall budget — why it can never be a flat number new ·

The slate tells you how many guns you must seat. This is what that costs in wall, and it is the reason the wall budget is a function rather than a figure. Upstream inputs — peak contacts, gun counts — are owned by Victory §3 and Deploy §2.

What the board gives you back — free perimeter is the triage currency

The figures above are total ring length, not new wall. Cliffs, map geography and the castles themselves (castles are wall — §4) donate perimeter for nothing, and choosing the set that donates most is precisely the cost-to-enclose lever at the top of this section. So:

new cells ≈ 10·√(g·k) × (1 − ffree)  +  standalone walls (troop-block, gun-conceal — §6)  +  the rebuild tax (§11)

Worked, mid-War: g = 9 guns across k = 3 courtyards → 52 cells of ring; a cliff-backed pair donating ffree ≈ 0.35 → 34 new cells; plus ~6 cells of standalone troop-block → ≈ 40 cells. At an average of ~4.5 cells per piece in the mid tiers that is 8–9 pieces for a sortie-1 plan, and 3–5 pieces on later sorties, which are repair-and-extend rather than build-from-nothing.

Wall demand is a DEADLINE inside the battle, not the battle's length corrected ·

An earlier pass of this section claimed wall demand sets the number of sorties. That was built on the wrong win condition and is corrected: victory is beating back the attack while maintaining the required enclosure count (Victory §2), so closing the rings does not end the battle. The fleet ends the battle; the wall is what you must have finished by then.

So the cells above are a deadline, and the battle is a race between two independent clocks:

The wall side is this doc's: Builds ≈ ⌈new wall cells ÷ (pieces per Build × mean piece size)⌉ + re-plugs the fleet forces. ⚠ Corrected v1.7.0 — this used to say "roughly 1 Build early and 3 late," and that figure was computed under the old 20 s clock, which has been deleted. Under the flat 54 s clock (§2), sortie 1 carries no tax — nothing exists yet to repair — so nearly the whole clock is placement time: ~47–50 s at ~3.5 s/piece is 13–14 pieces, comfortably more than the 7–9 a late-tier wall needs. Construction now finishes in one Build, every time, at every stage. The fleet side is Victory §4b's: queue ÷ elimination rate, giving 3 sorties early and 7–10 late — so the initial-construction race is not close; it is won on sortie 1 with room to spare. What actually races the fleet's clock from sortie 2 onward is the rebuild tax alone (§11) — repeated, permanent, and the entire source of ongoing pressure. This is a simplification, not a loss: the design now has one clock racing one thing, instead of two overlapping races that happened to reconcile.

What this makes Build, every sortie: a decision about how much of the phase to spend securing the requirement versus how much to leave for guns and courtyard that will shorten the fight. Ahead of the deadline, build for coverage. Behind it, build for the ring. The cannon slate (§5 above) is what makes that decision answerable, and this is the sharpest reason it is a requirement rather than a convenience.

The soft term is now landed: the rebuild tax is 2 pieces early, 3 mid, 3–4 late — derived in §11 from the margin between these two clocks, with one Build held in reserve for breakthrough recovery. In cells that is ~7 early and ~23 late, but cells are the wrong currency and §11 says why.

A note on board size — this section is why growth is affordable

The board is being resized upward (128×80 derived, Victory §3), and the reason that does not blow up the Build phase is here: wall demand is driven by perimeter, and perimeter is set by the fort core, not by board area. A board with 3.3× the area does not need 3.3× the pieces — the fort core is sized by triage variety (castle spacings that admit ≥2 near-equal solutions), and the extra board is water and approach depth, which cost nothing in wall. What growth does change here is the pan distance during Build and the amount of terrain the generator must author. Neither touches the piece budget.

What this handed the timer session — and what came back updated ·

Pieces-per-sortie is the direct input to the Build clock: phase time ≈ recognition + (pieces × per-piece placement time). At 7–9 pieces for a late-War sortie 1, 3–4 s per piece and 5–8 s of board recognition, sortie 1 wants roughly 33–40 s — leaving 14–21 s of discretion inside the 54 s clock.

The rebuild tax makes the repair sorties worse than sortie 1, which was not expected. A re-plug piece is not an open-ground piece: it needs an orientation search and a fit validation and a drift read against a windbreak, three decisions where open ground needs one, so it costs ×2 — 6–8 s. Against the tax of 2 / 3 / 3–4 pieces (§11), plus a damage diff read:

time spoken for before the player makes one discretionary choice ≈ 17 s early · 25 s mid · 30 s late

So from mid-campaign on, the tax alone exceeds the old 25→20 s band, and the worst Build in the game is not sortie 1 — it is a late repair sortie carrying a breakthrough re-plug, which wants a floor nearer 40–45 s.

6The Wall's Jobs — Enclose, Buffer, Block, Conceal

A wall is not only a piece of an enclosure. It does four jobs, and the interesting Build decisions come from the fact that the placement that is best for one job is often not best for another. This is where "walls protect, not just enclose" stops being a Mountain-theater lesson and becomes a general truth.

1 · Enclose

The ring that seals a castle (§4). The win-condition job.

2 · Buffer — defense in depth, and it is physical, not a rule

An outer wall shields the inner wall and the courtyard by being a buffer: more wall for the fleet to grind through, longer troop paths, a first thing to break that is not the ring you cannot afford to lose. This is entirely emergent and physical — enclosure stays binary: one closed ring encloses, and a second ring buys no enclosure-redundancy. Extra walls buy time and distance, never a change to the flood-fill. Deliberately simple, so the legibility never frays (The Why §6, tension #3).

3 · Block troops

Troops path to castles by weighted A* and will route around an enclosure if a way around is cheaper than breaking through (wall cost = hits-to-destroy + 1; rocks/DMZ/area-denial impassable — model in Ships §7). So a wall that closes a ring may not block a troop lane — and a standalone wall, part of no ring, can be exactly the tool: dropped across the lane to lengthen or deny the path. The enclosure-optimal wall and the troop-blocking wall are frequently different placements. That gap is the decision.

4 · Conceal guns

Enclosed guns are ghosts to the fleet — a hidden cannon is not on the target list (why enclosure protects your investment). A standalone wall can also break the fleet's line to an exposed gun, concealing it without enclosing it — a cheaper, partial protection when a full ring is not affordable. (Shots themselves lob over walls — see §11 — so this is target acquisition, not trajectory blocking; the concealment/targeting mechanic is owned by Cannon & Tower / Ships. Build's stake is only: standalone walls are worth placing for jobs 3 and 4.)

7Pieces — the Draw, the Fit, the Cognition Curve

You build from a two-piece draw of polyominoes — the raw material of every ring. How the piece set escalates across the game is the subtle part, and it is easy to get wrong: it is not a difficulty treadmill. It is the mechanism that keeps the Build phase interesting in its own right, so the player is engaged in it rather than merely waiting for the Fight.

Piece difficulty is contextual — and that is the whole point

The same piece is a burden or a gift depending on where you are building. An awkward heptomino is punishing to squeeze into a tight re-plug against a breach — it eats your time and may not seal the gap cleanly — but across an open stretch of ground it is a god-send, closing a long run in a single drop. So the interesting decision is not "this piece is hard," it is matching the hand you drew to the place it fits best — adapting your route to your pieces, or holding a piece in storage for the ground it wants. That is a genuine strategic act, and it is what makes the packing feel like thinking rather than tidying.

The escalation is engagement, woven into the cognition curve — not a parallel track

The 16-tier progression (below) shifts the draw toward larger, more awkward shapes as the campaign advances. Its job is to keep the Build phase alive as its own kind of thinking as the player's spatial skill grows — never to be the thing that makes a late board hard. That role belongs to the cognition curve (a sharper fleet, a tighter triage). Piece escalation is part of that one curve, subordinate to it, the way an enemy HP bump is (The Why §4). The red line: if awkward shapes ever become the reason a board is difficult, that is a fiddliness treadmill wearing a difficulty coat — and it is out of bounds.

The catalog & the escalation — spec

198 pieces in a 4×4 maximum, so every shape is mentally rotatable on a phone. Eight groups, colour-paired: A · B foundation + tetrominoes, C · D pentominoes, E · F hexominoes, G · H heptominoes. Pieces are one-sided polyominoes — unique under rotation but not reflection (no flip); mirror pairs display side by side, and a piece always starts in its catalog orientation so a seasoned player reads it instantly. Groups enter one at a time across 16 tiers (Introduce → Isolate → Combine), each new group below the previous group's introduction rate; pressure tiers only shift probabilities toward harder shapes, adding nothing new. A's ≥ 2 %, B's ≥ 3 % always.

The draw is deterministic-fair — the selection algorithm

The piece draw runs on its own RNG stream, seeded from the battle's fixed seed (The Why §3) and independent of the fleet's stream, so piece luck and ship behaviour never perturb each other. Two slots are drawn independently, with storage ignored (a protected space the algorithm never reads). Four rules: (1) No duplicate — the same piece ID cannot fill both slots at once. (2) Guaranteed distribution — a group at X % appears at least once every 50 draws per slot (a 2 % A-piece can't vanish for 200 draws). (3) Anti-streak, relaxing as the pool grows — 1–4 groups available (tiers 1–7): pure weighted draw; 5–6 groups (tiers 8–11): no 4 in a row from one group; 7–8 groups (tiers 12–16): no 3 in a row. (4) Within-group uniform — once a group is chosen, its specific piece is equal-odds. Implementation: roll the percentage table → check anti-streak → re-roll up to 3× → else take the least-recently-drawn group; the guaranteed-distribution counter resets per battle, because each battle is its own seeded stream. The stream is consumed as you draw: a battle played the same way deals the same pieces, and trashing a piece pulls the next from the fixed sequence — a costed, deterministic lever you work, never a luck-reroll. This is the variable-ratio engine of The Why §7 — a slot-machine feel over pieces on a board whose outcome is fully deterministic, with trash/storage/spackle as the valves that let skill manage bad luck without erasing it.

8The Build Economy — Scarcity With Valves

Build is a resource phase. The scarcity is what makes each placement matter; the valves are what keep bad luck from becoming unfairness (the anti-frustration table, The Why §7 — agency over randomness).

The four zones of the tray

The Build tray holds four fixed zones (mirrored for left-handed play): Trash (destroy a piece; budget of 1 per sortie), Spackle (§9; count = the battle's sortie total), the Wall Rack (the two draw slots), and Storage (1–5 slots, drop into any open slot, drag one over another to swap — no auto-reshuffle). The tray is the same physical size in Deploy for muscle memory, but its layout there is entirely different (the cannon carousel).

Storage is the planning bank

Storage is a protected player space — the draw algorithm never reads it, and it is not refreshed by the anti-hoard wipe. That makes it the phase's one cross-sortie plan tool: hold the perfect long piece for an open run, or bank a clean corner for the Last Stand. The scarcity (few slots, one trash) forces you to mostly play the hand you are dealt; storage is the small lever that lets skill shape luck.

9Spackle — the Emergency Valve

Spackle is the panic tool: a temporary patch to survive this sortie, meant to be rebuilt in stone next Build. Its whole design is a set of costs that stop it from being strictly better than a wall.

The defined tradeoff

Spackle is 2×2 (Cam 1–16), then 3×3 (Cam 17+), no rotation. Its costs, all in the GDD: limited supply (count = the battle's sortie total, no roll-over between battles), auto-clears after every Fight (removed in the next cleanup wipe), earns no cannon veterancy, and it fills only its valid grids (partial placement — unlike a wall piece, which fails whole). What it does get: it counts for enclosure (a spackled ring is enclosed — shown gray, not dark), it does not drift in wind (a deliberate exception — a last-ditch drop must land where you put it), it behaves as a wall for isolation cleanup (a wall touching spackle is saved), and a wall can be placed over it (upgrading the patch to stone, consuming the spackle). Because the enemy cannot tell spackle from wall, it seals a breach convincingly — for one sortie. That temporariness, the hard cap, and the missing veterancy are exactly why you do not just spackle everything.

10Wind & Drift — Skill-Based Recovery

Wind turns placement into a lead-your-shot puzzle: a piece drifts on drop, and reading the drift is a skill the board rewards (The Why §7). It is a Build-only mechanic — hidden in Deploy and Fight.

The model

One fixed direction per battle over 8 compass points (cardinals early; diagonals from Cam 10+, forcing two-axis compensation) at four strengths: Calm 0 / Low 1 / Medium 2 / High 3 grids, shown by a compass arrow's length and colour. Desert and Frozen carry a permanent +1 base drift — Desert Canyon is never calm. Drift applies on soft-lock — the ghost preview already shows where the piece will land, and every pick-up-and-drop re-drifts, so a piece moved in wind is a piece re-committed. Cannons are too heavy to drift; spackle is exempt.

Drift-stop is the skill expression

A drifting piece stops at the first obstacle that would make it invalid — an existing wall, castle, cliff, rock, the DMZ, the map edge. So existing structure acts as a windbreak: place a piece against a wall on the windward side and it does not drift at all. Skilled players use drift-stop to place precisely even in High wind — which is the design intent: wind is a puzzle to solve with position, not a random tax to endure.

11Why Ships Break Walls — the Build ↔ Fleet Loop

The Build phase does not end when the Fight starts — what you built is read by a mind that gets sharper each sortie (Fleet Memory). This is where the piece layer and the cognition layer meet, and it is a clean expression of difficulty-as-cognition rather than difficulty-as-stats.

THE REBUILD TAX — the model derived ·

A ship works a target chain (primary → secondary → tertiary; structures gone → stand down — Ships). Walls sit high on that chain on purpose: breaking a wall is not just opening a breach now, it is taxing your next Build. This section prices that tax, and the price is the last missing input to the wall clock and therefore to the race in Victory §2.

What is actually scarce — the step-two answer, and it is not wall HP

The imported evidence says wall damage is a volume problem. It is not one here, and the arithmetic says so plainly. A late ring is ~52 cells; a late Build can lay ~50; a sortie destroys single figures. Cells are abundant.

What is scarce is the number of independent places you are forced to work, measured against a hand two slots wide. Enclosure is binary, so a 1-cell hole breaks a ring exactly as thoroughly as a 12-cell one, and a wall piece fails whole — so each separate gap costs at least one piece and at least one full read-rotate-fit-drift cycle, whatever its size. Two gaps of one cell cost strictly more than one gap of ten.

gap count is the scarcity. gap size is the seasoning.

And the corollary is a red line, because the obvious escalation is out of bounds. If late damage arrived as more, smaller holes, the late draw — hexominoes and heptominoes — would be actively wrong for the job, and the tax would become the fiddliness treadmill §7 forbids. So the escalation runs the other way: late gaps get larger and worse-shaped, not more numerous. Gap size tracks the tier’s mean piece, so the piece curve and the tax curve point the same direction instead of fighting.

Damage is PER FORMATION, computed from filled core slots — never per hull

Per-hull damage would make a thick convoy hit the wall harder than a thin one, which is a hull-count treadmill (The Why §4) and, worse, would make the fading late fleet do less wall damage than the fresh early one. It would also let screens break wall, and screens have no purpose of their own (Victory §4a).

So: only cores break wall, and only the two core kinds whose purpose is wall — Bombard and Breacher (plus the Leviathan, which shells as a Firepower hull). Runner and Skyfall cores are delivery cores; their wall damage arrives second-hand, as troops and as craters. A formation’s wall output is therefore its filled wall-breaking core slots, which is a formation property that already persists between sorties (Ships §9). Kill a core and the tax drops next sortie — permanently, because cores do not come back.

Falls out with no new rule: the late roster carries 14 cores but only 5–6 wall-breakers (Bombard line 3, Breacher wedge 2, Leviathan 1), concentrated in two or three formations. That is why the tax stays flat while hull count rises, and it is why which core you hunt is a different decision from how many.

Gap cost is cells plus orphans. Isolation cleanup (§3, step 4) removes blocks left touching 0 or 1 neighbours, single-pass, so a hole punched next to a wall end costs two cells rather than one. Spackle is a loan. It auto-clears after every Fight, so every spackle placed is exactly one piece of guaranteed tax next Build — free in cells, full price in hand-width and seconds. The triage ladder scales the tax by making it MANDATORY, not by making it bigger

The obvious move — tax proportional to perimeter — is forbidden: a late board would carry a longer ring and more gaps, which is escalation on two axes and a stat treadmill on the Build side. So the tax is absolute: 2–3 gaps per sortie at every stage of the requirement ladder.

What the ladder changes is which gaps you are allowed to ignore. At 1-of-4 a hole in a non-required ring is a decision; at 9-of-10 every hole is an obligation. The same three gaps cost one piece early and four late without the number moving — the escalation is free, and it is the spatial-constraint difficulty §5 already claims for triage.

Which makes fleet cognition the tax’s only difficulty knob — and that is exactly right. A sharpening fleet reads enclosure (Fleet Memory) and aims at the required rings, converting discretionary gaps into mandatory ones. Difficulty-as-cognition, on the Build side, with no stat anywhere in it.

The imported anchors, and what the translation step did to them

Rampart. One hit destroys a wall block, and ships prefer the outer walls — so a double wall often emerged unscathed. Translated: one-hit blocks would make every shell a gap and the tax total, which breaks the two-clock law; blocks got depth instead. But the outer-face preference is kept and given a cause — a Bombard lobs at what LOS shows it, which is the skin. Depth beats shelling, mechanically rather than as a quirk.

Real siege. Four 24-pounders needed 4–5 days to breach and 3 more to make it practicable; Metz took ~7,000 rounds into one section before a portion collapsed; and the defenders’ counter was nightly repair and retrenchment behind the breach, with the besiegers firing into the working parties to stop it. Two things translate. (1) Breaching is concentration, not erosion — thousands of rounds into one spot, nothing anywhere else — which is what the concentration rule above reproduces. (2) The siege was already a race between battering rate and repair rate, which is the rebuild tax by its historical name.

What was scarce there, and what is scarce here. There: powder, days, and the fact that a hole was not enough — the breach had to be practicable, wide with a rubble ramp. Here: none of those. Cells are cheap, there is no ammunition cost to rebuilding, and a one-cell hole is already a total failure of enclosure. So the asymmetry pushes the way it always does — historically-calibrated volume feels slack, and the pressure has to be moved onto the axis that is actually short. Hence a tax priced in gaps and seconds rather than in cells, and hence the crater and the wreck, which are the only imports that cost the player what a practicable breach cost a garrison: ground you cannot build on.

This is the C×E interplay — and it must be choreographed

Two screws bear on the same wall: piece difficulty (harder to re-plug) and fleet cognition (smarter about which seam to break — the thin neck, the spackle patch, the wall a gun cannot cover). A sharpening fleet attacks the weakest seam of your ring, so Build difficulty rises by demanding seam-free rings and breach-anticipating gun placement — never bigger enemy numbers. The choreography constraint: piece-pressure (Layer C) and cognition (Layer E) must never turn together (Introduce → Isolate → Combine), or their combined load blows the legibility ceiling (The Why §6, tension #3). One screw per campaign.

Reconciling "shots lob over walls" with "ships target walls first"

These read as a contradiction and are not. Shots physically lob — walls do not block a projectile's arc, which is why enclosing your guns works (they fire out fine). Ships still prioritise walls as targets — by target-chain priority, for the rebuild-tax reason above, not because the wall stops the shot. Targeting priority, not trajectory blocking.

12Controls & Interaction — One Finger, Forgiveness Before Commitment

Every Build interaction is one-finger, and the design principle underneath is forgiveness before commitment: you can adjust freely right up until you commit, and committing is always a clear, deliberate act (the anti-frustration line, The Why §7). This keeps the low-load feel the north star demands.

The feel

Fast drag = smooth, slow drag = grid-snap (a continuous blend, not a toggle) — quick repositioning is fluid, precise placement is snappy. Every dragged piece shows a 4×4 ghost (always 4×4, monomino to heptomino, piece centered inside it) so you see exactly where each block lands; a magnify halo appears if you are zoomed too far out to place precisely. Tap to rotate 90° clockwise, in the tray or soft-locked on the board. Group colour tints the piece by difficulty until hard-lock, when it fades into one seamless white wall.

The state machine — spec

A piece moves In Tray → In Play → Soft-Lock → Hard-Lock (with a Storage side-state). It is not "in play" until its bounding-box center crosses the tray boundary and the finger lifts — until then it snaps back with no consequence. In play but unlocked: rotate, reposition, trash, or store — but it cannot return to the Wall Rack (a fresh piece already took its slot), and all other tray pulls are locked until it resolves. An invalid position (rocks, overlap, enemy territory, off-grid) shows red-✕ blocks and disables the Rack; a wall piece must be fully valid to soft-lock (partial placement is spackle's privilege, not a wall's). Hard-lock fires when any piece is pulled from any tray, when the timer expires, or on Ready — and a piece still invalid at that moment is removed, not locked.

The four states in full
  • In Tray (Wall Rack / Storage / Spackle) — tap to rotate in place. Not "in play" until the bounding-box center crosses the tray boundary and the finger lifts; lift with the center still inside and it snaps back, nothing happened.
  • In Play (on the board, unlocked) — a fresh piece immediately fills the vacated Wall-Rack slot. Rotate, reposition, trash, or store — but it cannot return to the Wall Rack, and every other tray pull is greyed until this piece resolves. Invalid position (rocks, overlap, enemy territory, DMZ, off-grid) → the offending blocks turn red-✕, the ghost reddens, and it will not soft-lock; it must be dragged fully valid, trashed, or stored. Partial placement is a wall piece's only forbidden move (spackle's privilege, not a wall's).
  • Soft-Lock (placed, adjustable) — bright white, a faded ghost box marking "still movable," tap to rotate / pick up to reposition. Hard-locks on the triggers above; invalid at the timer → removed, not frozen.
  • Storage — occupies the exact slot dropped into (no auto-reshuffle); drag one stored piece over another to swap. Tap a stored piece to rotate, or drag it out to the board (State 2 rules; still can't go to the Wall Rack).

Equivalence & shortcuts: tap-select-then-tap-place is identical to drag (a board hold lets you adjust before release); with a piece selected, tap a storage slot to store or the trash to trash — no drag. An invalid target fires a "no" haptic and nothing happens.

Off-viewport, no auto-scroll, full-zone rejection

Edge drag does not auto-scroll — you pan/zoom the camera first, then place; this keeps camera control deliberate during precise placement. A piece cannot cross the map edge (it stops at the boundary) but can be dragged off the visible viewport while zoomed; when a soft-locked piece (or a first-sortie cannon in Deploy) goes ≥ 50 % off-screen, a glowing frame appears in the lower corner (½ tray size, brightening as the piece leaves view) — tap it to jump the piece under your finger, hold to drag it back. Dragging a piece over a tray zone that can't take it (full storage, no trashes, the Wall Rack) turns it red with a continuous "no" haptic; drop it there and it returns to where the drag began.

Rotation — the algorithm

Rotate inside the 4×4 ghost — the ghost stays put

Tap rotates 90° clockwise (0 → 90 → 180 → 270 → 0); the ghost's board position does not move, only the shape inside it. Three steps per tap: (1) Transform — (row,col) → (col, −row) on every cell (a 90° CW matrix). (2) Normalize — shift so min-row and min-col are both 0 (removes the negatives). (3) Center in 4×4 — offset by floor((4−height)/2) rows and floor((4−width)/2) cols. floor() biases even-dimension pieces (1×2, 2×4) up-left by at most ½ grid; because the offset changes when the bounding box swaps (2×3 → 3×2), a piece can shift up to ½ grid during rotation — 6 px at 12 px/grid, invisible under a fingertip. A piece always starts in its catalog orientation, so a seasoned player reads original-vs-mirror instantly.

13War II Hooks — Deliberately Out of the Base Game

Wall stacking — reinforced / double walls

Vertical investment — spending extra pieces to harden a segment against the breach-front — is held to War II, not the base game. The reason is legibility and the stat-lever caution: per-block-HP variation adds a dimension to reason about, and a cheap version invites a "double-wall everything" dominant line. 14Why It Works — the North-Star Check

An honest read of the Build phase against the one test: does it keep the player making an interesting decision against a mind, rather than executing a memorised optimisation against a number?

What it gets right
  • The decision density is real — triage, route, fit, the economy, when to Ready, gun protection: most are non-dominant with legible consequences, which is why Build is the strongest-designed slice.
  • Triage as geometry turns "which castle" into "which efficient set," and the ≥ 2-solutions law keeps the deterministic board from solving to one line — the wide-solution-space rule, made a board-design obligation.
  • Contextual pieces make the packing a strategic act (match the hand to the ground), and framing the escalation as engagement inside the cognition curve keeps it from becoming a fiddliness treadmill.
  • The rebuild-tax loop is difficulty-as-cognition living inside Build — a sharper fleet hits your weakest seam, so you must build seam-free, not bigger.
  • The carryover state machine makes the fortress yours across sorties (endowment, loss-aversion), and wind's drift-stop makes even the "random tax" a skill to master.
What to keep honest
  • Build is the densest phase in the game — triage, fit, wind, gun protection, carryover, area-denial, degradation clocks, and anticipating the fleet's next seam-attack, all at once. It sits nearest the legibility ceiling (The Why §6, tension #3). The live previews carry the load today; every future addition must earn its load with a decision a preview can render, or it is cut. Guard this above all as systems combine.
  • This phase is where much of the mini-game work will live — Build is the game's greatest puzzle-piece game, and, mastered, its greatest heart (the on-ramp to the north star lives here more than anywhere: a player who can read a board and pick the efficient triage is a player who can enjoy the strategy). The Introduce → Isolate → Combine ladder maps cleanly onto Build drills — place one piece → close one ring → triage a set → run a full Build under wind and a reading fleet. Load-bearing, not decorative; full design waits for the Mini-Games doc (built from the phases).
  • The ≥ 2-solutions triage law needs a board-audit checklist — it is a per-board authoring discipline, not a mechanic, and the easiest thing to let quietly slide into one-optimal-set boards that play "hard" but aren't. Every board wants a check: does it admit at least two near-equal triage answers that trade wall-economy against coverage against exposure? A board that fails it has quietly broken the north star.
  • The timer band is a red line, not a free knob (§2) — tune it and a board can slip to fast-fingers-win. Difficulty must come from the problem.
  • Triage must actually ship its two solutions — a board that only looks like a choice but funnels to one optimal set has quietly failed the north star while playing "hard." This is a per-board authoring discipline, and it is the easiest thing to let slide.
  • Enclosure must stay a tradeoff, never a turtle (The Why §6, tension #4) — the courtyard cost, the breakthrough threat, and the Area/Battle-Score gradient are what hold it there. Keep them load-bearing.
