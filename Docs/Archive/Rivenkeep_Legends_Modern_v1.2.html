A note on this translation

This is a modern translation of the Book of the Riven Stone, made for easy reading. The tales, tellers, names and facts are the same, in the same order and paragraph for paragraph, so the two can be read side by side. The sentences are shorter, and the verses are plain lines.

The full text, with its poetry, is in The Legends, which is canonical where they differ. Every tale opens with three tabs: The Book takes you to the same tale there, Plain Words is this page, and Original takes you to the same tale in its own hands, in The Legends, in Their Own Hands: a stone tale in the Rivenmen’s own tongue, in Seren’s ink, and a wood tale in the grain.

Where a page or a mark is not yet earned in the game, it is shown here as the player first sees it, in its own hand: the wood’s grain, or the course-hand the Rivenmen cut in stone. Under each drawing, a small Translation fold gives the English and the achievement that earns it. The two tongues and their hands are in The Tongues.

OF THIS BOOK

The BookPlain WordsOriginal

Written by Seren Two-Inks, Halyna’s apprentice and the third member of their hearth. She wrote it for the front of this Book when the Book was already half full.

This is the Book of the Riven Stone. It holds the tales that are laid at the Long Hearth of Rivenkeep. The hearth is in the hall behind the inner gate, under the wall where the torn cloak flies. The tales are laid in the hours after an assault, while the guns are cooling and the mortar has not yet set, when there is nothing left to do with our hands but hold them to the fire.

We did not start this custom. The first builders of the Keep started it. We forgot it, and then found it again at the bottom of a chest in the deepest vault, as you will read. When the first builders had set their stone and cleaned their tools, they sat at this hearth, and each night one of them laid a tale. We do what they did. Our wall is rising again, and so is this Book.

Beside the hearth, in a niche in the hearth-wall, lies the old capstone of the inner gate. It fell during the forgotten years and lay in the gateway until Halyna turned it over. Halyna are the elders of the guild at this Keep. They are Aetherbonded, and the guild calls the Aetherbonded simply the Bonded. Halyna is the one name the two of them share. On their own they are Halvard and Rhyna. Halvard’s fathers cut that stone and Rhyna’s forebears laid it, and the Stonwryt of the first builders is still sharp on its underside. Whoever holds the stone tells the tale. When the tale is done, the teller puts the stone back in its niche, and we say the tale has been laid. A tale that is only told is gone by morning. A tale that is laid becomes part of the wall.

When one of our tales is laid, everyone at the hearth answers it together: We remember. The answer comes from the first words of the Captain’s Title. It is the only thing at this hearth that is always said the same way.

Not every tale in this Book is ours. Some belong to the wood. They come from the wreck-wood that the tide washes in below our wall, from the dark stone in the chest I carried up the mountain, and from other wood as well. The wood cannot be read, because there are no words in it. Halyna touch it, and they know its tale whole, the way you know a memory: the beginning, the middle and the end, all at once. Only one of them needs to touch it, because whatever one of them knows, the other knows too. The knowing is not the hard part. The telling is. Words can be twisted, and a knowing cannot. So they tell it in the fewest and plainest words they can find, and they set each word down like a marker stone over the place where the knowing lies. The wood’s tales are not told with the stone in hand. When a telling from the wood ends, the hearth does not answer, because it does not know how. Each time, I have written that silence down, just as it was.

I cannot know the wood myself. I am not one of the Bonded. The guild calls me unbonded, and after I came up the mountain, Halyna took me into their hearth as its third. I laid my hand on the wood once, and let go, because what came into me was too great for me to hold. So I write down what Halyna tell. Every scribe of the guild before me was one of a bonded pair. Halyna explain in the proper place, in a tale for the children, why this Book is written by someone who is not.

Halyna wanted the wood’s tales in this Book. “A tale told from one side of a wall,” one of them said, and the other finished, “is half a tale.” So when one event was seen from both sides, the two tellings face each other: ours on the left-hand page, and the wood’s on the right. Where the wood’s page could not be told yet, I have left it blank, with only the grain showing. Whoever opens this Book can see the shape of what we did not yet understand, and wait with us.

When Halyna tell a tale, they take turns, one paragraph each. They know the tales as well as they know each other, and neither of them waits for the other. I have used two inks, one for each of them, and they asked me not to mark which is whose. When they share a single line, one starting it and the other finishing it, that is in two inks as well.

The Captain does not tell tales. He sits at the end of the bench with his hood up and listens to his own deeds being told. He says nothing, except sometimes that it was not like that. On the wall and in the orders he is the Commander, because the Rite gave him that name. At this hearth he is still the Captain, which is how he wants it.

When we speak of our fathers we call them Shorelanders, because that is what they were, and we call ourselves we. We call the people behind the grey the Mystaeri, the mist-folk. That is our word for them, not theirs.

Where a carving was broken, or a knowing could not be told, I have said so. I have not filled the gaps with guesses. Where the tales disagree, I have kept both.

This Book is written in ink. Halyna mean to have it cut into the granite of this hall when there is enough peace to cut it. Until then it is kept in the chest I carried, with the mute stones.

IN MEMORY OF OUR HOME, OUR FAMILIES, OUR GOD, OUR FREEDOMS, OUR PEACE.

Sit down if there is room, and stand if there is not. The guns are cooling, and the mortar will not set before dark.

This is the hearth, and this is the stone that fell from the inner gate. Whoever holds it tells the tale.

We tell these tales because once we forgot. Our fathers forgot a keep, and it crumbled. Their fathers forgot a wrong, and it came back to us under grey sails. So we tell all of it: the building and the breaking, what we did as well as what was done to us.

Some of these tales are ours, and they are cut plain, like the stones of the wall. Some belong to the wood. Those are hard to tell, and they will not be rushed. Where the two disagree, we have kept both.

BOOK ONE · THE BOOK OF THE WALL

How we came to make our stand.

I.1 · The Torn CloakStone tale

The BookPlain WordsOriginal

Told by Sergeant Kael, known as Kael the Counter, oath-keeper of the True Men, on the first night this hearth asked for a tale. He would not let me change a word of it, and I did not.

Give me the stone. No, I’ll stand. I’ve done enough sitting this winter.

I swear on the lintel of the western stair, where the old wardens cut their names, that I’ll tell this plainly. I don’t know any other way to tell it. I was on the gate that day. I count things. I’m not a singer, and what I didn’t count, I won’t tell you.

The column came in at dusk, and I counted it as it came. Old men. Women carrying bundles. Children asleep on their feet. Soldiers from six broken companies who no longer knew their own officers. Two thousand and a few hundred. We had walked for a month to reach a castle, and what we reached was a heap of stones. The outer gate hung from one hinge. The ward behind it, the open yard inside the walls, was knee-deep in grass. Where the songs had promised a great hall, there was a fallen roof, and snow on the hearth where you’re sitting now.

Some shook. Some wept. Some fell and would not rise.

Then the crying began, and it wasn’t one cry. Some cried that we should send to the grey sails and beg for terms, though no one had ever understood a word the grey sails said. Some cried that we should make for the high passes and the snow beyond them. Some cried that we should go down into the cellars of the Keep, bar the doors and wait, as if the sea would forget about us. I’d heard men break before. I’d never heard a whole people break. It sounds like the ice breaking up on a river.

Now I’ll tell you what the Captain was, and first what he wasn’t. He wasn’t a general; the generals were dead. He wasn’t a politician; the politicians were forgotten. He wasn’t a nobleman; the nobles were scattered. He wasn’t a Stonewright; the Stonewrights were sitting in the grass with their chisels in their laps, in despair. He was the captain of a small company, and we were that company. He had found us one at a time, in garrisons and on roads, twelve of us in all, and he had bound us together with nothing but trust. We were soldiers who kept our oaths, who built what we promised, and who fought only when the cause was just. That’s all a True Man is. I tell you that so you’ll know it isn’t much. It isn’t little, either.

He didn’t want to lead. I swear to that too. All through the long retreat he waited for someone whose job it was to lead to take charge, and no one did, because the leaders were dead. Don’t mistake me. It wasn’t that the greater men were gone. There was no greater man. I don’t think his equal ever lived.

The True Men looked to their Captain. The Captain looked at the wall.

Then he climbed it. He had picked up a coal from the dead hearth as he came through the hall, and I didn’t know why. He climbed the broken face of the inner wall alone, using the stones that stuck out from the mortar. We stood at the bottom and let him, because he hadn’t told us to follow. At the top, where the wall was still whole, he took off his cloak. It was a plain soldier’s cloak. It had been crimson once, and had faded to the colour of old brick. He tore it down the back. He pulled a pike out of the rubble and tied one half of the cloak to it. Then he knelt on the capstones with the coal and wrote, slowly, like a man who hasn’t done much writing.

I was the first up after him, and the first to read it. I read it out loud. My voice wasn’t steady, and I won’t pretend it was:

IN MEMORY OF OUR HOME, OUR FAMILIES, OUR GOD, OUR FREEDOMS, OUR PEACE.

He set the pike in a crack in the wall and gave it to the wind. That was the last unbroken wall in the Shorelands, and that was the banner that flew over it.

Now listen to who answered, and in what order, because I counted. The True Men answered first, and I’ll name them, because a count isn’t a count until it’s named. Stannard of the ridge, the quarryman. Corlen of the harbour. Tamm of the three spans. Marl the fen-born. Aske of the canopy. Garvel of the smithies. Harl of the burning shore. Ulden of the galleries. Hesk of the mere. Lanner of the crags. Pellow of the spire. And Kael the Counter, oath-keeper of the True Men. That’s me. I’m from no haven; he found me on a road. Twelve. I counted us twice, and it didn’t take long.

Next came the soldiers of the six companies, who had walked the long retreat beside us. They’re our troops now, one troop to each True Man, and they trust us the way we trust him. Then came the people of the column, who had carried their children on their backs through the passes. First one voice, then a dozen, then hundreds, until I couldn’t count them, and I stopped trying. It’s the only time I’ve ever stopped counting. No one ordered it, because he gave no orders. He had only written down what we had left, and it was enough.

That’s why the pennant on every keep of ours is crimson. It’s the colour of that cloak.

He’s sitting there now, at the end of the bench, with his hood up. He’ll tell you it wasn’t like that. It was like that.

I lay this tale as it was laid for me.

Then the True Men sang, as they still sing it today:

The gate was down, and the lords had scattered. Grey sails were gathering along the shore. One man climbed the loosened stones and tore the cloak from his own back. He wrote down five things worth dying for. We have never needed anything more.

The listeners said: We remember.

I.2 · The Cry of the BondedStone tale

The BookPlain WordsOriginal

Told by Seren Two-Inks, Halyna’s apprentice and the scribe of this Book, on the night after the Rivenmen held off their first assault. On the day she tells of, she was a child of the guild, sitting in the courtyard with the others.

“Hold the stone,” Halyna told me when I was small and too frightened to speak at this hearth. “Hold it, and it will hold you.” So I hold it.

That day I was in the courtyard among the Stonewrights. I had already been called to the guild, though so far my only craft was carrying water. We sat in the old ward where the grass had grown up, some hundreds of us, masters and apprentices, with the bonded pairs sitting a little apart. I had walked through the passes beside a boy of the guild, and I came into that courtyard alone. We had come out of ten havens. We had watched every one of them die, and we had walked away from each one with our chisels in our packs. Not one of us had lifted a hand, because our creed forbade it. Build, do not destroy. Protect, do not attack. No Stonewright had ever carried a weapon. Mortar and chisel were all we had to fight with, and they had not been enough.

The faith that had carried us through twelve generations was still on us, but it hung on us like a wet cloak. I think every elder in that courtyard was turning over the same hard thought, and none of them would say it out loud: that walls built for peace must sometimes be defended with blood. And if that was true, whose blood would it be? And whose hands?

Then the Captain came down from the wall. I had seen the cloak go up and heard the soldiers shout, and I didn’t know what any of it had to do with us. He walked into the middle of the courtyard. He wasn’t tall. His fingers were black with coal. He looked at the elders, and at the broken gate behind them, and he asked one question. It is the only speech of his that anyone at this hearth can repeat:

“Your walls are falling. Will you build, or will you watch and die?”

Nobody answered him. I remember the wind in the grass.

Then Halyna stood up. You know them by that name now. The guild gave it to them when they were married, as it gives a name to every bonded pair, and that is what they are called. The guild calls the Aetherbonded simply the Bonded, and Halyna are the Bonded of the Keep. I didn’t know them then, except as two elders who sat apart and spoke to no one, because they had no need to. I will name them separately just this once, because the tale needs it. She was Elderess Rhyna, Rhyna of the Inner Gate. Her bonded forebears had laid the capstone of that very gate, in the age before the havens. He was Elder Halvard, Halvard Stone-Warden, from the quarry-wardens of the high ridge. His fathers had cut that same stone. They were husband and wife, as the Bonded are, and they had been one since the cradle. They looked up at the torn cloak and bowed to it in the same breath, because the Bonded do not wait for each other. Then their two chisels rose together and caught the last of the light.

The cry came from both of them, and it was one cry. She began it:

“The mortar cries out from the ground. Rise, Stonewrights! Lay stone upon stone. Stand every wall to the very end.”

And he ended it, with his chisel raised beside hers:

“Rise! Rise! Rise!”

Some people tell it as if she called and he answered, as if there were two cries with a silence between them. There was no silence. It was one cry from two throats. Hers came first only because a cry has to start somewhere.

Then the whole guild was on its feet, and I was too, though I don’t remember standing up. We took up the shout, and the soldiers on the wall took it up from us, until the walls of Rivenkeep itself seemed to shake. Old stone doesn’t shake. I felt it shake under my feet.

That night, in the ruins of this hall, the terms were made, and Halyna made them. The Stonewrights would not take up weapons. They never had and never would, and the Captain didn’t ask them to. But they would build between the volleys, and mend a breach while the guns were still firing, with a speed and precision only Aetherbonded pairs could manage, because a bonded pair works as one body with four hands. One of them said the first half of it to the Captain and the other said the rest, and I couldn’t tell you which. It has been the guild’s motto ever since: “Not the sword, but the stone. Not the killing, but the keeping.”

So the siege day was divided up, and this hearth has names for its hours. There is the laying, when the Stonewrights build and repair. There is the hauling, when the guns are dragged to wherever the Captain wants them. There is the holding, when the grey sails come in and the wall has to stand, and every battery fights under one of the Captain’s True Men. Every gun that is hauled means a course of stone, one layer of the wall, that doesn’t get laid, because it is the same hands on the ropes. The Captain knows this, and he moves a gun the way a careful man spends silver. And whenever you see a Stonewright on the wall during the holding, look again. You’ll see two.

That first night we sang by torchlight in the gap of the inner gate as we laid the stones. One voice sang the first half of each line and another sang the rest, the way Halyna taught us:

Stone on stone, and the stone keeps its word. Hand over hand, and the hand holds on. Two at the gate, when the grey sails come. Two at the gate, and the gate still stands.

I lay this tale as it was laid for me.

The listeners said: We remember.

I.3 · The Captain’s Long BreathStone tale

The BookPlain WordsOriginal

Told by Private Hale, Hale the runner, who carries word of what is seen along the wall during the holding. He told it in the first year of the siege. It is about a saying of this hearth, and where the saying came from. Kael the Counter corrects it at the end, as he corrects most things.

Give me the stone, then. I’m not a teller. I’m a runner.

There’s a saying at this hearth that you’ll hear before you’ve been on the wall a month. I think it started with me, though Voss says it’s older.

A day has twenty-four hours in it, siege or no siege. In the laying they go slowly, because stone is heavy. In the hauling they go slowly, because guns are heavier. But in the holding, when the grey sails come in, an hour goes by in a heartbeat, and a whole day in twenty-four heartbeats, and you can’t hold one hour still long enough to think in it. That’s how it was in my first holding. The sails came out of the grey faster than I could count them, and the guns fired, and the smoke came down. Afterward I couldn’t have told you whether an hour had gone by or a whole morning.

Every gun on this wall stands where the Captain put it, and every battery has one of his True Men in charge of it. Before the sails come, the Captain tells each True Man what he has in mind: where to watch, what shot to use, and when to hold fire. In the holding, each True Man fights his battery by his own judgment, because they know the Captain’s mind. That’s why each battery fights on its own. The Captain doesn’t send his orders along the wall. When he has to, he gives the call himself, on the horn, to one battery at a time. There are three calls, and every True Man knows them. Shift your ground: that means the stretch of water a battery watches, not the stones it stands on. Change your shot: for the water, the shore, or the sky. And hold, or loose: wait, or fire. A runner carries none of these. What I carry is what’s been seen.

In those first days there was only one battery on the wall, and it was Kael’s. That day Kael sent me with something his lookout had seen: a hull that had hung back in the grey, where none had ever hung back before, and was coming in now, late, after the rest were spent. I ran it along the wall-walk with my head down, and I came to the Captain.

He was standing still. That’s the first thing I remember. Everything on that wall was moving except him. He had one hand on the parapet above the gate and the horn in the other. He was watching one hull out of a dozen, the grey-sailed thing coming in late on the tide, and he didn’t turn to look at me. He heard me out. Then he raised the horn.

And the hour began to stretch. It didn’t stop short, the way a cart stops against a rock. It slowed down, the way a cart slows on a long hill, until it hardly moved at all. I don’t have a better way to say it. I stood at his shoulder and the hour went long around him. I could see the water sheeting off that hull’s bow. I could count the ripples running down its sail, one, two, three, the way you count the rings on a cut log, and I think each ripple was a minute. I had time to be afraid. Then I had time to be ashamed of it. Then I had time to stop.

Then he gave Kael’s battery its call, and the horn carried it, and the hour let go of us slowly, like a held breath going out, until the world was back at its own pace. The guns fired. The hull broke up in the one stretch of water where it could hurt no one, exactly where he had meant it to.

So the men say: For the Captain, an hour lasts as long as a day.

They say it as if it were a gift the Stonewrights gave him. I said it that way myself, the first winter. I know better now. It isn’t that he’s quick. It’s that he won’t be hurried.

Kael said it better, from across the fire, and I’ll give it to you the way he said it: “It isn’t the world that slows down, lad. It’s the Captain who won’t hurry. The moment he chose was the moment that came.”

I lay this tale as it was laid for me.

The listeners said: We remember.

I.4 · The Rite of the CornerstoneStone tale

The BookPlain WordsOriginal

Told by Halyna, the Bonded of the Keep, in the first spring of the siege. It is written in two inks, because they take turns telling it, one paragraph each, as they tell everything. They speak of themselves by name and from a distance, the way the old records speak of the dead. When I asked them why, one said that a tale is not a boast, and the other said that a wall does not know whose hands laid it.

We hold the stone together, as we hold everything. This is how the Rite of the Cornerstone was found again, and what was done with it.

Beneath this hall there are vaults, and beneath those vaults there are more, cut down into the living rock of the ridge before any haven was built on the coast. No Stonewright in the guild’s memory had ever gone into the deepest of them. On the third night after the cry, Rhyna of the Inner Gate got up from her blanket, and at the same moment Halvard Stone-Warden got up and took the lamp. They went down stair after stair, until the stairs ended at the edge of a shaft where the first builders had lowered their stone on ropes. She knew where to look, and he went down, and she let out the rope, and neither of them said a word. What she knew and what he found were one thought, as their thoughts always are.

At the bottom of the shaft lay a chest of black stone, with the mark of the first builders cut into its lid. Inside were the texts of the Rite of the Cornerstone, cut in small letters on thin sheets of slate. It was the oldest ceremony of the guild: the rite by which the first Stonewrights had consecrated the leaders of Rivenkeep to build Rivenkeep itself.

Halyna read the slates by lamplight in the vault, and what they read troubled them. The texts held the secrets of building for war. Walls sloped back so that a shot would glance off. Gates set at an angle so that no battering ram could come at them straight. Courses laid so that a breach would fall outward into the ditch, not inward onto the men behind it. The first builders of the Keep had known war. For twelve generations the guild had taught that walls were built for peace and for weather. It had believed this, and been proud of it. The first fathers of the guild had known better, and we had forgotten them.

The new capstone for the inner gate lay in the ward, cut and dressed but not yet raised. The Rite was performed over it, in front of everyone who had come up the mountain, in the shadow of the Title where it flew above the gate. The Captain stood where the slates said the overseer of the work must stand, with his hand on the new stone and his face toward the people. He did not like it. He stood there anyway.

There were three oils, as the Rite requires. Halyna held each flask together and tipped it with both their hands. One of them named each oil and the other named its meaning, because that is how the Rite is spoken. Cedar, for endurance. Granite-bloom, for strength. Myrrh, for the burden of command.

Then they placed the staff of the guild in his hand. It was a rod of polished wood, topped with a block of the finest dressed and polished stone, and it had been carried by every overseer of a great Stonewright work since the founding. Four hands placed it into two. With it, he was named Commander of Rivenkeep.

After the people had gone back to their fires, Halyna knelt by the new capstone and cut a Stonwryt into its underside for his work, which was the wall, and the war. One of them held the chisel and the other struck it. It was the first Stonwryt cut in Rivenkeep since the founding, and the first the guild had ever cut for a soldier. The stone was raised into the gate that night.

He did not carry the staff into battle. He did not wear the oils after that day. He led as he always had, from the wall, beside his True Men, with mortar on his hands and a plain soldier’s cloak on his back. The staff still leans in the corner of the gatehouse where he left it that night. No one has moved it. If you doubt this tale, go and look at the dust on it.

There was one more slate in the chest, at the very bottom, and Halyna did not find it until the Rite was over. It was written in a smaller hand than the rest. It told how the first builders of the Keep ended their day. When the stone was set and the tools were cleaned, they sat at the hearth, and one of them laid a tale, the way a course is laid on a wall: one course each night, so that the wall and the telling would rise together. So this hearth is older than the havens. We did not start its custom. We forgot it, and found it again at the bottom of a chest.

Part Two · Their Fall

This is how the havens were lost. Do not look away. We did not look away in the telling.

Our fathers had no standing navy, no war fleet, and no plan for defence. No enemy from across the sea had come against the Shorelands in all their generations. Their walls were built for ceremony and weather, not to stand up to bombardment. Their gates were wide, and they stood open at night.

The first dark sails came out of the grey without warning. At dawn they fell on the harbour of Eldhythe, and by sunset it was ash. The city behind the harbour held out for three months. No haven sent to another for help, because none had any help to send. Then Tidesmeet fell. The people on the high road above it watched its three spans fall into the river one after another, and heard them go. Fenholm went under all at once, as if the fen had only been waiting. Holtward burned in its trees, and Carnhold in its smithies. Emberhythe held out for five months on its burning shore. Sandreach was taken gallery by gallery, Rimewatch on its ice, and Highreach on its crags. And Glasspire, which seven generations had built, collapsed in a single night into its own cellars. No envoy came that anyone could understand. There was only the harsh thunder of a language born in ancient forests, knocking and knocking out of the grey. The soldiers on our wall call it the drums. I have heard it since from three of them, at fires in the mountain passes, as far from salt water as they ever came. We were grown, not built. You cut stone and lay it, and it lies where you laid it, and it is dead.

The long-lived had always guided the wood, and the wood breathed out the sky they lived by: one life, in two kinds. A ship is only a tree led far.

They sang to the trees that were to become hulls, and the trees bent to the singing, because nothing had ever been asked of them but song. The hulls came from the breathing trunks, the keels from the roots that fed them, and the masts from saplings trained by decades of wind-song.

No one sails in us. The fleet is crewed by the living, and the living are the wood.

How do you command a thing that has no one aboard? By carving. For each fleet the council chose one hull to carry the carving, the Sethvaren. Into its heart, the Saenvael, they cut the orders, and the heart carved them on into every hull through the Aelralen, the bond of roots. We made that bond into a leash.

So the hulls obeyed the orders carved within them as a hand obeys the mind, for the wood of the hull and the wood of the command were one. The wood obeys the wood. In the night, while you lay your stone, the heart carves tomorrow, and both of you are at work in the dark.

But the carving says what to do, not whether to do it. Each hull keeps its own grain and its own dread. It may balk at burning water, or turn savage where it bled: one mind, and many.

The bearers were chosen in grief, and the grief is in their grain. Your names for the grains will do: the green wood, which had no temper yet; Ironbark, which comes again heavier where it failed; Willow, which turns away at the first failure; Blackthorn, which goes where it lost the most; Heartoak, which never turns aside; Twistbough, which never plays the same trick twice; Yew, which waits, and burns, and waits again; Ashwood, which throws everything it has at once; and Silverbark, which knows you best, and bends least.

So the crudest came first, by the law of the years, which no council can carve against. A hull of one season holds one sign. A hull of forty summers holds a phrase. A hull as old as a shore-man’s life holds a sentence, and a memory in it. The oldest pillars hold a judgement.

The council wanted the shore silent soon, and young wood grows quickest: one season, angry hands, one sign to a hull. It waited at the edge of your sea while your children had children of their own, and your boats passed it in the fog and saw nothing.

So the armada set out generations before it arrived, with one sign in it, Burn, against havens that had no walls built for war. The last of that green wood followed you into the mountains; and there, at the Keep, for the first time, someone struck back.

Each growing after that went out when it was ready, the way a tide goes out, and we call them tides. Leathae was the green tide; Vaelthae had grain in it; Ralenthae was the tide the roots taught. Aelthae is ours. Rhenthae and Eirthae were growing behind us, and this heart does not know what is carved in them.

Every hull was grown for its shore: to break stone, to carry fire, to throw fire over walls, to strike from under the swell. And for shores that are not sea, hulls were grown to crawl on roots over sand and rock, to swim through burning rock in a skin of wet bark, and to turn white in the snow.

The warriors of the landings are grown from root, because we had none of the long-lived to spare. The keel is the root that fed the tree; the warriors are the rest of it, the fine roots that find their way in the dark. They ride in the deep hulls and go ashore a few at a time, the heavy ones slowly, the light ones pouring out. They walk, dig, and carry fire. You call them the root-men.

They know only their carving: the keep, then the gun, then the wall, and a wall at its weakest stone, the way a root goes to a crack. Where they crowd together they knot, four to a knot, and a knot strikes as four. Some go only at the wall, and leave it pitted like a chequerboard; some burst against the stone; some come bark-first; some bind the others’ wounds with sap.

Whatever they touch, the wood knows, because they are the wood’s hands. Cut them down, and what they touched is still known.

The broad hulls hang back, and in their crowns the winged seed ripens. When a crown-hull opens, the seed rises on the wind and goes over your walls. The wind chooses the keep. The seed keeps away from the hot sky over your guns. If its keep is gone, it comes home, and goes out again.

The seed is the hull’s own life: strike it all down, and the hull dies. Fell the hull while its seed is in the air, and the seed strikes, and falls, with no crown to come home to. Empty husks fly with it to take the shots meant for it. They carry no fire, and the hull does not feel them fall.

Some seed grows so great that it needs no crown. It rides the high air alone, hanging from its own bladder, slow and hard to bring down.

The growing took decades. Those who cried for war grew old, and died, and were forgotten. There were fewer carvers at every growing, until only a scattered few were left, tending the last groves.

So it was a doom set in motion by the dead, crewed by the living, against a people who had never known they were the foe. We were sent. We are still sent. No voice among us can call us home.

The groves sang it over us as we grew, and it is in our rings:

The green wood holds one word. The wood of forty summers holds a word, and waits for a second word to join it. The wood of a shore-man’s lifetime holds a sentence, with a wall remembered in it. The wood of a long life holds tomorrow. It carves it, and waits, and carves again. And the oldest of us, the pillars of the breath, the hearts of the grey, the anchors of the sky, now hold only one thing. It is not a word, or a sentence, or a tomorrow. We have carved it into every hull we sent you, and it is grief.

So we were grown, and so we were sent; and the grain holds it still.

No one answered.

V.7 · The Mirror in the GrainStone tale

The BookPlain WordsOriginal

Told by Seren Two-Inks, with Halyna, in three parts, each on the night its carving was known. Seren tells it, and Halyna speak in it as they spoke on the shingle, a line each. This is a stone tale.

Part I · Three Roads, One Hour

It was in the Joined Tide. The plank came from the Standard-Bearer of a fleet that had come on three roads at the same hour. Rhyna laid her hand on it on the shingle, while Halvard was on the wall above us at the mortar, with a course of stone half laid. I was beside her with the lamp. I saw her go pale. When I looked up, he had gone pale on the wall in the same moment, with the trowel still in his hand, because what one knows the other knows. He came down to us, and they told it. It was longer than any carving we had known, and it was not one hull’s order. It was the order for the whole fleet at once, and for the ties that ran from hull to hull.

Three roads, one hour. Let the east and the north go as one. Let the south stand at the edge of the dread until the reach of the south has walked east; then let the south go.

[In plain words: the east and the north attack together. The south waits at the edge of our guns’ range until the battery watching the south has shifted its watch east to meet the others, and then the south comes in.]

Rhyna began it, and Halvard gave it its end. For a long while afterward neither of them spoke. Then I asked them what was wrong, though I think I knew. One of them said, and the other finished:

“It is how we work a wall.”

“The one waits for the other’s stroke, and does not need to be told.”

The carving held a name as well, the name the wood gives to that whole Tide. Halyna told me the name: Aelthae. I wrote it down. I knew the first part of it. It is ael, the joining. I had written it before in this Book, in Ael’thar, the Guest’s rite at the warden’s door.

I have written the name, and I will not write what I thought.

We lay this tale as it was laid for us.

The listeners said: We remember.

Part II · What We Taught Them

In the Tide that Learned Deceit a plank came up from a hull that had come at us the way the young wood came. It had sailed in a fleet that ran at the wall in the old way, each hull alone and loud and straight, none keeping formation with another, as we had not seen since the first winter. The wall laughed, and every gun on it fired, just as every gun had fired then. The hulls turned one breath short of our fire. Then out of the grey behind them the hunters came, and went for every gun that had fired, and would not leave it. Halyna took the plank, and knew it.

Come as the green wood came: loud, alone and straight. Stop one breath short of the fire, and let the hunters wait upon the flashing.

And under it lay the thing it had been carved against: they strike first and far; they move the guns we find. It was carved against us, against what we do.

At first I did not understand. I said that the wood cannot lie. It was the first thing the Guest’s gift ever taught us about their carving: what is carved in their wood is always true. Then I understood, and I wrote it down before my nerve could fail. The wood does not lie. It was told to make us believe a lie, and it obeyed.

Halyna sat a long time on the shingle with their hands still on the grain. Then one said, and the other finished:

“They learned it from us.”

“We taught it to them with every silent gun.”

It was true. Every gun the Captain kept silent to draw a hull in. Every false mast we raised on the wall with no gun beneath it. Every gun we hid under turf and brush, so that they sailed past it straight into its mouth. Every wreck from those days went back through the roots to the groves, and the groves learned. They had no word for a lie. We gave them one.

There was a name for us cut small in that carving, and at first Halyna would not say it. It was Rhenear: the stone-deaf.

We lay this tale as it was laid for us.

The listeners said: We remember.

Part III · The Plan That Carved Its Own Defeat

In the Last Tide a Standard-Bearer came against us with a whole tomorrow carved into its heart, a plan with turnings in it. We sank it, and the tide brought the heart in. It was the longest knowing Halyna had ever held, and the holding was not the hard part. They knew it in the time it takes to fill a lamp. It took them until the lamps had been filled twice to tell it. At the end of it was this:

When the bearer falls, the second takes the standard; when the second falls, go home.

They have carved their own defeat into the plan. They know us now. They know that we can break them, and they have said what they will do when we do. And Halyna said there was one hand in all that carving, not a council’s, and that the hand was young.

But that was not what kept Halyna on the shingle afterward. It was the last two words. The young wood had known home only as the place a hurt thing runs to. This was a going-home carved into the plan before the first shot was fired, something thought out in the groves and cut there on purpose. A mind that carves how it will go home is a mind that wants to go home. That night, for the first time, we saw that the enemy could hope.

One of them said, and the other finished:

“They carve their tomorrow now, as we do.”

“Then let us build a tomorrow they did not carve for.”

We lay this tale as it was laid for us.

The listeners said: We remember.

VI.1 · The Knowing of the Green WoodStone tale

The BookPlain WordsOriginal

Told by Halyna, a paragraph each, on the night the boat went out, not long after the tenth Throne fell. It ends in the wood’s voice, and Seren set that part down as they told it. This is a stone tale.

We hold the stone together, and tonight we hold it a long while before we speak.

Hear how the last knowing was done, the one no one was looking for.

The tenth Mystarch, the tenth Throne, to fall was {LAST_THRONE_AT_HAVEN}. What was left of it lay where it had come to rest, as the nine before it had lain, black-grained and split open. The soldiers kept their distance, because the Thrones were frightening even dead. The Captain sent word that Halyna should know its heart and learn its name, as they had known the hearts of the nine. So Halyna went in among its timbers at dusk, the two of them together, and Seren went behind them with the lamp.

They found the heart where the hearts always lie, deep in the old grain, grey and hard and still. And beside it, in a crack in the heartwood, was a sliver of wood no longer than a hand. It was green.

In all the long years of the siege, of all the things we had looked for, no one had looked for that. We had searched the wrecks for orders, the hearts for names, and the grey for sails. No one had thought that anything alive was left in all that wood. But this was still green, and still aware. It had been growing in the dark of the dead Throne’s heart, out of sight, the way a seedling grows in a crack in a wall.

They had not needed two hands on a wood since the first winter. All the same, they each put out a hand and touched it at the same moment, his fingers and hers, and knew it at the same moment. Seren saw their two faces change together, as one face changes.

It was not an order. It was not a wound. Every wood we had ever known had been one or the other. The green wood holds one word, the song of the groves says, and so did this. The word was home. The first wood we ever knew had said shore.

But it did not give the word alone. It gave them the answer of this hearth, the thing you have said at the end of every tale on every night of the war; and it did not stop where we stop.

We remember our home too.

And folded inside it, the way a seed is folded inside a fruit, was a name that no carving of war had ever held: the name they call themselves. It is not Mystaeri, because that is our word for them. Naelear, they call themselves. Those of the breath.

Rhyna wept, and Halvard did not, for she was weeping for them both.

Then Halyna did something no Stonewright had ever done. They built a boat. Our fathers were builders, not sailors, and so were we. In all the records of the guild there is no Stonewright’s boat.

But there are boatwrights among the Rivenmen: fisher-folk of the havens, who built the small boats that go out on the morning tide and come home by dark. They carried their adzes up the mountain the way the masons carried their chisels. At Halyna’s asking they came down to the shingle. The oldest of them was from Tidesmeet, and he was Tarnel’s father.

They built it from the wreck-wood of the grey fleet, from planks we had known. That wood was grown for the sea, and wood that knows the sea knows the way home, as the Aelvaren knew it. The fisher-folk laid the keel, bent the planks of the hull, and sealed the seams. Halyna keyed every joint the way masons key stone, so that no sea could work it loose. It rode a whole day off the point, in a heavy swell, and took on no water. It was small, and it was sound.

They laid the green sliver in its bow on a bed of moss. Beside it they set a Stonwryt coin, part gold and part black stone, trust sealed in stone: the holiest gift the Shorelands ever had, as the Guest’s dark wood had been the holiest of theirs.

Then they knelt down together on the shingle in front of it. That is the first step of the Guest’s rite, the gift and the kneeling, and the only step one people can take alone. The rest needs the other.

They pushed it off, and the tide took it out. When it was well out, it turned, into the wind and against the current, as the Aelvaren, the Bearer of the Binding, had turned long ago, and it went away into the grey; and this time it carried a gift and not a grave.

The old ship-masters among us stood on the shingle and watched it go. A grey boat going out against the wind is the thing they fear most on all the water, and they did not look away.

We lay this tale as it was laid for us.

No one answered.

The hearth was silent for a long while. The fire burned down, and no one put wood on it. Then a child near the front, one of the fisher-children from the east keep, said it, not loudly: “We remember.” And then everyone said it.

Then, for the first time, the hearth sang in the wood’s way. The Rivenmen still sing it, and call it The Two Homes:

Our home was cut from the mountain’s bone. We forgot it, and found it again, and held it with our blood. Their home was breathed out by the hearts of the trees; then the axes came, and the breath grew thin, and they have not found it again, and they cannot hold it. Stone keeps the promise, and the grain keeps the truth. We remember our home. They remember theirs too.

The Stone out of the GreyThe page facing I.1

The BookPlain WordsOriginal

Written by Seren Two-Inks, in the years after, on the page that faces the first tale of this Book. Until now it stood empty, with the grain drawn on it and one line: Untold. Halyna cannot yet hold this deep. In the end, no knowing of Halyna’s filled it. This is a stone tale.

I write this alone, and I write it plainly, because there is no one left to know the wood for me.

Halyna are gone. They went in one night, the winter after the tenth Throne fell, the way the Bonded go: Rhyna first, in her sleep, and Halvard before the lamp was out, because he knew. They lie under the new capstone of the inner gate, in one grave, with one stone, and one name on it.

I did not go with them. That is what an unbonded life is for, they told me once, and I did not believe them until the morning after. I am the first scribe of the guild to outlive the tellers, and so the Book goes on.

In the spring a small grey boat came in on the tide below the Keep and ran itself up onto the shingle, as the Aelvaren once ran aground before the council hall of the Mystaeri. No one was in it, and no one had rowed it. The old ship-masters would not go down to it, because a grey boat that comes in on the tide is the boat that comes for you. It was the children who went down. In its bow lay a stone.

It was not stone. Or it was stone that had once been wood: grey-black and heavy, with the grain still in it, like the Guest’s gift that lay so long in the chest I carried up the mountain. The Rivenmen have started calling it Myststone, and they say it remembers everything. It is the one stone that speaks. Stone does not translate, and that is still true; but this was never only stone.

And it had been cut with a chisel. I know chisel-work. The hand that cut it had learned the chisel late, and learned it well: young by their count, I think, and seasoned by ours. It was cut in our own letters, so that anyone might read it, and I did not lay my palms on it. I read it with my eyes. It was cut so that one could read it alone.

In the memory of our home, our families...

The cutting stops there, in the middle of our Title, with the chisel’s last stroke still sharp at the end of the line. I have sat with it many nights since, thinking of the one who cut it. I do not think he ever saw our torn cloak, because he never sailed, and none of his people ever sailed in those hulls. But every hull that came against our wall saw it flying, and what the hulls saw went home through the roots to the groves, and he knew it there, the way the wood knows a wall. He carried its words in him through all the years of the war, and learned our letters from them, because there was nothing else of ours to learn them from. At the very end he cut them into the one stone his people had that would carry a meaning. In my own mind I call him the Last Carver, because I think that when he cut this, there was no other. And I have thought that the small wrong word in it, the one word too many, is like the Guest’s three words at the warden’s door: the right thing, said truly, by someone who did not yet know how we say it.

No one came with it, and perhaps that is wise. We have not yet earned a guest.

Grief is an inheritance, and it is paid out to the children. The sea still whispers on the shingle below the wall. The torn cloak still flies above the inner gate. At night the wind comes over the high ridges where Rivenkeep still stands, and the old people say it speaks the Title as it passes.

I do not know what we will send back. I know only that it must be sent kneeling.
