OF THIS BOOK

The BookPlain WordsOriginal

Written by Seren Two-Inks, apprentice to Halyna and the third of their hearth, at the front of this Book, when the Book was already half full.

This is the Book of the Riven Stone. It holds the tales that are laid at the Long Hearth of Rivenkeep, in the hall behind the inner gate, under the wall where the torn cloak flies. They are laid in the hours after an assault, when the guns are cooling and the mortar has not yet set, and there is nothing left to do with the hands but hold them to the fire.

We did not make the custom. The first builders of the Keep made it, and we forgot it, and found it again at the bottom of a chest in the deepest vault, as you will read. When their stone was set and their tools were cleaned, the first builders sat at this hearth, and each night one of them laid a tale. We do as they did. Our wall is rising again, and so is this Book.

Beside the hearth, in a niche of the hearth-wall, lies the old capstone of the inner gate. It fell in the forgotten years and lay in the gateway until Halyna turned it over. Halyna are the elders of the guild at this Keep. They are of the Aetherbonded, whom the guild calls simply the Bonded, and Halyna is the one name the two of them share; singly they are Halvard and Rhyna. Halvard’s fathers cut that stone and Rhyna’s forebears laid it, and the Stonwryt of the first builders is still sharp on its underside. Whoever holds it tells. When the tale is done the teller sets it back in its niche, and we say that the tale is laid; for a tale that is only told is gone by morning, and a tale that is laid is part of the wall.

When a tale of ours is laid, the hearth answers it, all together: We remember. The answer comes from the first words of the Captain’s Title, and it is the only thing at this hearth that is always said the same way.

Not every tale in this Book is ours. Some are the wood’s. They come from the wreck-wood that the tide brings in under our wall, and from the dark stone that lies in the chest I carried up the mountain, and from other wood besides. The wood is not read, for there are no words in it. Halyna touch it, and its tale is known to them whole, as a memory is known, the beginning and the middle and the end of it at once. One of them need only touch it, for what the one knows, the other knows. The knowing is not the hard thing. The telling is. Words can be turned, and a knowing cannot; so they tell it in the fewest and plainest words they can find, and set each word down like a marking-stone where the knowing lies. The wood’s tales are not told with the stone. When a wood telling ends, the hearth does not answer, for it does not know how. I have written that silence down each time, as it was.

I do not know the wood myself. I am not of the Bonded: the guild calls me unbonded, and after I came up the mountain, Halyna took me into their hearth as its third. I laid my hand on the wood once, and let go, for what came into me was too great for me to hold. So I write down what Halyna tell. Every scribe of the guild before me was one of a bonded pair; why this Book is written by one who is not, Halyna tell in its own place, to the children.

Halyna would have the wood’s tales in this Book. “A tale told from one side of a wall,” one of them said, and the other finished, “is half a tale.” So where one thing was seen from both sides, the two tellings are set facing each other, ours on the left leaf and the wood’s on the right. Where the wood’s leaf could not yet be told, I have left it blank, with only the grain showing. Whoever opens this Book may see the shape of what we did not yet understand, and wait with us.

When Halyna tell, they take the tale by turns, a paragraph each, for they know the tales as they know each other, and neither waits on the other. I have used two inks, one for each of them, and they would not have me mark which is whose. When they speak a single line between them, one beginning it and the other ending it, that is in two inks as well.

The Captain does not tell. He sits at the end of the bench with his hood up and hears his own deeds told, and says nothing, unless it is to say that it was not like that. On the wall and in the orders he is the Commander, for so the Rite named him. At this hearth he is the Captain still, as he would have it.

When we speak of our fathers we call them Shorelanders, for so they were, and we call ourselves we. The folk behind the grey we call the Mystaeri, the mist-folk. That is our word for them, and not theirs.

Where a carving was broken, or a knowing could not be told, I have said so, and have not mended it with guesses. Where the tales do not agree, I have kept both.

This Book is ink. Halyna mean it to be cut into the granite of this hall when there is peace enough to cut it. Until that day it is kept in the chest I carried, with the mute stones.

THE INVOCATION

The BookPlain WordsOriginal

Spoken at the Long Hearth before the first tale of a night, and set here before the first tale of the Book.

IN MEMORY OF OUR HOME, OUR FAMILIES, OUR GOD, OUR FREEDOMS, OUR PEACE.

Sit, if there is room, and stand if there is not. The guns are cooling, and the mortar will not set before dark.

Here is the hearth, and here is the stone that fell from the inner gate. Whoever holds it tells.

We tell these tales because we forgot once. Our fathers forgot a keep, and it crumbled. Their fathers forgot a wrong, and it came back to us under grey sails. So we tell all of it: the building and the breaking, and what we did as well as what was done to us.

Some of these tales are ours, and they are cut plain, like the stones of the wall. Some are the wood’s, and those are hard to tell, and will not be hurried. Where the two do not agree, we have kept both.

Hold the stone. Lay the tale. When it is laid, the hearth will answer.

And the hearth said: We remember.

BOOK ONE · THE BOOK OF THE WALL

How we came to stand.

I.1 · The Torn CloakStone leaf

The BookPlain WordsOriginal

Laid by Sergeant Kael, Kael the Counter, oath-keeper of the True Men, on the first night this hearth asked for a tale. He would not let me change a word of it, and I have not.

Give me the stone. No, I will stand. I have sat enough this winter.

I swear on the lintel of the western stair, where the old wardens cut their names, that I will tell this plain, for I have no other way of telling. I was on the gate that day. I am a counter of things and not a singer, and what I did not count I will not tell you.

The column came in at dusk, and I counted it as it came: old men, and women with bundles, and children asleep on their feet, and soldiers of six broken companies who no longer knew their officers. Two thousand and some hundreds. We had walked a month to reach a castle, and we reached a heap of stones. The outer gate hung from one hinge. The ward behind it was grass to the knee. Where the songs had put a great hall there was a roof fallen in, and snow on the hearth where you sit now.

Some shook. Some wept. Some fell and would not rise.

Then the crying began, and it was not one cry. Some cried that we should send to the grey sails and beg for terms, though no one had ever understood a word the grey sails said. Some cried for the high passes and the snow beyond them. Some cried that we should go down into the cellars of the Keep and bar the doors and wait, as if the sea would forget us. I had heard men break before. I had never heard a people break. It is a sound like ice going out on a river.

Now I will tell you what the Captain was, and first what he was not. He was no general, and the generals were slain. He was no politician, and the politicians were forgotten. He was no nobleman, and the nobles were scattered. He was no Stonewright, and the Stonewrights sat in the grass with their chisels in their laps and despaired. He was the captain of a small company, and we were his company. He had found us one by one, in garrisons and on roads, twelve of us in all, and bound us by nothing but trust. We were soldiers who kept our oaths, who built what we promised, who fought only when the cause was just. That is all a True Man is. I say it so you will know it is not much. It is not little, either.

He did not want to lead. I swear to that as well. All through the long retreat he waited for someone whose place it was to lead to take it up, and none did, for the leaders were dead. Do not mistake me. It was not that the greater men were gone. There was no greater man. I do not think his equal ever lived.

The True Men looked to their Captain. The Captain looked at the wall.

Then he climbed it. He had taken a coal from the dead hearth as he came through the hall, and I did not know why. He went up the broken face of the inner wall alone, by the stones that stood proud of the mortar, and we stood at the foot and let him, for he had not told us to follow. At the top, where the wall still stood whole, he took off his cloak. It was a plain soldier’s cloak, crimson once and gone to the colour of old brick. He tore it down the back. He took a pike out of the rubble and bound the half of it there. Then he knelt on the capstones with the coal, and wrote, slowly, as a man writes who has not written much.

I was the first up after him, and the first to read it. I read it out, and my voice was not steady, and I will not pretend it was:

IN MEMORY OF OUR HOME, OUR FAMILIES, OUR GOD, OUR FREEDOMS, OUR PEACE.

He set the pike in a crack of the wall and gave it to the wind. That was the last unbroken wall of the Shorelands, and that was the banner above it.

Now hear who answered, and in what order, for I counted. The True Men answered first, and I will name them, for a count is not a count until it is named. Stannard of the ridge, the quarryman. Corlen of the harbour. Tamm of the three spans. Marl the fen-born. Aske of the canopy. Garvel of the smithies. Harl of the burning shore. Ulden of the galleries. Hesk of the mere. Lanner of the crags. Pellow of the spire. And Kael the Counter, oath-keeper of the True Men; that is me, of no haven, for he found me on a road. Twelve. I counted us twice, and it did not take long.

Then the soldiers of the six companies, who had walked the long retreat beside us; they are our troops now, a troop to each True Man, and they trust us as we trust him. Then the people of the column, who had carried their children on their backs through the passes: one voice, and a dozen, and then hundreds, until I could not count, and I stopped trying. It is the only time I ever stopped. It was not by command, for he gave none. He had only written down what we had left, and it was enough.

That is why the pennant on every keep of ours is crimson. It is the colour of that cloak.

He is sitting there now, at the end of the bench, with his hood up. He will tell you it was not like that. It was like that.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

Then the True Men sang, as they sing it still:

The gate was broken, the lords were scattered, the grey sails gathered along the shore; one man climbed where the stones lay loosened and tore the cloak his shoulders wore. He wrote five things a man might die for, and none of us has needed more.

And the hearth said: We remember.

I.2 · The Cry of the BondedStone leaf

The BookPlain WordsOriginal

Laid by Seren Two-Inks, apprentice to Halyna and scribe of this Book, on the night after the first assault the Rivenmen held. She was a child of the guild on the day she tells of, and sat in the courtyard with the rest.

“Hold the stone,” Halyna told me when I was small and afraid to speak at this hearth. “Hold it, and it will hold you.” So I hold it.

I was in the courtyard that day among the Stonewrights, for I had been called to the guild already, though I had no craft yet but carrying water. We sat in the old ward where it had gone to grass, some hundreds of us, masters and prentices, and the bonded pairs a little apart. I had walked the passes beside a boy of the guild, and I came into that courtyard alone. We had come out of ten havens. We had watched every one of them die, and we had walked away from each with our chisels in our packs, and not one of us had lifted a hand, for our creed forbade it. Build, do not destroy. Protect, do not attack. No Stonewright ever bore a weapon. Mortar and chisel were our only arms, and they had not been enough.

The faith that had sustained us through twelve generations was on us still, but it sat on us like a wet cloak. I think every elder in that courtyard was turning over the same hard thing and would not say it aloud: that walls built for peace must sometimes be defended with blood. And if that was so, whose blood? And whose hands?

Then the Captain came down from the wall. I had seen the cloak go up, and heard the soldiers shout, and did not know what it had to do with us. He walked into the middle of the courtyard. He was not tall. There was coal-black on his fingers. He looked at the elders, and at the broken gate behind them, and he asked one question. It is the only speech of his that anyone at this hearth can repeat:

“Your walls are falling. Will you build, or will you watch and die?”

No one answered him. I remember the wind in the grass.

Then Halyna stood up. You know them by that name now. The guild gave it to them when they were wed, as it gives one to every bonded pair, and by it they are called. The guild calls the Aetherbonded simply the Bonded, and Halyna are the Bonded of the Keep. I did not know them then, save as two elders who sat apart and spoke to no one, for they had no need. I will name them singly this once, for the tale needs it. She was Elderess Rhyna, Rhyna of the Inner Gate, whose bonded forebears had laid the capstone of that very gate in the age before the havens. He was Elder Halvard, Halvard Stone-Warden, of the quarry-wardens of the high ridge, whose fathers had cut that same stone. They were husband and wife, as the Bonded are, and had been one since the cradle. They looked up at the torn cloak, and they bowed to it in the same breath, for the Bonded do not wait on each other. Then their two chisels went up as one and took the last of the light.

The cry came out of them both, and it was one cry. She began it:

“The mortar cries out from the ground. Rise, Stonewrights! Lay stone upon stone. Stand every wall to the very end.”

And he ended it, with his chisel beside hers:

“Rise! Rise! Rise!”

Some tell it that she called and he answered, as though there were two cries and a silence between them. There was no silence. It was one cry in two throats, and hers came first only because a cry must begin somewhere.

Then the whole guild was on its feet, and I with them, though I do not remember standing. We took up the shout, and the soldiers on the wall took it from us, until the walls of Rivenkeep itself seemed to tremble. Old stone does not tremble. I felt it do so under my feet.

The terms were made that night in the ruin of this hall, and Halyna made them. The Stonewrights would not take up arms. They never had and never would, and the Captain did not ask it. But they would build between the volleys, and mend a breach while the guns still spoke, with the speed and precision that only Aetherbonded pairs could achieve, for a bonded pair works as one body with four hands. One of them said the first half to the Captain and the other the rest, and I could not tell you which. It has been the guild’s word ever since: “Not the sword, but the stone. Not the killing, but the keeping.”

So the siege day was shared out, and this hearth has names for its hours. There is the laying, when the Stonewrights raise and mend. There is the hauling, when the guns are dragged to where the Captain wants them. There is the holding, when the grey sails come in and the wall must stand, and every battery fights under one of the Captain’s True Men. Every gun that is hauled is a course of stone not laid, for it is the same hands on the ropes; and the Captain knows it, and moves a gun as a careful man spends silver. And wherever you see a Stonewright on the wall in the holding, look again. You will see two.

We sang that first night by torchlight in the gap of the inner gate as we laid, one voice to the comma and the other the rest, as Halyna taught us:

Stone upon stone, and the stone keeps faith; hand over hand, and the hand holds true; two at the gate when the grey sails gather, two at the gate, and the gate stands through.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

And the hearth said: We remember.

I.3 · The Captain’s Long BreathStone leaf

The BookPlain WordsOriginal

Laid by Private Hale, Hale the runner, who carries word of what is seen along the wall in the holding, in the first year of the siege. It is a saying of the hearth, and he tells where it came from; Kael the Counter mends it at the end, as he mends most things.

Give me the stone, then. I am not a teller. I run.

There is a saying at this hearth that you will hear before you have been a month on the wall. I think it began with me, though Voss says it is older.

A day has four-and-twenty hours in it, siege or no siege. In the laying they go slowly, for stone is heavy. In the hauling they go slowly, for guns are heavier. But in the holding, when the grey sails come in, an hour goes by in a heartbeat, and a whole day in four-and-twenty of them, and a man cannot hold one hour still long enough to think in it. So it was in my first holding. The sails came out of the grey faster than I could count them, and the guns spoke, and the smoke came down, and afterward I could not have told you whether an hour had gone or a morning.

Every gun on this wall stands where the Captain set it, and every battery has one of his True Men over it. Before the sails come, the Captain tells each True Man his mind: where to watch, what shot to lay, and when to hold. In the holding each True Man fights his battery by his own judgment, for they know the Captain’s mind; that is why each battery fights on its own. The Captain does not send his word along the wall. When he must, he calls it himself, by the horn, to one battery at a time. There are three calls, and every True Man knows them. Shift your ground: that is the water a battery watches, and not the stones it stands on. Change your shot: for the water, or the shore, or the sky. And hold, or loose. A runner carries none of these. What I carry is what is seen.

In those first days there was only one battery on the wall, and it was Kael’s. That day Kael sent me with what his lookout had seen: a hull that had hung back in the grey, where none had hung before, and was coming in now, late, when the rest were spent. I ran it along the wall-walk with my head down, and came to the Captain.

He was standing still. That is what I remember first. Everything on that wall was moving but him. He had one hand on the parapet above the gate and the horn in the other, and he was watching one hull among a dozen, the grey-sailed thing coming in late on the tide, and he did not look round at me. He heard me out. Then he lifted the horn.

And the hour began to stretch. It did not stop short, as a cart stops against a stone; it slowed, as a cart slows on a long hill, until it hardly moved at all. I have no better way to say it. I stood at his shoulder and the hour went long around him, and I could see the water sheet off that hull’s bow, and I could count the ripples going down its sail, one and two and three, as you count the rings on a cut log; and each of them, I think, was a minute. I had time to be afraid. Then I had time to be ashamed of it. Then I had time to stop.

Then he gave Kael’s battery its call, and the horn carried it, and the hour let go of us slowly, as a held breath goes out, until the world was back at its own pace. The guns went. The hull broke up in the one stretch of that water where it could hurt no one, where he had meant it to.

So the men say: For the Captain, an hour lasts as long as a day.

They say it as though it were a gift the Stonewrights laid on him. I said it so myself, the first winter. I know better now. It is not that he is quick. It is that he will not be hurried.

Kael said it better, from across the fire, and I give it to you as he said it: “It is not the world that slows, lad. It is the Captain who will not hurry. The moment he chose was the moment that came.”

In the age before the havens, when there was no city yet on the eastern coasts and no road between the mountains and the sea, the first Stonewrights came up onto the high ridges. They found a place where the ridge is riven in two, as though a great blade had come down on it, and in the cleft they raised a hold out of the ridge’s own grey stone. This was Rivenkeep, the first work of the first builders. Eldhythe is called the Eldest Haven, and rightly; but it is the eldest only of the havens, for the Keep was old before the first stone of Eldhythe was cut.

The quarry-wardens of the high ridge cut its stone, and their names are cut in the lintel of the western stair, one beneath another, for as many generations as the quarry was worked. Halvard Stone-Warden is of their line. And Aldwena, a bonded pair of the first builders, laid the capstone of the inner gate with their own four hands, and Rhyna of the Inner Gate is of their line. It is a vow sealed in living rock, earned by toil and skill: a master’s promise that the wall will hold. It cannot be bought. It can only be earned, by the hands that laid the wall, and it is cut where no eye will see it again unless the wall falls.

In later days, when the havens were raised and grew rich, the Shorelanders named their coin for it. The Stonwryt coin was part gold and part black stone, and it was minted only by bonded master-pairs of the guild, in workshops no outsider had ever entered. So the markets of the havens stood on the same footing as their temples: trust, sealed in stone.

Then the folk went down. The ridges were cold and the harbours were warm, and trade came up the coasts and not over the mountains. The Stonewrights followed the folk, for the folk needed temples and walls. Generation by generation the Keep was left, until it was more myth than memory: the castle that had never fallen, a name in the songs, a place the children of the havens believed had never been.

No one kept it. No Stonewright climbed up to point the mortar or to clear the drains. The rain came into the joints, and the frost came after the rain, and every winter the ice split what the rain had loosened. It had never fallen. It had only been left.

When the havens fell one by one, the folk who lived fled up the mountain roads to the one place the songs said would hold. What they found was a shadow: broken gates, crumbled walls, a hall with its roof lying in its hearth. What man could not destroy, time had withered. Forgetting had done what no enemy ever could.

Part Two · Of Their Fall

Hear now how the havens were lost, and do not look away, for we did not look away in the telling.

Our fathers had no standing navy, no war fleet, no defensive doctrine. No enemy from beyond the sea had come against the Shorelands in all their generations. Their walls were built for ceremony and weather, not for bombardment, and their gates were wide, and stood open at night.

The first dark sails came out of the grey without warning. At dawn they fell upon the harbour of Eldhythe, and by sunset it was ash. The city behind the harbour held three months. No haven sent to another for help, for none had any help to send. Then Tidesmeet fell, and the people on the high road above it watched its three spans go down into the river one after another, and heard them go. Fenholm went under all at once, as if the fen had only been waiting. Holtward burned in its trees and Carnhold in its smithies. Emberhythe held five months on its burning shore. Sandreach was taken gallery by gallery, and Rimewatch on its ice, and Highreach on its crags. And Glasspire, that seven generations had raised, collapsed in a single night into its own cellars, and the dark beneath it lies open still.

No envoy came that anyone could understand. There was only the harsh thunder of a tongue born in ancient forests, knocking and knocking out of the grey; the soldiers on our wall call it the drums. And no one stood upon the decks. Our fathers looked for a captain on those ships and saw none, nor any crew, and they called it the ghost fleet. Who had sent it they did not know; and because it came out of the fog the sailors called the Mystlands, they named the senders the Mystaeri, the mist-folk. We use both names still.

In the oldest archives of the guild the fog is written down as a blackish veil at the edge of the sea, and after that only as a question, asked in the night-watches and never answered. What is the grey? What does it keep? Who breathes it? The masters who asked were long dead before any answer came, and the answer, when it came, was not written by us.

Then came the high summer of the Shorelanders’ pride. The havens were rich and their roads were heavy with carts, and the counsel of the Stonewrights had come to seem a voice from humbler days, a thing the grandmothers said. In those years the ships of the coast went further out than they had ever dared, along the edge of the grey, and there they found the black pillars of the fog, which the ship-masters named the Mystholders, for they seemed to hold the grey up as posts hold up a roof. They stood in ranks at the fog’s outermost edge, tall and dark-trunked and straight as a plumb-line, and their timber was harder than oak, lighter than iron, proof against rot.

For two generations the Trade Council sent out the axes. The rolls keep the count of it, cargo by cargo. They felled the pillars for the buttresses of fortresses and for bridge-beams. They put them into the three spans at Tidesmeet and the pilings of Fenholm, into the roof-trees of Holtward, into the cranes of Highreach that lifted the floating stones. And they put them into things that needed nothing of the kind. A lord of Eldhythe floored his dancing-hall with the black wood. The harbour-houses made their trade-tables of it, and their dice-boards. [Here the roll is stained, and three lines are lost.] A merchant of Tidesmeet had one pillar hewn into a single bed, and slept in it, and was proud.

The rolls keep a stranger count beside that one, and no one at the Council read it. Where a pillar was felled, the stump put up new growth, straight and black, and in a few summers a young pillar stood where the old one had, and the grey stood close about it again. The axemen went back for the young growth, for it was light and straight and made good poles, and the guild bought them for scaffold. They cut it, and it grew, and they cut it again, and it grew again, and they cut it again. Then there is a line in a clerk’s small hand at the foot of a cargo-list, that the stumps had stopped putting up growth, all along the edge. No one wrote down why, and no one asked.

Season by season the fog thinned, like a dying enchantment. The ship-masters came home and said the grey stood further off than it had in their fathers’ day. The Council named it fortune.

The Stonewrights warned them. It is in the rolls, and it is in our own hand: “These trees grow in strange soil. The carvings we find beside them bear a tongue we cannot read. Where wisdom is absent, let restraint be our guide.” But trade had become king, and the Stonewrights were reckoned relics, and the warning was read aloud once in the Council hall at Eldhythe and laid on a shelf.

For there were carvings. At the feet of the black pillars the axemen found stones set upright, and on them were cut letters no one could read, fine and curling, as beautiful as frost on a window. They were warnings. We know that now. The Mystaeri had cut them in their own letters, and here and there among the letters they had tried to cut our own words by their sound, as near as their letters would go; the sounds were close, and the symbols wrong. They had set them there trusting the stone to carry the meaning as their wood carries it. But stone carries nothing. Stone does not translate. The stones came to the cities beautiful and mute. Some were locked in the archives of the guild, and it was we who turned the key. Others were sold as curiosities and hung on the walls of harbour-houses, holy pleas reduced to ornament, and men drank beneath them and did their reckoning.

So the Stonewrights warned, and then filed the warning away with the stones they could not read. That is our share of it. We will not lay it all on the Council.

The harvest ended at last, not by wisdom but by the fickleness of men. The pillars near the coast grew scarce and the voyages grew long, and cheaper timber came down from the northern hills, and the Council lost its taste for the black wood. So the axes stopped. No one decided it.

Then came the forgetting. The forebears had not known what they destroyed. Their children would not know. The Shorelanders of the final days remembered nothing at all. The stones hung on the walls of the harbour-houses, and the children grew up beneath them and asked what they said, and were told they said nothing.

Late, very late, the wheel of pride turned. The temples of the havens were mended. Men climbed the steps of the guild-halls again and asked the Stonewrights’ counsel, and it was given. But humility came too late. The first dark sails were already on the sea.

I was at the outpost at the end of the land, where the rock runs out into the sea toward the grey. I was nineteen. There were twelve of us and the warden.

It was a poor post, but it did not look poor. The warden had set the black wood everywhere: in the doorposts and the lintels, in the walls of his hall, in his table and his chair and the frame of his bed. The stranger came in the morning, in a small grey boat, out of the grey. There was no wind, and the boat came anyway. He beached it on our shingle and walked up to the warden’s door. He was tall and pale, clad in woven mist-cloth that moved when he did not. I saw no weapon on him.

“Stop… Sky… Dying…”

They were the right words, spoken truly. I know it now. But words without sentences are only sounds, and we heard only sounds.

Then he knelt on the step of the warden’s door and set down before him a strange dark stone, the size of two fists. He set it down the way you would set down bread before a guest.

The warden kicked it aside. It went into the dust by the wall. And his men laughed. I laughed. I have not laughed at anything since.

The stranger rose. He put his right hand on the back of the warden’s neck, gently, the way my mother did when I had a fever, and drew their heads together until their foreheads touched, in silence. But there was one story the ship-masters would not tell, and she told it to me more than all the others, so I think she half believed it.

It came from the men of the outermost outpost, the one at the end of the land. They swore in their cups that they had once pushed a boat off the shingle burning, with a dead man in it, and that the tide took it out, and that when it was well out it turned. It turned into the wind and against the current, which no boat can do, and went away into the grey still burning, and screaming as it went. They swore to the scream above all. They said you could hear it after the fire was too far off to see.

The shore laughed at that, and my grandmother laughed with it, and knocked out her pipe on the grey stone over the fire. The ship-masters did not laugh. They would not speak of it, for it is ill luck anywhere near the sea, and I have seen a master set down his cup and go out into the rain rather than hear it told.

I have heard it since from three of them, at fires in the passes, as far from salt water as they ever came. Each had seen it himself, or sailed with a man who had, and their tellings were all the same: a small boat burning far out in the grey, where no fire should be, turning into the wind; and after it, weather. They say it brings the foul weather. They say it is the boat that comes for you when you die. The shore laughs, because the shore will never have to meet it. The ship-masters do not laugh. And the cities had it in writing that it was nothing: a stranger come armed, and put down, and his boat burned and set adrift. That was all the writing said, and who argues with writing?

But the children had a rhyme for it. I do not know who made it. We skipped to it on the quay with a rope, two turning and one jumping, and we thought the strange part was the greeting, for who ever heard of such a greeting as that:

Grey boat, grey boat, out of the grey, knelt at the warden’s door one day; a stone for a present, a cut for a kiss, and who ever heard of a greeting like this? Burn it and shove it and give it the tide; but the grey boat turned, and the grey boat cried.

There were two lines more. We did not sing those on the quay, for if a ship-master heard them he would take the rope out of our hands, and not say why:

Grey boat burning, and after, the rain; and the one it comes for will not come home again.

I skipped to that rhyme at seven. I understood it at forty, on the shingle under this wall, the first time I heard one of their hulls burn. For it screamed. It screamed as it went, the way the outpost men had sworn, and the old sailors on the wall turned their faces from it; and I stood in the smoke with my mouth open and the rhyme going round in my head, and I thought of the three grey stones over my grandmother’s fire, and the pipes knocking on them.

We forget. We have always been good at that. But Mystwood remembers.

The last count.

Into its heart, the Saenvael, they cut the orders, and the heart carved them on into every hull through the Aelralen, the bond of roots. We made that bond into a leash.

So the hulls obeyed the orders carved within them as a hand obeys the mind, for the wood of the hull and the wood of the command were one. The wood obeys the wood. In the night, while you lay your stone, the heart carves the morrow, and both of you are at work in the dark.

Yet the carving says what, and not whether. Each hull keeps its own grain and dread, and may balk at burning water, or turn savage where it bled: one mind, and many.

The bearers were chosen in grief, and the grief is in their grain. Your names for the grains will serve: the green wood, that had no temper yet; Ironbark, that comes again heavier where it failed; Willow, that turns from the first failure; Blackthorn, that goes where it lost the most; Heartoak, that never turns aside; Twistbough, that never plays one trick twice; Yew, that waits, and burns, and waits again; Ashwood, that throws all it has at once; and Silverbark, that knows you best, and bends least.

So the crudest came first, by the law of the years, which no council can carve against: a hull of one season holds one sign; of forty summers, a phrase; of a shore-man’s life, a sentence, and a memory in it. The eldest pillars hold a judgement.

The council wanted the shore silent soon, and young wood grows quickest: one season, angry hands, one sign to a hull. It lay at the edge of your sea while your children had children, and your boats passed it in the fog and saw nothing.

So the armada sailed generations before it arrived, with one sign in it, Burn, against havens with no walls for war. The last of that green wood followed you into the mountains; and there, at the Keep, for the first time, someone struck back.

Each growing after went out when it was ready, as a tide goes out, and we call them tides. Leathae was the green tide; Vaelthae had grain in it; Ralenthae the roots taught. Aelthae is ours. Rhenthae and Eirthae were growing behind us, and what is cut in them this heart does not know.

Every hull was grown for its shore: to break stone, to carry fire, to throw it over walls, to strike from under the swell; and for shores that are not sea, to crawl on roots over sand and rock, to swim burning rock in a skin of wet bark, to go white in the snow.

The warriors of the landings are grown of root, for we had none of the long-lived to spare. The keel is the root that fed the tree; the warriors are the rest of it, the fine roots that find their way in the dark. They ride in the deep hulls and go ashore a few at a time, the heavy slowly, the light pouring. They walk, dig, and carry fire. You call them the root-men.

They know only their carving: the keep, then the gun, then the wall, and a wall at its weakest stone, as a root goes to the crack. Where they crowd they knot, four to a knot, and a knot strikes as four. Some go only at the wall, and leave it chequered; some burst upon the stone; some come bark-first; some bind the others’ hurts with sap.

What they touch, the wood knows, for they are the wood’s hands. Cut them down, and what they touched is known still.

The broad hulls hang back, and in their crowns the winged seed ripens. When a crown-hull opens, the seed goes up on the wind, over your walls. The wind chooses the keep; the seed shuns the hot sky over your guns. If its keep is gone, it comes home, and goes again.

The seed is the hull’s own life: strike it all down, and the hull dies. Fell the hull while its seed is aloft, and the seed strikes, and falls, with no crown to come home to. Empty husks fly with it to take the shot meant for it; they have no fire, and the hull does not feel them fall.

Some seed grows so great it needs no crown. It rides the high air alone, hung from its own bladder, slow, and hard to bring down.

The growing took decades. Those who cried for war grew old, and died, and were forgotten, and the carvers were fewer at every growing, until only scattered souls were left, tending the last groves.

So it was a doom set in motion by the dead, crewed by the living, against a people who had never known they were the foe. We were sent. We are still sent. No voice among us can call us home.

The groves sang it over us as we grew, and it is in our rings:

The green wood holds one word. The wood of forty summers holds a word, and waits for its brother. The wood of a shore-man’s life holds a sentence, and a wall remembered in it. The wood of a long life holds the morrow, and carves it, and waits, and carves again. And the eldest of us, the pillars of the breath, the hearts of the grey, the anchors of the sky, hold one thing only now, and it is not a word, and it is not a sentence, and it is not a morrow, and we have carved it into every hull we sent you, and it is grief.

So we were grown, and so we were sent; and the grain holds it still.

And no one answered.

Part I · Three Roads, One Hour

It was in the Joined Tide. The plank came from the Standard-Bearer of a fleet that had come on three roads at one hour. Rhyna laid her hand on it on the shingle, and Halvard was on the wall above us at the mortar, with a course half laid. I was beside her with the lamp. I saw her go pale; and when I looked up, he had gone pale on the wall in the same breath, with the trowel still in his hand, for what the one knows the other knows. He came down to us, and they told it. It was longer than any carving we had known, and it was not one hull’s order. It was the order of the whole fleet at once, and the ties that ran between hull and hull.

Three roads, one hour. Let the east and the north go as one. Let the south stand at the edge of the dread until the reach of the south has walked east; then let the south go.

Rhyna began it, and Halvard gave it its end. For a long while after, neither of them spoke. Then I asked them what was wrong, though I think I knew. One of them said, and the other finished:

“It is how we work a wall.”

“The one waits for the other’s stroke, and does not need to be told.”

The carving held a name as well, the name the wood gives to that whole Tide. Halyna told the name: Aelthae. I wrote it down. I knew the first part of it. It is ael, the joining, and I had written it before in this Book, in Ael’thar, the Guest’s rite at the warden’s door.

I have written the name, and I will not write what I thought.

This we lay as it was laid for us.

And the hearth said: We remember.

Part II · What We Taught Them

In the Tide that Learned Deceit a plank came up from a hull that had come at us as the young wood came. It had sailed in a fleet that ran at the wall in the old way, each hull alone and loud and straight, and not one keeping station with another, as we had not seen since the first winter. The wall laughed, and every gun on it spoke, as every gun had spoken then. The hulls turned one breath short of our fire; and out of the grey behind them the hunters came, and went to every gun that had spoken, and would not leave it. Halyna took the plank, and knew it.

Come as the green wood came: loud, alone and straight. Stop one breath short of the fire, and let the hunters wait upon the flashing.

And beneath it lay the thing it had been cut against: they strike first and far; they move the guns we find. It was carved against us, against what we do.

I did not understand it at first. I said that the wood cannot lie. It is the first thing the Guest’s gift ever taught us of their carving: what is carved in their wood is always true. Then I understood, and I wrote it down before my nerve could fail. The wood does not lie. It was told to make us believe a lie, and it obeyed.

Halyna sat a long time on the shingle with their hands still on the grain. Then one said, and the other finished:

“They learned it from us.”

“We taught it them with every silent gun.”

It was true. Every gun the Captain held silent to draw a hull in; every false mast we raised on the wall with no gun beneath it; every gun we hid under sod and brush so that they sailed past it into its mouth. Every wreck from those days went back through the roots to the groves, and the groves learned. They had no word for a lie. We gave them one.

There was a name for us cut small in that carving, and Halyna would not say it at first. It was Rhenear: the stone-deaf.

This we lay as it was laid for us.

And the hearth said: We remember.

Part III · The Plan That Carved Its Own Defeat

In the Last Tide a Standard-Bearer came against us with a whole morrow carved into its heart, a plan with turnings in it. We sank it, and the tide brought the heart in, and it was the longest knowing Halyna had ever held; and the holding was not the hard part. They knew it in the time it takes to fill a lamp. It took them until the lamps had been filled twice to tell it. At the end of it was this:

When the bearer falls, the second takes the standard; when the second falls, go home.

They have carved their own defeat into the plan. They know us now. They know that we can break them, and they have said what they will do when we do. And Halyna said there was one hand in all that carving, and not a council’s, and that the hand was young.

But that was not what kept Halyna on the shingle after. It was the last two words. The young wood had known home only as the place a hurt thing runs to. This was a going home carved into the plan before the first shot was fired, a thing thought of in the groves and cut there on purpose. A mind that carves how it will go home is a mind that wants to go home. That night, for the first time, we saw that the enemy could hope.

One of them said, and the other finished:

“They carve a morrow now, as we do.”

“Then let us build a morrow they did not carve for.”

This we lay as it was laid for us.

And the hearth said: We remember.

Hear how the last knowing was done, the one that no one looked for.

The tenth Mystarch to fall was {LAST_THRONE_AT_HAVEN}. What was left of it lay where it had come to rest, as the nine before it had lain, black of grain and split open, and the soldiers stood off from it, for the Thrones were fearful even dead. The Captain sent word that Halyna should know its heart and learn its name, as they had known the hearts of the nine. So Halyna went in among its timbers at dusk, the two of them together, and Seren went behind them with the lamp.

They found the heart where the hearts always lie, deep in the old grain, grey and hard and still. And beside it, in a crack of the heartwood, there was a sliver of wood no longer than a hand. It was green.

Of all the things we had looked for in the long years of the siege, no one had looked for that. We had searched the wrecks for orders, and the hearts for names, and the grey for sails. No one had thought there was anything left in all that wood that was alive. But it was still green, and still aware. It had been growing in the dark of the dead Throne’s heart, out of sight, as a seedling grows in the crack of a wall.

They had not needed two hands on a wood since the first winter. They put out a hand each all the same, and touched it in the same moment, his fingers and hers, and knew it in the same moment. Seren saw their two faces change together, as one face changes.

It was not an order. It was not a wound. Every wood we had ever known had been the one or the other. The green wood holds one word, the song of the groves says; and so this did, and the word was home. The first wood we ever knew had said shore.

But it did not give the word alone. It gave them the answer of this hearth, the thing you have said at the end of every tale on every night of the war; and it did not stop where we stop.

We remember our home too.

And folded in it, as a seed is folded in a fruit, was a name that no carving of war had ever held: the name they give themselves, which is not Mystaeri, for that is our word for them. Naelear, they call themselves. Those of the breath.

Rhyna wept, and Halvard did not, for she was weeping for them both.

Then Halyna did a thing that no Stonewright had ever done. They built a boat. Our fathers were builders and not sailors, and so were we, and in all the annals of the guild there is no Stonewright’s boat.

But there are boatwrights among the Rivenmen, fisher-folk of the havens who built the small boats that go out on the morning tide and home by dark, and who carried their adzes up the mountain as the masons carried their chisels; and at Halyna’s asking they came down to the shingle. The eldest of them was of Tidesmeet, and he was Tarnel’s father.

They built it of the wreck-wood of the grey fleet, of planks we had known; for that wood was grown for the sea, and wood that knows the sea knows the way home, as the Aelvaren knew it. The fisher-folk laid the keel and bent the strakes and caulked the seams, and Halyna keyed every joint as stone is keyed, so that no sea could work it loose. It rode a whole day off the point, in a hard swell, and took no water. It was small, and it was sound.

They laid the green sliver in its bow on a bed of moss. Beside it they set a Stonwryt coin, part gold and part black stone, trust sealed in stone: the holiest gift the Shorelands ever had, as the dark wood of the Guest had been the holiest of theirs.

Then they knelt down together on the shingle before it. That is the first step of the Guest’s rite, the gift and the kneeling, and the only step one people can take alone. The rest needs the other.

They pushed it off, and the tide took it out. When it was well out it turned, into the wind and against the current, as the Aelvaren, the Bearer of the Binding, turned long ago, and went away into the grey; and this time it carried a gift and not a grave.

The old ship-masters among us stood on the shingle and watched it go. A grey boat going out against the wind is the thing they fear most on all the water, and they did not look away.

This we lay as it was laid for us.

And no one answered.

The hearth was silent a long while, and the fire burned down, and no one put wood on it. Then a child near the front, one of the fisher-children from the east keep, said it, not loudly: “We remember.” And then everyone said it.

Then, for the first time, the hearth sang in the wood’s way. The Rivenmen sing it still, and call it The Two Homes:

Our home was hewn from the mountain’s bone; we forgot it, and found it, and held it in blood. Their home was breathed from the hearts of the trees, and the axes came, and the breath grew thin, and they have not found it, and they cannot hold it. Stone keeps the vow, and the grain keeps the truth. We remember our home. They remember theirs too.

Written by Seren Two-Inks in the years after, on the leaf that faces the first tale of this Book. It stood empty until now, with the grain drawn on it and one line: Untold. Halyna cannot yet hold this deep. In the end no knowing of Halyna’s filled it.

I write this alone, and I write it plain, for there is no one now to know the wood for me.

Halyna are gone. They went in one night, the winter after the tenth Throne fell, as the Bonded go: Rhyna first, in her sleep, and Halvard before the lamp was out, for he knew. They lie under the new capstone of the inner gate, in one grave, with one stone, and one name on it.

I did not go with them. That is what an unbonded life is for, they told me once, and I did not believe them until the morning after. I am the first scribe of the guild to outlive the tellers, and so the Book goes on.

In the spring a small grey boat came in on the tide below the Keep and beached itself on the shingle, as the Aelvaren once beached before the council-hall of the Mystaeri. No one was in it, and no one had rowed it. The old ship-masters would not go down to it, for a grey boat that comes in on the tide is the boat that comes for you. It was the children who went down. In its bow lay a stone.

It was not stone. Or it was stone that had been wood: grey-black and heavy, with the grain still in it, like the Guest’s gift that lay so long in the chest I carried up the mountain. The Rivenmen have taken to calling it Myststone, and they say it remembers all. It is the one stone that speaks. Stone does not translate, and that is still true; but this was never only stone.

And it had been cut with a chisel. I know chisel-work. A hand had cut it that learned the chisel late and learned it well, young by their reckoning, I think, and seasoned by ours. It was cut in our own letters, so that anyone might read it, and I did not lay my palms on it. I read it with my eyes. It was cut so that one could read it alone.

In the memory of our home, our families...

The cutting stops there, in the middle of our Title, with the chisel’s last stroke still sharp at the end of the line. I have sat with it many nights since, and thought of the one who cut it. He never saw our torn cloak, I think, for he never sailed, and no one of theirs ever sailed in those hulls. But every hull that came against our wall saw it flying, and what the hulls saw went home through the roots to the groves, and he knew it there, as the wood knows a wall. He carried its words in him through all the years of the war, and learned our letters from them, for there was nothing else of ours to learn them from; and at the last he cut them in the one stone his people had that would carry a meaning. I have called him, to myself, the Last Carver; for I think that when he cut this there was no other. And I have thought that the small wrong word in it, the one word too many, is like the Guest’s three words at the warden’s door: the right thing, said truly, by one who did not yet know how we say it.

No one came with it, and perhaps that is wise. We have not yet earned a guest.

Grief is an inheritance, and it is paid out to the children. The sea still whispers on the shingle below the wall. The torn cloak still flies above the inner gate, and at night the wind comes over the high ridges where Rivenkeep still stands, and the old ones say it speaks the Title as it goes.

What we will send back, I do not know. I know only that it must be sent kneeling.

HOW THE LEGENDS ENTER THE GAME

For the integrator. This section is not part of the Book. Nothing in it touches the simulation: every surface named here is cosmetic and sits off the sim path. Where this section names a design document, it says “the fleet design (admiral doc)”; there is no admiral in the fiction (§9).

1 · The standing laws

  1. Cosmetic, off the sim path. No leaf, Whisper or message changes a number, a spawn or a behaviour. Every unlock is keyed to a campaign milestone or to an event the sim has already recorded, so two players who play exactly the same way open exactly the same leaves at exactly the same moments.
  2. Low-load moments only. Lore appears at first launch, in the debrief, in the interstitial between campaigns, and at battle load. It never appears during timed Build or a live Fight. Two fleet surfaces are not lore and follow the fleet design (admiral doc) instead: the between-sortie dispatch (§8 below), and, from the Tide that Learned Deceit, the root sign a sunk hull of the running carving floats for the rest of its sortie, a drawn glyph with no words (admiral doc §9b). The root sign is Seren’s eye-work: a root can be read by eye, and only the rest of a carving must be known.
  3. After, never before. A tale about a fleet behaviour unlocks only after the player has met that behaviour. The Knowing’s law (it explains this battle and never hints at the next) binds the tales too.
  4. No forced playstyle. Every unlock is reachable by any way of holding a battle. Sinking a fleet and routing it both yield wood. No leaf is earned only by one particular counter.
  5. Every EVENT unlock has a CAMPAIGN fallback, so no player misses a leaf.
  6. One new leaf per debrief or interstitial. Any others queue for the next. A ripening (a fragment filling in, or a caption added) shows as a small mark and does not count toward the one.
  7. The Book only adds. It never removes or changes a word the player has already read.

2 · The prologue (first launch)

  • What plays. The Invocation, then the climb-and-writing passage of I.1 (marked PROLOGUE EXCERPT, unchanged in v1.1.0). It runs 60 to 90 seconds of text on the torn-cloak art. It is skippable, it plays once, and it can always be read again in the Book.

4 · The Mystwood tellings, unlocked by knowing

The rule of the wood (N9, N13). Mystwood is not read. Touch it and its tale is known, whole, as a memory is known, and a knowing cannot be turned the way words can; the hard part is the telling. Only one of Halyna need touch the wood, for what one knows, the other knows. At first a shore-mind receives only the shape of a knowing, because the wood’s thoughts are a forest’s. Over the Tides Halyna learn to hold more of it and to tell it.

Every wood leaf is gated by how much Halyna can hold and tell, and that rises Tide by Tide. It is the Ender’s Game ordering turned on the tellers: the first wood is crude and the first tellers are clumsy, and both get better together. Halyna cannot hold the gift whole until the wreck-wood has taught them (Cam 18). They cannot tell the council until they can hold the ties between hulls (Cam 23 to 24). 6 · Battle messages

6.1 · The style rules

  1. When, and for how long. At battle load or briefing, or during the 3-2-1 transition, for three seconds. Never during timed Build or a live Fight.
  2. The draw. Each battlefield has a pool of 5 to 8 messages, and a replay shows a different one. The draw is cosmetic and never touches the battle’s seed. Because of rule 6, any draw gives every player the same information.
  3. Who speaks.
    • Wall-voices: Hale, Voss, Kael and the other True Men, by name (the Twelve, §9), wall-hands, file-leaders, masons.
    • Seren and Halyna speak rarely. A Halyna line is two halves, a voice each.
    • Two further voices: a Trade Council lord who fled to the Keep, whose old pride is turning, and old Idrenna, the Theolith pair who taught Halyna, who take the long view; their line also falls in two halves.
    • The Captain never speaks. There are no admirals, generals or modern ranks, because there was never a navy and the generals were slain; and no one is aboard the fleet.
  4. Length. At most 16 words, in one or two sentences.
  5. Register.
    • The plain speech of their world. Humour comes from character, never from anachronism.
    • No design vocabulary and no mechanic named (no AoR, no piece names, no campaign numbers). “Sortie” is allowed, in soldiers’ mouths only; so is “treelings”.
    • At most one oath in a pool, and faith is never the butt of a joke.
  6. Hint discipline. If one message in a pool hints at the battle’s carving, every message in that pool carries the same hint in a different voice. Otherwise none of them does. A hint may name a tell that fits the Tide. It never names the counter.
  7. Truth.
    • Never say what the fleet will do.
    • What the fleet remembers lasts one siege only. The wood remembers the wall, not the hand.
    • A Throne line may count groans.
    • Use fear-names only, until a true name has been told.
    • A treeling line goes only in the pool of a battlefield that lands root-men, and a seed line only where crown-hulls sail.
  8. Flavour.
    • The Tide sets the fleet’s behaviour: one-word mobs, then pairs, then looking, then three roads, then seams, then morrows, then groans.
    • The theater sets the texture: salt, river, fen, canopy, cairn, ember, sand, rime, crag-wind, crystal in the dark.

7 · Command Time in the fiction

  • Your ruling (2026-09-26, N4). A day has 24 hours, and an hour 60 minutes. At the pace of the siege a day passes in 18 real seconds, so an hour goes by in 0.75 s. The racing hour is meant: it is the urgency and the anxiety of the holding.
  • Under command. When the player starts an order in a Fight, time ramps down (it does not snap) until an hour takes 18 real seconds. That is 1/24 speed: the hour becomes as long as a day was, and the minutes tick every 0.3 s. When the order is committed or cancelled, time dwells a moment at that floor, so a second planned order can follow without racing the ramp, and then it ramps back up. Orders are stamped in sim ticks, so wall-clock speed never changes an outcome. The ramp and dwell numbers live in admiral doc §12.
  • Void. The v1.0.0 mapping, in which a day had fewer hours and one game-hour was one second and one sim tick, is void. Admiral doc §12 (v0.3.0) restates the tick-to-hour mapping under the 24-hour day: the sim tick stays one real second at the siege pace, which is 1⅓ game-hours, and no tick count changes. The Fight doc must still restate any grid-per-second figures. The Legends do not decide it.
  • The command, told as fiction (N5). The Captain commands, and his True Men know his command. Before the assault he gives each True Man his intent: where to watch, what shot to lay, when to hold (the Deploy order card). In the holding each of the Twelve fights his battery by his own judgment, because he knows the Captain’s mind; that is why each battery fights on its own (auto-fire running the player’s plan). The Captain can call to one battery at a time, by horn, with three calls every True Man knows, which are the game’s three Fight verbs (Cannons & Towers §6):
    1. Shift your ground: the water a battery watches, not the stones it stands on (re-task the zone).
    2. Change your shot: for the water, the shore or the sky (switch domain).
    3. Hold, or loose (fire control).

    The runners (Hale among them) carry word of what is seen, never commands. When the Captain lifts the horn to call a battery, the moment seems to stretch: that is Command Time. A battery is a group of guns, not one gun (admiral doc §12). Twelve True Men matches the late cap of twelve guns, so even if every gun stood alone as its own battery, none would lack a True Man; that is a fit, not a rule, and the Book states no number of guns.

  • How the Book names it. I.3, The Captain’s Long Breath, is the only place, and it is named as something men say, never as a rule. “A day has four-and-twenty hours in it.” In the holding “an hour goes by in a heartbeat, and a whole day in four-and-twenty of them” (a resting heartbeat is about 0.75 s). “For the Captain, an hour lasts as long as a day” is now exact. Hale counts ripples going down a sail, “and each of them, I think, was a minute”. His picture follows the ramp: the hour slows the way a cart slows on a long hill, not the way it stops against a stone, and it lets go the way a held breath goes out.
  • The law, told as character. Kael’s correction states determinism: “It is not the world that slows, lad. It is the Captain who will not hurry. The moment he chose was the moment that came.” The stamp is the tick, not the clock. “A second earlier or later” becomes a deliberate choice, never a reflex. Strategy, not skill.
  • The rules. The Book gives no numbers and names no mechanic. No text of any kind appears during a live Fight.
