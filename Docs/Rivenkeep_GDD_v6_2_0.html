Part I — Foundations

Game Overview

Rivenkeep is a single-player mobile fortress defense game that expands the classic Rampart arcade loop into a deep, long-playing experience. The player repeats a three-phase cycle — Build (place wall pieces to repair/construct fortifications), Deploy (place and configure cannons), and Fight (battle enemies in real-time tick-based combat) — across multiple sorties within each battle. After the final sortie, one Last Stand Build phase determines victory.

The game contains 190 pre-constructed battles across 10 theaters and 32 campaigns of escalating difficulty, plus a Boss Finale. The difficulty matrix grows naturally from 5 difficulty layers (A: terrain/theater, B: advanced enemies, C: hard pieces, D: environmental effects, E: castle pressure) — every combination explored across the 32 campaigns. Separately, 4 difficulty levels (Recruit, Commander, Veteran, Legend) modify each battle, creating 760 distinct experiences. All maps hand-designed, identical for all players. A War system resets everything for replay on harder maps. (Note: "layers" = the 5 difficulty dimensions baked into campaigns; "levels" = the 4 player-selectable difficulty settings. They are independent systems.)

Victory requires enclosing the required number of castles. The player builds fortifications with 198 unique wall pieces across 8 groups (A–H) and 16 tiers. One-finger touch on iOS (portrait or landscape), 2–8 min per battle. Destroying enemy ships is NOT required for victory — but the star system rewards ship destruction, cannon preservation, and territory control.

The Legend of Rivenkeep

"In memory of our home, our families, our God, our freedoms, our peace."

Thus is the ancient cry inscribed upon the banner that once fluttered above the last unbroken wall, and thus does it endure upon the title of every tale told of that place. It is the sorrowful heart of the myth, spoken by the wind that moves through the high ridges where Rivenkeep still stands — a fortress raised by hands that knew both stone and grief. Hear now the full telling of its making, as the old chroniclers would have set it down in runes upon enduring granite, that the deeds of builders and the wrath of the mist-folk might never be forgotten while the world endures.

I. The Shorelands and the Stonewrights

In the elder days, when the world was yet young and the sea spoke in low murmurs to the living rock, there arose along the eastern coasts a realm that men named the Shorelands. A land of mountains and high hills that tumbled down to meet the restless sea. Its people, the Shorelanders, were not wanderers of the waves nor bold venturers upon the deep; they were children of the earth, builders and carvers of stone. They paved vast roads like veins across the mountainous land, and from the bones of the earth they raised ten great harbour-cities about their natural havens: the eldest Coastal city, where the first walls had been laid; the River city, where waters met the tide; the Swamp settlement, the Forest keep, the Mountain stronghold, the Volcanic forge, the Desert citadel, the Frozen outpost, the Sky tower, and the Crystal spire that caught the sun like a blade of ice. Ten cities, ten harbours, ten kindreds who called themselves one nation beneath the same wide, unchanging sky.

At the very soul of that realm dwelt the Stonewrights — an ancient guild of master-builders and holy elders, keepers of the religion and guardians of absolute truth as it had been revealed since before mortal memory. Every fortress, every temple, every monument in Shoreland memory was their labour. Their creed was graven deeper than any chisel could cut: Build, do not destroy. Protect, do not attack. No Stonewright ever bore a weapon; mortar and chisel were their only arms. Women of the guild bore the title Elderess, and they stood equal in all things to their husbands in the craft.

Not all Shorelanders were Stonewrights, yet all Stonewrights were Shorelanders. Children were called to the guild in earliest infancy — drawn not only from Stonewright blood, though a majority were, but from any household in the realm. It was a great honour to be called, and no parent objected. Among the guild's most sacred practices was the Aetherbond: the ordained pairing of infants, husband and wife chosen at birth by the guild's seers. Their union was designed by God, with ordination tendrils that predated this mortal life. The Aetherbonded were raised together by both families but taught by the Theoliths — great husband-and-wife Stonewrights called to the teaching for a season. These unions were not mere marriage; they were Covenants of Completion. The paired Stonewrights lived, worked, and worshipped as one. Their movements wordless. Their coordination always in sync. Their work was not doubled but multiplied twelvefold. Every great wall and soaring temple was the work of such pairs.

No Aetherbond ever fell apart. The bond ran so deep they were one; such that the pain of one was felt by the other; the thought of one, the thought of the other. Separation, God did not permit. As such, one of the deepest mysteries of the guild was this: Aetherbonded Stonewrights died together. The longest separation was rumoured to be eight days, but most held that a fancy; in truth, hours separated them, and many drew their last breath in the same moment. Birth united them; death could not separate them.

The guild's most ancient symbol was the Stonwryt — a vow sealed in living rock, earned by toil and skill, more precious than any earthly metal. In the elder days a Stonwryt was carved into the foundation of every great work: a master's promise that the wall would hold. It could not be bought, only earned. In time, the Shorelanders named their very currency after it — the Stonwryt coin, fashioned partly of gold and partly of fine-hewn black stone. It was minted only by the finest craftsmen couples of the guild in workshops no outsider had ever entered. Thus was the economy of the Shorelands built upon the same foundation as its temples: trust, sealed in stone.

The Shorelanders feared the open sea. They traded along sheltered shores in modest ships, and some brave souls had tempted the deeper waters near the edge of a vast and everlasting fog-bank, but they turned back with wild stories no one ever quite believed. The sailors called it the Mistlands. The Stonewrights recorded its blackish veil in their oldest archives as wonderings, pondering, in the deep watches of the night, what hidden truths it guarded.

II. The Mystaeri and the Living Mist

That fog was no common mist. It was a living barrier — ancient, deliberate, and breathing — woven in forgotten ages by a people the Shorelanders had never dreamed existed. Beyond its shroud, across measureless flat forestlands that stretched to the world's far edge, dwelt the Mystaeri, a people older than the builders by many centuries, who lived twice and half again as long as any Shorelander, and who had raised the mist to cloak their realm from all the outer world.

The Mystaeri needed the encompassing mist as the Shorelanders needed the stone beneath their feet. They lived in deep kinship with the Mystwood — the vast and dreaming forests that breathed within the fog. The trees exhaled the mist; the mist sustained the trees and the folk who dwelt among them. It filtered the raw light of the sun into a gentler radiance the Mystaeri could endure, for though they were not creatures of darkness, their existence had long since woven itself to the twilight beneath the canopy. The unfiltered sun would wither them as it withers a deep-forest fern dragged into open desert. Without the mist the Mystwood sickened; without the Mystwood the mist could not renew itself; and without either the Mystaeri slowly perished — their newborns growing frailer, their eldest dwindling long before the fullness of their years. The mist was not a wall to bar strangers. It was the air they breathed, the water they drank, the soul of their world entire.

Through the veil the Mystaeri could gaze outward and mark every Shoreland hull that brushed the boundary. But the Shorelanders saw only grey emptiness — never knowing that each brush was a cut, every trespass counted as a wound.

To the Mystaeri, the Mystwood was more than timber. It was their tongue of truth. A covenant carved in living Mystwood would interpret the writer's intent and render meaning clear to any who touched it, bridging even the many dialects of the Mystaeri thunder-tongue so that no word could be twisted and no promise misheard. For anything sacred, anything binding, anything that must not be misunderstood, they trusted only the wood. Among the many kindreds of the Mystwood, there were trees of every purpose — twisted boughs for ceremony, pale saplings for song, silver-barked elders for council — but one kind stood apart: the Mystholders, tall and dark-trunked, growing in solemn ranks at the fog's outermost edge. These were the living pillars of the barrier itself, the anchors that bound the mist to mortal earth. Their timber was harder than oak, lighter than iron, proof against rot and the gnawing of years. It was these trees, and these alone, that the Shorelanders chanced upon and coveted.

III. The Pride of the Shorelands

Now the Shorelands stood in the high summer of their pride. Their cities were rich, their trade-routes heavy with wealth, and the counsel of the Stonewrights had come to seem a voice from humbler days. For two generations the Trade Council sent expeditions to fell the Mystholders for the buttresses of fortresses, bridge-beams, and all manner of decadence and wealth. Season by season the fog thinned — the ancient barrier unwinding like a dying enchantment. But the Trade Council named it fortune, never guessing that the felled trees were the very spine of the veil. Repeatedly, the Stonewright elders spoke in warning: "These trees grow in strange soil. The carvings we find beside them bear a tongue we cannot read. Where wisdom is absent, let restraint be our guide." But commerce had become king, and the Stonewrights were reckoned relics.

On the far side of the thinning fog, the Mystaeri watched their sky open and their world begin to bleed away. They carved warnings upon the stone — believing that stone, the builders' medium, would translate their words as Mystwood translated theirs. But stone has no intelligence. Stone does not translate. The warnings arrived beautiful and mute: some locked in Stonewright archives, others sold by merchants as curiosities, holy pleas reduced to ornament on the walls of harbour-houses.

When at last the harvesting ceased, it was not by wisdom but by the fickleness of men — the Mystholders grown scarce and the trades of commerce satisfied by cheaper substitutes. But the wound was mortal. The forebears had not known what they destroyed. Their children would not know. The Shorelanders of the final days remembered nothing at all. By the time the first dark sails appeared, the wheel of pride had turned. With trade faltering and harvests failing, the people had begun to humble themselves, the Stonewright counsel sought anew, temples mended. But humility came too late to prevent what pride had set in motion generations before.

IV. The Emissary and the Ael'thar

Among the Mystaeri, the Ael'thar was the holiest rite of fellowship — a binding of gift, blood, and kinship across any divide. Kneeling, the supplicant would gift a prized possession. Then rise, take the back of the other's neck gently with his right hand, draw their heads forward until foreheads touched in silence, then with his left hand open a shallow cut upon the other's right arm. He would release the neck, cut his own left arm, and the two would turn and press their bleeding arms together, shoulder to shoulder, mingling blood — a promise of united front, that harm to one was harm to both. It was a sacred ritual beyond all measure.

A single emissary sailed through the dying mist and stepped upon the Shoreland's shore. The outermost Shoreland outpost was chosen where an abundance of Mystwood had been set about doorways, decorated walls, and lavishly built into furniture — signs, to the Mystaeri's eyes, that the Shorelanders understood the wood's sacred nature. He did not know they were but the trappings of a rich man's vanity — trophies of commerce, not reverence.

The outpost was commanded by a young nobleman's son — arrogant, quick to anger, a leader of no men who loved him. He had been given his post not by merit but by his father's leverage in the Trade Council, and when his rash temper proved dangerous in civilized company, he was banished to the outermost edge of the realm where he could do no further damage. He ruled his small garrison with spite and bluster. He had never known battle. He had never known patience.

The emissary, tall and pale, clad in woven mist-cloth, spoke in the Mystaeri thunder-tongue — sharp syllables that rolled like storm-wind through ancient branches. He had learned fragments of the Shoreland language from listening across the thinning veil. He spoke words: "Stop… Sky… Dying…" — the right words, spoken truly, but words without sentences are only sounds. Without context they gave no meaning. The young commander heard only a stranger shouting foreign noise.

Beginning the Ael'thar, the emissary knelt and set before the commander a piece of petrified Mystwood — the most sacred of all Mystwood, wood that time itself had forgotten, so rare that even a sliver was the most treasured possession a Mystaeri could offer. Petrified Mystwood spoke truths in the old tongue — slowly, requiring long contact, but speaking the everlasting truth that could not be denied. The emissary hoped the commander would touch it and, in time, understand. But the young commander saw only a strange dark stone and kicked it aside. His henchmen chuckled under their breaths.

The emissary rose and tentatively reached for the commander's neck. The foreheads met. Then the blade touched the commander's arm — but the young man drew back in terror. He had never known battle, never felt a blade, never endured even a training wound. His sudden recoil made the cut deeper than intended — deep enough to bleed freely, deep enough to frighten a man who had never seen his own blood flow. Bleeding profusely, the young commander screamed in terror and called for his men to seize the stranger. Trembling with fear and shame, he ordered the killing blow. His henchmen obeyed.

What followed was born to hide his crime of ignorance and cowardice. The body was thrown upon the small Mystwood vessel and set ablaze. But the old wood raged in its grief, fire leaping higher than any natural flame — as though the wood itself was screaming. In terror, the garrison shoved the burning vessel away from shore. His report was a coward's lie: "Unknown intruder. Hostile intent. Neutralized. Vessel burned and released."

But Mystwood remembers. Even charred, even dying, it remembers. The Aelvaren — for so that small vessel was named, proud to be the Bearer of the Binding — turned against the current and sailed itself home. Through secret channels it journeyed until it beached before the Mystaeri council-hall. The Mystaeri saw their holiest peace — the one sent with the Ael'thar — answered with murder and flame. They did not know it was ignorance. They saw only an act of war.

V. The Fleet

Long had the Mystaeri watched their barrier dissolve and their warnings go unheard. Their children grew weaker with each thinning season, and the elders who counselled patience were themselves dying of the very wound for which they counselled patience — their thoughts clouding, their strength ebbing, their long lives shortening as the mist bled away. When the bones of the emissary washed ashore, something broke. The younger generation had grown beneath a vanishing sky and watched their parents fade. The elders pleaded: send another, learn their words. But the elder voices were too few and too faint. The young put the question to council, and the vote was carried by the weight of anger and grief.

The Mystaeri fleet was not built — it was grown from their sacred Mystwood. Battle orders were carved into the living wood. The wood shaped vessels: hulls curving from massive breathing trunks, keels from sustaining root-systems, masts from saplings trained by decades of windsong. Warships to shatter stone. Fire Ships to scorch the earth. Transports to pour warriors through every breach. Mortar Ships to rain death beyond ramparts. Torpedo Boats to pierce the thickest defences. And ten colossal Mystarchs — not mere vessels but the sovereign commanders of the entire armada, the final exalted expressions of the eldest Mystholders themselves. The last living pillars of the barrier, now grown into moving thrones of war — one for each Shoreland harbour-city. The ships obeyed the orders carved within them as a hand obeys the mind, for the wood of the hull and the wood of the command were one.

The growing took decades. During those decades the mist continued to thin and the Mystaeri continued to fade. By the time the fleet was ready, those who had cried for war were themselves old and passing — forgotten. The armada was built and sailed generations before it would arrive — carrying the rage of a dying people across a measureless fog. By the time it broke through the veil, few Mystaeri remained: only scattered souls tending the last groves. The war was a doom set in motion by the dead, crewed by the living, against a people who had never known they were the foe.

VI. The Fall and the Flight to Rivenkeep

The armada came without warning, for to the Shorelanders of that day there was no warning to be had. The cities fell swiftly, for the Shorelands were wholly unprepared. They had known only turmoil from within and relative peace from without — no foreign enemy had ever struck their shores. They had no standing navy, no war fleet, no defensive doctrine. Their walls were built for ceremony and weather, not for bombardment. At dawn the fleet fell upon the Coastal harbour. By sunset it was ash. No envoy the Shorelanders could understand — only the harsh thunder of a tongue born in ancient forests. City by city the ten harbours fell. The Coastal city endured three months. The Volcanic forge five. The Crystal spire, most impregnable of all, raised by seven generations of Stonewrights, collapsed in a single night when the Mystaeri ghost fleet unleashed the Leviathan Mystarch. The Stonewrights bore their chisels and their broken faith and watched the labour of their lives crumble. They did not fight. They simply walked.

The survivors fled into the mountains toward Rivenkeep — a name that was more myth than memory, the vaunted Keep of ancient stories, the castle that had never fallen. It had been abandoned for the lowlands generations ago, its halls empty, its purpose forgotten. But the old stories promised refuge, and so the remnant walked toward a promise.

But what they found was a shadow. What man could not destroy, time had withered. Gates broken, walls crumbled, halls dark with the dust of centuries. The Keep that had never fallen had simply been forgotten, and forgetting had done what no enemy ever could. The people gazed mournfully upon the ruin and their hope broke. Some shook. Some wept. Some fell and would not rise. For the first time, the Shorelanders began to fracture.

VII. The Captain and the Title of Liberty

He who would become the Commander was no general, no politician, no nobleman, no Stonewright. He was a captain of a small company — a leader of the few loyal men he called his True Men. They were soldiers who kept their oaths, who built what they promised, who fought only when the cause was just. The Captain had found them one by one over years of service, bound them not with rank but with trust, and they would follow him into any darkness. He was a humble man. Power was not in his nature. He had watched the Shorelanders' long retreat with the same helpless grief as all others and had waited for some greater leader to arise. But the generals were slain, the politicians forgotten, the nobles scattered, and the Stonewrights despaired. When the last weary column of refugees reached Rivenkeep's gates, the people began to fracture. Some cried for surrender. Some urged flight into the mountains. Some wished only to hide in Rivenkeep's ancient halls until the storm passed. The True Men looked to their Captain. The Captain looked at the wall.

He climbed it alone. He tore his own cloak and raised it upon a pike. Upon the cloth, in his own hand, he had written:

"In memory of our home, our families, our God, our freedoms, our peace."

His True Men were the first to shout their answer. Then the soldiers who had walked beside them in the long retreat. Then the refugees who had carried children on their backs through mountain passes. One by one, then by dozens, then by hundreds, the people of the Shorelands looked up at the torn cloak on the ancient wall and chose to unite — not by command, but because a humble captain had become the cynosure, and because in asking he had given voice to the last thing they still possessed: the will to stand.

But the banner was not for the soldiers alone. It called for the Stonewrights, too. They had seen ten cities die. They had carried their tools and their creed and their refusal to fight, and they had watched everything they had ever built turned to rubble and ash. Their pacifism had been the sacred soul of Shoreland civilization — the highest expression of their faith, the living proof that building was holier than destroying. Now the cities they had built were gone. The temples they had raised were dust. And the faith that had sustained them through twelve generations stood naked before a truth it had never been asked to face: that walls built for peace must sometimes be defended with blood.

The Captain's Title of Liberty was a challenge as much as a cry: Your walls are falling. Will you build, or will you watch and die?

Elderess Rhyna — it was said that her ancestor-grandfather and mother had laid the capstone of Rivenkeep's inner gate in the age before the harbour-cities — stood at the foot of the wall and gazed upward at the torn cloak. She bowed her head in reverence. Then she lifted her chisel high in the air and cried with a voice that rang from the ancient stones:

"The mortar cries out from the ground. Rise, Stonewrights! Lay stone upon stone. Stand every wall to the very end."

Her husband raised his own chisel beside hers and joined the cry: "Rise! Rise! Rise!" — and one by one the Stonewrights took up the shout, lifting their tools to the sky, their voices rising from the ancient courtyard until the walls of Rivenkeep itself seemed to tremble with the sound of a guild awakening from its long and pacifist despair.

While the Stonewrights would not take up arms — they never would — they would build. Beneath the rain of fire, death, and destruction, they would raise walls between volleys, mend breaches while the sky burned, shore up crumbling towers with the speed and precision that only Aetherbonded pairs could achieve. Where soldiers held the line, Stonewrights rebuilt the ramparts behind them. Not the sword, but the stone. Not the killing, but the keeping.

VIII. The Anointing

When the guild pledged itself to the defence, it was not their chisels alone that would save them. It was their faith. Among the records sealed in Rivenkeep's deepest vaults, Elderess Rhyna found what she had believed lost to time: the texts of the Rite of the Cornerstone — the most ancient ceremony of the guild, the rite by which the first Stonewrights had consecrated the leaders of Rivenkeep to build Rivenkeep itself. Within its texts lay the secrets of building for battle: fortification under siege, architecture meant to withstand not weather but war.

Elderess Rhyna and her husband together performed the ancient Rite of the Cornerstone for the Captain — the ceremony by which the Stonewrights consecrated a great work and its overseer. In the shadow of the Title of Liberty, before all assembled, they anointed the Captain with the sacred oils: oil of cedar for endurance, oil of granite-bloom for strength, and oil of myrrh for the burden of command. In his hand they laid the ceremonial staff of the guild — a rod of polished wood capped with the finest polished ashlar, borne by every overseer of a great Stonewright work since the founding. It named him Commander of Rivenkeep.

Yet the Captain remained humble. He did not carry the staff into battle. He did not wear the oils beyond that day. He led as he ever had — from the wall, beside his True Men, mortar upon his hands and a soldier's plain cloak upon his back. The people called him Commander. In his own heart he was still only the Captain.

IX. The Rivenmen

The Commander fought. The Stonewrights built. And from this union of sword and stone — forged in the fires of a siege that none had chosen and none could escape — there rose a people remade. They were no longer the Shorelanders of old, those proud builders of harbour-cities and paved roads, those children of a realm that had fallen to dust and forgetting. In the crucible of Rivenkeep they were reborn. They named their lands anew — Rivenkeep, all of it, from the high ridge to the farthest fallen harbour — and they vowed upon the Commander's banner to reclaim every stone of it. Some among the eldest still murmured the old name, the Shorelands, but it belonged to a gentler age, a time before the mist-ships and the burning. It was a name to return to, perhaps, when the last wall stood and the last Mystarch lay broken. But until that day, they called themselves the Rivenmen — the people of the riven stone, the keepers of the keep that would not fall.

But in the quiet hours, the Stonewrights opened the ancient archives and began at last to decipher the enemy's gentle Mystwood tongue — the quiet truths the Mystaeri had tried, and failed, to speak through dead stone. It was only after the final Mystarch fell that Rhyna found a sliver of living Mystwood — still green, still aware. When Rhyna touched it, the wood spoke clearly at last:

"We remember our home too."

X. Culmination of Myth

Thus ends the legend. There is no sequel, no easy healing. Only the weight of inherited sorrow, as it has since the world was young. The sea whispers on, the walls that yet stand, the Commander's torn cloak flying above the ancient stones. But somewhere, across the thinning fog, a seasoned young Mystaeri engraves the words of his youth, "In the memory of our home, our families..." into the Myststone that remembers all.

XI. The Whispers — A Truth That Unfolds

Progressive Revelation Across Campaigns

Every wall the player places is laid by Stonewright hands. Every cannon is positioned by the Commander's orders. Build, then Deploy, then Fight — the sacred union of Commander and Stonewrights, the last defiance of a people who learned too late that even peace must be guarded with stone and blood.

Through the long siege the truth came not in trumpet-blast but in whispers. As the Stonewrights learned to read the Mystwood tablets, the full history emerged — warnings, emissary, the harvesting, the pride. Twenty voices tell the story: soldiers (Private Hale, Corporal Voss, Sergeant Kael), the True Men, and the Stonewrights (Elderess Rhyna and her apprentice Seren).

Campaigns 1–6: "They picked the wrong fortress." "Twelve generations of Stonewright walls. Let them try."

Campaigns 7–14: "Rhyna says they tried to write in our tongue. The sounds were close, but the symbols wrong." "Stone does not speak as they believed."

Campaigns 15–22: "Rhyna held the petrified wood for an hour. She said she could hear a voice — faint, like water over stone." "The Ael'thar. Blood to blood. A frightened boy gave back fire."

Campaigns 23–31: "The fleet cannot be recalled. The wood obeys the wood." "Our forebears were proud. Their response was rage. Both are true. Build the wall."

Campaign 32 + Boss Finale: "We do not fight because we are right. We fight because our children are behind these walls." After the final Mystarch falls, a sliver of living Mystwood is found — still green, still aware. Rhyna touches it. The wood speaks clearly at last: "We remember our home too."

XIV. Battle Messages

Every Battle Starts With a Unique Message

Each of the 190 battlefields has a flavor theme tied to its theater, campaign stage, and narrative position. When the battle begins (during the first Build phase, below the phase bar), a message displays for 3 seconds. The message is different every time the player starts that battle — drawn from a pool of 5–8 messages per battlefield. All messages share the battlefield's flavor but offer different perspectives.

Example — Coastal Theater, Campaign 3 ("The Walls Will Hold"):

— "The salt air stings. The walls don't care."

— "Grandfather built this harbor wall. I'll be damned if I let it fall."

— "Two ships on the horizon. Standard formation. This will be easy."

— "The Commander says hold the coast. We hold the coast."

— "Fresh mortar, fresh stones. The wall stands renewed."

Example — Frozen Theater, Campaign 24 ("The Reckoning"):

— "Ice in the mortar. Ice in my bones. The wall still holds."

— "They sent the Leviathan against the Frozen pass. We sent it back."

— "Voss asked why we fight for ice and stone. I said it's not the ice I'm fighting for."

— "The wind carries their drums. Let it carry our cannons' answer."

Messages are stored in the battlefield data file. The game selects one at random each time the battle loads. On replay, a different message appears — keeping the experience fresh even on the 10th retry. The messages deepen the narrative without interrupting gameplay.

Game Theory & Player Psychology

This section documents the psychological principles underlying every design decision. A developer reading this should understand why each mechanic exists, not just what it does. Every mechanic must serve the core goal: make the game easy enough that anyone can pick it up, but challenging enough that players keep coming back — even when they fail.

Flow State (Csikszentmihalyi)

The game must keep players in the "flow channel" — the zone between boredom (too easy) and anxiety (too hard). Rivenkeep achieves this through dynamic challenge scaling: the 16 piece tiers, 5 difficulty layers, and their combinatorial progression create a smooth difficulty curve. The player is always slightly challenged but never overwhelmed, because they've already mastered each component individually before facing combinations.

Time pressure calibration is critical. Build phase timers are tight enough to create urgency but generous enough that a skilled player can finish with time to spare (banking bonus time for the Last Stand). This "just enough time" feeling is the hallmark of flow — the player is fully engaged because idle moments don't exist.

Clear goals with immediate feedback: Every action has instant visual feedback — pieces snap to grid, ghost preview shows landing, wall blocks glow on placement, cannon HP bars update in real-time. The player never wonders "did that work?"

Loss Aversion (Kahneman & Tversky)

Humans feel losses approximately 2× more intensely than equivalent gains. The design uses this deliberately:

The drop-back mechanic (lose a battle, drop back one) creates a sting that motivates retry. But the campaign floor prevents cascading loss. The sting motivates; the floor prevents rage-quit. This is the same principle as Dark Souls bonfires.

Cannon investment makes protection personal. A cannon at 140% (6 consecutive enclosed sorties) represents real investment. Losing it to a bomber hurts emotionally. Players will prioritize protecting veteran cannons, creating organic decision-making that doesn't need to be scripted.

Castle degradation (the 3-strike counter) creates escalating dread. Watching a castle tick from 1/3 to 2/3 makes the player feel the loss approaching and motivates urgent protective action.

Variable Ratio Reinforcement (Skinner)

The most addictive reinforcement schedule — rewards at unpredictable intervals. The dual piece selection system is the primary vector: two pieces, but what you get is random from the tier pool. Sometimes you get exactly what you need. Sometimes both pieces are terrible. The trash, storage, and spackle systems are "pressure valves" — they let the player manage bad luck without eliminating it entirely.

The ★★★! (4-star) exclamation mark is a rare achievement that can happen on any battle. The player doesn't know when it'll happen. When it does — dopamine spike. This is the slot machine principle applied to skill-based gameplay.

Frequency target: A skilled player should earn ★★★! roughly once every 15–20 battles. This is rare enough to feel genuinely hard-earned and surprising, but common enough that every player experiences it multiple times during a full playthrough. Server-tuned star thresholds should target this frequency. The exclamation mark should be a total surprise — the player should never be able to predict it mid-battle. Every player should have at least one ★★★! by Campaign 15 — early enough to hook them on chasing the next one.

Near-Miss Psychology

Near-misses are more motivating than clear failures. The design creates abundant near-miss scenarios: a pentomino that almost fits a gap but is one cell off ("if I'd rotated it..."), one wall block missing from enclosure at the end of Build ("I was so close..."), a ship at 3% HP rolling over to the next sortie ("one more tick of targeting..."). Each near-miss creates a learning loop that fuels the "just one more try" impulse.

The Zeigarnik Effect

Incomplete tasks create psychological tension that motivates completion. The earned time bank is visible throughout the battle, growing with good play. The player doesn't want to "waste" their banked time by losing. A battle with ★★☆ on the home screen is an incomplete task. The player wants to fill that third star. The campaign progress bar ("4/6 battles complete") calls to the player. Every incomplete element pulls them back.

Endowment Effect & Self-Determination Theory

People value things they've invested in. Walls carefully placed across multiple sorties feel "mine." Losing them is personal. A cannon at 130% is a companion, not just a number. This emotional investment makes the Build phase feel important, not just a chore between fights.

Self-Determination Theory identifies three needs: autonomy (meaningful choices every sortie), competence (visible skill improvement through stars and drift mastery), and relatedness (identical-for-all-players maps create shared experience).

Four Nested Engagement Loops

The "Just One More" Architecture

Micro loop (10–30 sec): Place one piece → see result → choose next. Immediate feedback, tiny decisions.

Phase loop (30–90 sec): Complete Build → Deploy → Fight. Three distinct activities prevent monotony.

Battle loop (2–5 min): Multiple sorties, earned time growing, ships accumulating. "One more sortie" becomes "let me finish this battle."

Campaign loop (20–60 min across sessions): Star collection, unlock milestones approaching. "I'm 3 stars from unlocking the Mortar." Long-term pull that spans days of play.

Design Philosophy

Introduce → Isolate → Combine

No battle ever asks the player to handle more than one new thing at a time. When a new mechanic appears, the terrain and enemies are familiar. When a new enemy appears, the terrain mechanic is off. Challenge comes from combining things the player already knows, never from confusion. The player should always understand why they lost.

Build First

The phase order is Build → Deploy → Fight. Always. The player builds/repairs walls FIRST, then places cannons, then battles. Every sortie begins with a puzzle (piece placement) and ends with action (combat). After the Fight phase, any wall damage carries into the next sortie's Build phase — what the enemy broke, you must fix.

Easy to Play, Hard to Master

Like Marble Master and Brick n' Balls, the core interaction must be dead-simple on a phone screen: grab a piece, drag to position, tap to rotate, release to place. One finger does everything. Complexity comes from the decisions, not the controls. A 6-year-old should be able to play Battle 1. A veteran gamer should be sweating at Battle 90.

Theaters Are Variety, Layers Are Difficulty

Switching theaters is about freshness — different scenery, sounds, and feel. It is NOT about being harder. Difficulty comes from the five named layers (A through E) that are mixed and combined independently of theater. A player going from Coastal to Mountain in Campaign 1 should feel change, not punishment.

Deterministic Puzzles Reward Replay

The game uses a three-layer map system: 10 base maps + 190 environmental overlays + 190 enemy overlays — all pre-constructed, identical for all players. No procedural generation. Because every element is consistent — same spawns, same terrain, same ship destinations — the player gets better every replay. They learn where the Sapper comes from, which wall section gets hit first, where to place the Long Cannon. This is a puzzle game. The puzzle has a solution. Retrying is not grinding — it is learning. Leaderboards are fair, strategy guides are possible. See Maps & Terrain for details.

Single Player Only

This is strictly a one-person game. No multiplayer, co-op, or PvP. The game's identity is the solitary puzzle-and-defend loop. Leaderboards and star comparisons provide indirect competition without requiring networking infrastructure or matchmaking.

Consistent Map Structure

Each theater has its own unique base map — a completely different battlefield with distinct castle positions, DMZ shape, and zone layout. The Coastal map plays nothing like the Mountain map. What is consistent: every base map has 10 castle positions and a DMZ. Spawn points are defined in the Enemy Overlay (2–4 per battle, varying by battlefield). The environmental overlay (rocks, trees, cliffs) is unique per battle and varies by theater style. This creates 10 distinct spatial puzzles that the player masters over the course of a War.

Build Is Hard

Wall isolation cleanup happens AFTER the player builds, not before. Fight debris (fragments, craters, burn zones) remains on the board during the Build phase. The player must work around existing damage. This matches the original Rampart arcade difficulty and makes the Build phase a genuine puzzle under pressure. Newly-placed wall blocks that are isolated (touching 0–1 other blocks) will also be cleaned up after the Build phase ends — the player must plan connections or lose their pieces.

Enclose the Same Castles

The game's mechanics naturally incentivize defending the same castles across sorties: castle recovery is gradual (1/3 per enclosed sortie), cannon investment is location-specific, and switching castles mid-battle means abandoning existing walls and cannons. Switching is a valid but costly strategic pivot — the game rewards commitment.

What Makes This Game Special

1. Deterministic Puzzle Identity

Rivenkeep is a puzzle game disguised as a war game. Every battle is deterministic — the same ships spawn in the same order from the same points with the same troops every time. There is no luck in the enemy's behavior. When the player loses, they lost because they didn't solve the puzzle, not because the dice rolled against them. This single design decision transforms frustration into motivation. The player knows that the solution exists and they just need to find it. Retry rates in deterministic puzzle games outperform random games by a wide margin, and this is why.

2. Three-Layer Map Engineering

The three-layer map system is the engine behind 190 unique battlefields without 190 maps worth of art. Ten base maps (one per theater) combine with 190 environmental overlays (terrain, wind, ice) and 190 enemy overlays (spawn order, troop composition, boss position) to produce 390 reusable assets per War. The same Coastal base map plays completely differently when Campaign 1's overlay gives the player generous cliffs and a handful of warships versus Campaign 28's overlay with dense rocks, high wind, and a fleet including artillery barges and siege ships. The layers are independent — terrain difficulty and enemy difficulty are separate knobs that the campaign system dials independently.

3. Castle Sacrifice Strategy

The castle sacrifice mechanic is where strategy lives. The player never has to enclose every visible castle (except in the Boss Finale). There is always at least one spare — a castle that can be abandoned if the walls are crumbling and the pieces aren't cooperating. This means the game constantly asks "which castles do you protect?" rather than "can you protect everything?" The former is a strategic question with interesting answers. The latter is a binary pass/fail that generates frustration. The progression from 1/4 (three spares, total freedom) to 9/10 (one spare, agonizing triage) creates a natural difficulty curve that doesn't require any new mechanics — just tighter margins on the same decision the player has been making since Campaign 1.

4. Time Rollover as Currency

The Time Rollover System turns efficiency into a tangible reward at every skill level. Unused Build time rolls to Deploy. Unused Deploy time rolls to Fight. Fifty percent of unused Fight time rolls to the next Build. The player can feel this working — finishing a Build phase five seconds early means five more seconds to place cannons, which means more time in Fight, which means time banked for the next sortie. Time is the universal currency, and every second the player saves through faster, smarter building pays dividends later. The earned time bank for the Last Stand is the culmination — 100% of the final Fight's remaining time rolls to the Last Stand, and the player watches their investment grow throughout the battle. The Zeigarnik Effect ensures they don't want to waste it by losing.

5. Anti-Streak Fairness

The Wall Rack's anti-streak algorithm is designed to feel random while being secretly fair. The guaranteed distribution rule (every group appears at least once per 50 draws) prevents the nightmare scenario where a player at Tier 16 draws nothing but H-pieces for an entire Build phase. The anti-streak rules (no same group twice in a row at higher tiers) ensure variety without removing the tension of "what will I get next?" The player will say "I always seem to get what I need eventually" — and they'll be right, because the algorithm ensures it.

6. Cannon Emotional Investment

Cannon emotional investment is where the game creates stories without writing dialogue. A cannon that has been enclosed with a castle for eight consecutive sorties sits at nearly 200% — maximum accuracy, double firepower, a veteran of every fight. That cannon carries forward to the next battle in the campaign, starting with all its accumulated power. When a Siege Ship breaches the wall and destroys that cannon, the player feels genuine loss — not just for this battle, but for the entire campaign investment. They'll rebuild the wall tighter next time. They'll prioritize that corner. They'll remember the cannon they lost at Battle 47 the way a chess player remembers a blundered queen. This emotional weight comes entirely from mechanical investment — no cutscenes, no voice acting, just numbers that the player watched grow over twenty minutes of careful play.

7. Three-Phase Cognitive Rotation

The three-phase cognitive rotation prevents the monotony that kills most mobile games. Build is a spatial puzzle — fitting oddly-shaped pieces into damaged walls under time pressure. Deploy is strategic planning — choosing which cannon types go where based on the enemy overlay. Fight is real-time action — directing fire, prioritizing targets, managing two threats at once. Three different brain modes cycle every 30–90 seconds. The player never does the same type of thinking for long enough to get bored, and each phase creates anticipation for the next.

8. Piece Nicknames as Social Language

Piece nicknames create the shared language that communities form around. When every player sees "The Crab" appear in their Wall Rack, they all groan the same groan. When someone posts "I got The Curse on my last draw — GG" on social media, every player who has faced Campaign 30 knows exactly what that means. The 51 starter nicknames seed this language. The player naming system ($1.99–$5.99 for temporary naming rights) turns it into a living, evolving vocabulary that generates social engagement and revenue simultaneously — without touching gameplay balance.

9. Victory Celebration as Viral Engine

The victory celebration is engineered to be shared. The fortress reveal (camera pulls back, golden light, particle effects), the star progress bar (filling left to right with fireworks at each milestone), and the ★★★! fly-in (the exclamation mark zooms from behind the camera with a flash and "Exceptional, Commander!") — this five-second sequence is designed to be screenshotted, screen-recorded, and posted. It is the primary organic viral growth mechanic. Every element — the visual spectacle, the personalized commander name, the star count — makes the player want to show someone what they accomplished.

10. Daily Micro-Battles for Retention

Daily micro-battles solve the retention problem without creating a second game. Seven challenge types (one per day, 60–90 seconds each) complement the main campaign rather than competing with it. Thursday's Sniper Duel teaches targeting skills that make the player better at Friday's campaign battles. Saturday's Puzzle Fortress is pure Build-phase practice. The cumulative streak rewards (modest bonuses that help but never create pay-to-win advantage) give the player a reason to open the app every day without punishing them for missing one.

11. Monetization Built on Trust

The monetization model is built on trust. "You can never buy victory" is not a marketing slogan — it is a structural guarantee. No purchase grants extra time, better pieces, stronger cannons, or any gameplay advantage. Leaderboards are fair whether the player spends nothing or a hundred dollars. The revenue streams (ad removal, cosmetic team colors, piece naming rights, avatar packs) generate income through self-expression and social engagement, not power. Players who discover this will say so in their reviews, and "fair, no pay-to-win" is the single most powerful phrase in mobile game marketing.

12. The War Reset

The War reset is nostalgia engineering. After completing all 32 campaigns and the Boss Finale, the player starts War II — everything resets to Campaign 1, Tier 1 pieces, no cannons, no storage. But the maps are completely new. The player brings mastery, not stats. Blowing through the first few campaigns with expert-level Build skills is a dopamine reward for the hundreds of battles that came before. Then the tighter parameters of War II (shorter timers, harder castle requirements, different enemy compositions) ensure the mid-game and late-game are genuinely challenging again. The appeal is universal: "start over, but better this time."

13. Boss Encounters as Theater Capstones

The ten unique bosses are the endgame payoff — not just bigger enemies, but mechanically distinct encounters that are each thematically inseparable from their theater. The Leviathan submerges and erupts near castles, sending tidal waves through walls. The Crystal Lich bounces beam attacks off the crystal formations the player has been building around for 32 campaigns. The Sandworm burrows underground and erupts directly beneath castles — the player must read the sand trail to predict which castle is about to take a hit. Each boss demands its own tactical approach layered on top of the normal fleet that is still spawning, still firing, still landing troops. These are 8–12 sortie marathon encounters — the longest battles in the game — where every system the player has mastered (wall building, cannon placement, time management, castle triage) is tested simultaneously against a threat that doesn't play by the same rules as anything else in the game. When a player finally drops the Colossus after ten sorties of focused fire, they have earned it in a way that no star rating can capture.

Part II — The Battlefield

Grid & Map System

Map Dimensions

All maps are 64×48 grids (64 wide × 48 tall, landscape orientation). 3,072 total grids. Each grid is 12pt × 11.25pt (iOS points — the system scales automatically to pixel density on all devices). The grid is intentionally wider than tall to correct for the horizontal-vertical optical illusion: a horizontal line appears longer than a vertical one of equal length, so making grids slightly wider than tall causes them to appear as perfect squares on screen. A 4×4 piece bounding box = 48pt × 45pt — precise enough for finger placement. Default view shows ~65% of the map. Pinch to zoom in (25% visible, grid detail) or out (100% visible, strategic overview). The 4:3 ratio is optimized for horizontal video sharing and matches landscape phone screens.

Points vs pixels: iOS points are resolution-independent. On a standard iPhone, 1pt = 3 physical pixels (3× Retina). On older devices, 1pt = 2px. The game renders in points; the system handles scaling. This means the grid is always the same physical size regardless of device pixel density.

Portrait and landscape mode: The game supports both orientations. In portrait mode, the player sees a vertically-cropped portion of the board and pans left/right to see the full width. In landscape mode, the board fills the screen more naturally. Pan and zoom are available in all phases. The game detects orientation and adjusts the UI layout accordingly.

Two Visual Modes — Map View and World View

Build & Deploy = The War Table. Fight = The Real World.

The Build and Deploy phases use a flat, color-coded "map view" — a deliberate aesthetic inspired by WWII command centre plotting tables, where officers moved markers across simplified maps to coordinate battles. In this view, all grids are rendered as clean, uniform tiles: open space is one colour family, trees another, rocks another. Castles, cannons, walls, ships, and spawn points all use standardised icons that look the same across all theaters. There are no theater-specific textures or environmental details in map view — the visual language is consistent and readable, optimised for spatial puzzle-solving. A Coastal battle and a Volcanic battle look nearly identical in Build/Deploy except for the arrangement of terrain.

The Fight phase transitions to the "world view" — the rich, theater-specific 2.5D isometric rendering with detailed textures, lighting, and environmental effects. This is where the Coastal harbor looks different from the Volcanic forge, where walls have stone texture and cannons have smoke trails. The dramatic crossfade from map view to world view (the 3-2-1-FIGHT transition) is the visual crescendo of every sortie.

Grid colour variation (map view): To prevent the flat map view from looking sterile, each open-space grid and each tree grid uses one of 3 subtly different colour variants, randomly assigned per grid at map generation. This creates a natural, organic texture — like a hand-painted military map — without adding visual noise. The 3 variants are close enough to read as "the same type" at a glance but varied enough to break up the grid pattern.

Three Layers, One Battlefield

Every battlefield the player sees is assembled from three design layers. At build time, these layers are collapsed into a single compressed data file — the battlefield — which is the game's primary asset per battle.

Layer 1 — Base Map (10 total, one per theater): The theater's permanent geography. Defines the 64×48 grid with castle positions (#1–#10), zone boundaries (friendly zone, waterways), and the theater's terrain identity (water for Coastal, lava for Volcanic, ice for Frozen). The base map is the spatial skeleton the player learns over many battles.

Layer 2 — Environmental Overlay (190 total, one per battle): The terrain puzzle placed on top of the base map. Defines the position and size of rocks, trees, and cliffs, plus fragile grids, wind direction, and the DMZ position (the DMZ can shift between battles on the same base map, creating different defensive challenges). This is a difficulty knob — early overlays are generous and later overlays are hostile.

Layer 3 — Enemy Overlay (190 total, one per battle): The combat puzzle. Defines spawn point locations, the number of sorties, which ships appear, spawn order, troop composition per Transport, and Mystarch starting position (Boss Finale only). Each ship uses deterministic AI to find its tactical position.

Future consideration: Rocks may be destructible in the Build phase (blown up using Stonwryts), but the cleared space cannot be used as open ground until the next Build phase. This is a future feature — not in the initial release.

Install bundle: All three layers are pre-merged into a single compressed battlefield file per battle. 190 battlefields per War.

Map Zones

Friendly Zone (60%→40%): Where the player builds walls and places cannons. All castle positions are here. The ratio starts at 60:40 for Coastal and compresses as theaters get harder — late theaters can reach 35:65 as the DMZ advances. Can be fragmented by terrain (River, Volcanic, Sky).

Enemy Zone / Waterways (~30%): Where ships spawn and travel. Enemy ships move on waterways — oceans, rivers, lakes, lava channels, frozen seas, or void depending on the theater. The waterways are visually distinct from the friendly zone (colored differently to clearly indicate no-build territory). Even theaters without literal water (Mountain, Forest) use theater-appropriate waterway coloring to signal that the area is out of bounds for building. Waterways can cut through friendly territory, fragmenting buildable land — the River theater's channels and the Volcanic theater's lava rivers are prime examples. Any grid designated as waterway is unbuildable regardless of its position on the map.

DMZ (~10%): A dashed black line between zones. Any grid the line touches is a DMZ grid (1-2 grids wide). No building on DMZ grids. See The DMZ for full rules.

Terrain in enemy territory: Rocks, cliffs, and trees can appear in the waterways too — same rules apply. The environmental overlay shapes enemy approach paths as well as player build space.

The DMZ

The DMZ (Demilitarized Zone) is the boundary between the friendly zone and the waterways. It defines where buildable land ends and where enemy ship territory begins. Everything about the DMZ is defined here.

Definition & Appearance

The DMZ is a dashed black line drawn on the map. The line is smooth (not grid-snapped). Any grid that the DMZ line touches is a DMZ grid. DMZ grids do NOT look visually different — they only turn red if the player attempts to place something on them (same red + X invalid-placement language used everywhere). The DMZ may be 1 or 2 grids wide depending on where the line falls relative to the grid.

Crossing Rules

Only Transports and Bosses cross the DMZ. All other ships remain in the waterways. Transports dock on the friendly-zone grids just beyond the DMZ line. Troops and Bosses can cross into the friendly zone. No walls, cannons, or spackle can be placed on DMZ grids or waterway grids.

DMZ Advance

In all theaters except Coastal, River, and Swamp, the DMZ advances 1 grid toward the friendly zone each sortie, applied after the Deploy phase ends (just before the Fight countdown). The enemy gains ground. The friendly zone shrinks. The visual reason differs per theater — lava (Volcanic), ice (Frozen), void (Sky), collapse (Crystal), troop pressure (Mountain), sand (Desert), forest growth (Forest) — but the mechanic is identical.

Static DMZ theaters: Coastal (Cam 1), River (Cam 3), Swamp (Cam 5). These three have a fixed DMZ that does not move throughout the battle.

Advance Shape

Straight-line DMZ: Each grid along the DMZ front moves 1 grid inward.

Irregular DMZ / islands: Each DMZ grid independently advances 1 grid toward the nearest friendly-zone grid. On islands (Sky), the DMZ surrounds the island — it closes in from all edges. The width of the DMZ never changes — the entire DMZ shifts as a unit.

Protection Zones

Structures on the DMZ front protect the space around them from the advance:

— Cannons: Protect 4×4 area around the cannon (2×2 footprint + 1 grid border).

— Castles: Protect 5×5 area around the castle (3×3 footprint + 1 grid border).

— Cliffs: Protect 4×4 area around the cliff formation.

— Walls: Each wall block protects a 3×3 area as long as the wall stands. On fragile ground, walls hold for 2 sorties before crumbling.

— Rocks, trees, open ground: Do NOT protect any space.

Fragile Grid Interaction

The advancing DMZ pushes adjacent fragile grids inward. Fragile ground can only be pushed onto open ground or under trees. The DMZ cannot push fragile ground under walls, castles, cliffs, or rocks — the fragile grid simply disappears. Fragile grids are always in the friendly zone.

Cannons on stable ground: If the DMZ pushes a fragile grid toward a cannon's 2×2 footprint, the cannon shores up the ground — the fragile grid cannot be pushed under the cannon and disappears from the battlefield. The cannon's foundation was laid on stable ground; the instability is rejected. This is permanent — even if the cannon is later moved, the fragile grid does not return. (Other fragile grids from future DMZ advances can still fill the space where the cannon was.)

Docked Transports

If a Transport was docked at the old DMZ line and the DMZ shifts, the Transport redocks at the new friendly-zone edge first before attempting to disembark troops again. It can seek a better landing spot during the redock.

Terrain

Three terrain types appear in both the friendly zone and waterways. Their positions are defined in the Environmental Overlay (Layer 2) and vary per battle.

Rocks

Nothing can be placed on a rock. Ever. No walls, no cannons, no spackle, no pieces of any kind. Rocks are permanent, indestructible, and completely impassable — for the player AND for enemies (troops and ground units cannot move through rocks; airborne bombers ignore them).

Three sizes: 1×1 (minor nuisance), 2×2 (significant barrier), 3×3 (major terrain feature). Early campaigns have few, small rocks. Later campaigns pile on more and larger rocks, shrinking buildable space. Rocks do NOT protect space from DMZ advance.

Trees

1×1 terrain element. Block enemy troops — troops cannot pass through trees unless all other paths are blocked (then ¼ speed in healthy trees, ½ speed in damaged trees). When a troop enters a tree, the tree turns red with a breach sound.

Cannon and ship shots hitting trees: Any shot (cannon or ship) that hits a tree with no enemy inside damages the tree (broken branches, splintered). The first shot on an occupied tree destroys the tree; the second shot hits the troop.

Wall pieces can overlap trees. A tree under a soft-locked piece shows a preview (dimmed tree). The tree is clear-cut at hard lock — permanently removed. If the piece is moved before hard lock, the tree remains. Only trees damaged by troops during Fight grow back between sorties. Clear-cut trees are gone for the rest of the battle.

Dense early campaigns, sparse later. Density varies by theater (Forest = extremely dense, Desert = almost none). Trees do NOT protect space from DMZ advance.

Cliffs

Nothing can be placed on a cliff. Like rocks, cliffs are unbuildable. However, cliffs act as free wall segments for enclosure — walls attach to cliffs side-by-side (4-way adjacency). A wall touching a cliff does NOT count as isolated in cleanup — cliffs count as walls for isolation checks. Two cliffs touching diagonally count as closed for enclosure — this is the only diagonal closure rule in the game. All other perimeter connections (cliff-to-wall, cliff-to-castle, cliff-to-spackle) must be side-by-side (4-way).

Troops CAN climb cliffs at ½ movement speed and are fully exposed while climbing (no cover, easy to target). Cliffs are a defensive barrier but not an absolute wall.

L-tromino (A4) shape, rotatable. Long, numerous ridges early → short, few late. Cliffs protect a 4×4 area from DMZ advance.

Fog of War

Fog of war applies in theaters with limited visibility: Forest, Mountain (clouds), Desert (sandstorm), Frozen (blizzard), Crystal (darkness).

Universal Rules

The friendly zone is always fully visible. The player can see all of their own land, walls, castles, cannons, and terrain at all times. Fog only affects the waterways.

Troops and bombers are always visible. Troops are on the player's land — the garrison can always see and hear them. Bombers are airborne troops — always visible even over enemy territory. Fog of war only hides ships in the waterways.

Ship Visibility

Hidden ships show as yellow "?" markers in the approximate area (±2 grids from actual position). Ships become visible when they:

— Fire (~3 seconds visible)

— Are hit by a cannon (~5 seconds visible)

— A shot lands near them within 3 grids (~4 seconds visible, 3×3 area exposed)

— Enter the outermost edge of visibility (faint shimmer)

Carriers are fully exposed when launching bombers (~6 seconds). In the Crystal theater, each shot bounce clears a 3×3 fog area.

The land: Extremely dense tree cover. The tree count here is the highest of any theater — trees are everywhere, creating natural walls that block troop movement. Clearings are small and precious. Rocks are moss-covered boulders.

Part III — Build Phase

Controls & Piece Interaction

One-Finger Design Principle

Every interaction in the game — piece placement, rotation, cannon targeting — is designed for one finger. The player never needs two hands or two fingers simultaneously, with one exception: during the Fight phase, the player can use up to two fingers to target two different areas simultaneously.

Drag Behavior — Smooth + Snap

When dragging a wall piece or cannon across the board: fast drag = smooth movement (piece follows the finger fluidly without snapping to the grid). Slow drag = grid snap (piece locks to the nearest grid position). The transition between modes is continuous, not a hard toggle — as the player slows their finger, the piece increasingly snaps to grid positions. This creates a natural feel: quick repositioning is smooth, precise placement is snappy.

4×4 Ghost Bounding Box

While any wall piece is being dragged, a ghosted 4×4 rectangle appears on the grid. This is always 4×4 regardless of the actual piece size — a monomino gets the same 4×4 ghost as a heptomino. The wall piece is centered within the 4×4 ghost using the rotation algorithm's centering formula.

Ghost visual: The ghost shows a light 4×4 grid — faint lines dividing the 16 cells. This helps the player see exactly where each block of the piece will land and makes rotation behavior intuitive (the piece changes cells within the visible grid). The ghost locks to the map grid — it snaps to grid-aligned positions even during smooth drag. The ghost disappears the moment the piece is dropped (finger lifted).

Ghost during soft-lock: When a piece is soft-locked (placed but adjustable), a faded ghost box remains visible around it — lighter and more transparent than the active drag ghost. This signals "you can still move this" and helps the player visualize the rotation frame when tapping to rotate a soft-locked piece.

Finger anchor position (game setting): When dragging a piece or cannon, the player's finger is positioned at a corner of the ghost — defaulting to lower-right for right-handed players. A settings toggle switches to lower-left for left-handed players. This keeps the player's finger out of the way of the piece being placed, since the ghost extends up and away from the finger.

Zoom & Magnify Halo

The player can pinch to zoom in and out on the map at any time. If the player is zoomed out far enough that placing pieces becomes difficult, and they are actively dragging a wall piece, a magnify halo appears around the piece: a circular zoomed-in view centered on the drag point showing the piece and surrounding grid at higher magnification. This lets the player see the big picture (zoomed out) while still placing pieces precisely.

Interaction Modes (Universal)

Both wall pieces and cannons share the same two interaction modes:

Drag mode: Touch the item in the tray, drag it onto the board, release to drop. The item follows the finger with smooth/snap behavior. Ghost preview shows where it will land.

Tap-select + tap-place mode: Tap the item in the tray to select it (it highlights). Then tap a position on the board to place it there. If the board tap is a hold (long press), the piece enters play at that position and the player can adjust before releasing. Once the finger lifts from the board tap, the piece is in play at that position.

Both modes result in the same state transitions. Tap-place is faster for experienced players; drag is more intuitive for beginners.

Rotation — Algorithm & Specification

Tap the piece to rotate it 90° clockwise. Each tap: 0° → 90° → 180° → 270° → 0°. Click haptic confirms. Works identically in the Wall Rack, storage, or on the board (while soft-locked).

Rotate Within the 4×4 Ghost — The Ghost Stays Put

All pieces rotate inside their 4×4 bounding box ghost. The ghost position on the board (or in the tray) does not move during rotation. Only the piece's shape changes within the ghost. The player positions the ghost; rotation changes the piece inside it.

Three-step algorithm on every tap:

Step 1 — Transform: Apply (row, col) → (col, −row) to every cell of the piece. This is a standard 90° clockwise rotation matrix.

Step 2 — Normalize: Shift all cells so the minimum row and minimum column are both 0. This removes negative coordinates produced by Step 1 and places the piece at the origin of its bounding box.

Step 3 — Center in 4×4: Calculate the piece's bounding box (height × width after normalization). Offset all cells by floor((4 − height) / 2) rows and floor((4 − width) / 2) columns. This centers the piece within the 4×4 ghost.

Why floor(): Even-dimension pieces (1×2, 2×4, 1×4) cannot be perfectly centered in a 4×4 grid — there's no exact midpoint. The floor() biases the piece toward the top-left by at most ½ grid. This shift is imperceptible on a phone screen and consistent across all rotations.

Maximum visual shift during rotation: ½ grid. Because the centering offset changes when the bounding box dimensions swap (e.g., a 2×3 piece rotating to 3×2), the piece may shift up to ½ grid within the ghost. At 12px per grid, this is 6 pixels — invisible under a fingertip.

🗑️ Cannon Trash (Far Left for RH / Far Right for LH)

This is the consequence of failing to protect a cannon — the loss is permanent for the battle. This makes cannon protection emotionally meaningful: a destroyed cannon is worse than a trashed one.

Arrows on either side let the player scroll the carousel in both directions — even if all cannons are already placed or locked. Tap a cannon type to select it, then tap the board to place. Or drag directly from the carousel to the board.

The cannon limit counter is visible above the carousel: "4/6 deployed." Each cannon type shows a stats tooltip on long-press.

Piece State Machine (Critical — Implementation Guide)

A piece transitions through states with strict rules about what actions are possible at each state. This section is the most important control specification for implementation.

State 1: In Tray (Wall Rack, Storage, or Spackle)

The piece sits in one of the tray zones. The player can tap to rotate it in place. They can begin dragging it toward the board. The piece is NOT in play until its bounding box center crosses outside the tray boundary AND the player lifts their finger. While the finger is still down, the player can drag the piece back and forth freely — only the finger-lift position determines whether the piece enters play or returns to its slot.

If the player lifts their finger with the piece's bounding box center outside the tray boundary, the piece enters play (transitions to State 2). If the center is inside the tray boundary on finger-lift, the piece returns to its slot with no consequence — nothing happened.

State 2: In Play (On the Board)

The piece has entered play. It is now on the board at the drop location. A new piece immediately fills the vacated selection slot if the piece came from the Wall Rack (or if a piece was trashed/stored from the Wall Rack or from the board).

While a piece is "in play" but not yet locked, the player can:

— Tap to rotate the piece on the board.

— Pick it up and drag it to a new position on the board.

— Drag it to the trash zone (if trashes remain).

— Drag it to a storage slot (if slots available).

The player CANNOT return it to the Wall Rack. The Wall Rack already has a new piece in the vacated slot. All other tray interactions are grayed out — the player cannot pick up a second piece from the Wall Rack, storage, or spackle until this piece is resolved (placed, trashed, or stored).

Invalid position: If the piece is dropped on an invalid location (overlapping walls, on rocks, outside grid, enemy territory, DMZ), each invalid wall block within the piece turns red with an X overlay, and the ghost outline turns red. The piece stays at that position but is NOT soft-locked — the player MUST drag it to a fully valid position (all blocks on valid ground), trash it, or store it before any other action. Partial placement is not allowed for wall pieces — every block in the piece must be on valid ground for the piece to soft-lock. (This is different from spackle, which places only the valid blocks.)

State 3: Soft Lock (Placed but Adjustable)

When the player releases the piece on a valid grid position, it is soft-locked. The piece appears placed on the board — bright white with no outline between blocks, still looks like a connected "piece." A faded ghost box (lighter than the active drag ghost) remains visible around the piece, signaling "still adjustable." The player can tap to rotate or pick it up and reposition.

Map edge behavior: A piece cannot be placed off the map. If the player drags toward the map edge, the piece stops at the edge — it will not cross the map boundary. However, the piece CAN be moved out of the viewport (the visible screen area) by dragging it while zoomed in. The map extends beyond the viewport; the piece stays on valid map grids.

Off-viewport frame: If a soft-locked piece, a first-sortie cannon, or a selected previous-sortie cannon during Deploy is scrolled off the visible viewport, a glowing frame appears in the lower-right corner of the screen (lower-left for left-handed mode). The frame shows the piece/cannon at ½ the size of items in the tray — small but recognizable.

Brightness animation: As the piece scrolls toward the viewport edge, the off-viewport frame fades in progressively — dim when the piece is mostly visible, fully bright when completely off-screen. The frame is selectable at any brightness level, even when barely visible. The brightness is purely an animation to draw the player's eye — it does not affect functionality.

The frame appears/disappears based on visibility: if 50% or more of the piece is off the viewport in either direction, the frame becomes active. If the piece returns to >50% visible, the frame fades out.

Frame interactions:

— Tap the frame: The ghost piece jumps to a position directly under the player's finger, perfectly placed on the map grid at the frame's screen location. This is an instant placement — the piece drops there on the board.

— Hold the frame: The ghost piece appears under the finger at the frame location and the player can drag it anywhere in the viewport before releasing. Same drag behavior as picking up a soft-locked piece.

This frame mechanic applies identically to first-sortie cannons during Deploy.

The piece becomes permanently hard-locked when ANY of these happen:

— The player pulls ANY piece out of ANY tray (Wall Rack, storage, or spackle) past the bounding-box-center exit threshold.

— The Build phase timer runs out.

If a piece is in an invalid position when the timer expires (or the player presses Ready), the piece is fully removed from the board — it does not hard-lock in an invalid state. The piece is lost.

— The player taps the "Ready" button to advance to the next phase.

On hard lock: the piece's group color tint fades and it merges into the contiguous wall. The wall should NOT look like individual blocks — it renders as one solid wall with seamless edges between adjacent blocks. Spackle blocks look visually distinct (patched texture) through all phases, even when hard-locked. The piece can no longer be moved, rotated, or interacted with.

No Auto-Scroll on Edge Drag

Dragging a wall piece to the edge of the visible board does NOT auto-scroll the viewport. The player must first navigate to the desired map location (by pinching/panning), then play the piece. Alternatively, they can drop the piece at the current location, then pan the map, then pick it up again (if still in soft lock). This prevents accidental viewport jumps during precise placement and keeps the player in control of the camera at all times.

Dragging Over Full Tray Zones

If a player drags a piece over a tray zone that cannot accept it (storage is full, trash counter is 0, or dragging a soft-locked piece over the Wall Rack which already has 2 pieces), the piece turns red while hovering over that zone and a "no" haptic vibrates continuously while the finger remains over the invalid zone. If the player drops the piece while red, it returns to the position where the drag started — either back to its board position (if it was soft-locked) or back to the tray slot it came from.

State 4: In Storage

If dragged to storage from the board, the piece occupies the specific slot the player dropped it into — no auto-reshuffling. The piece stays where the player placed it. The player can tap a stored piece to rotate it, or drag it out to return to State 2 (on the board). A stored piece dragged onto the board follows all State 2 rules — it can go to trash, be placed, or go back to storage, but cannot go to the Wall Rack.

Storage slot swapping: If the player drags a piece from one storage slot over another occupied storage slot, the two pieces swap positions. The dragged piece goes into the target slot, and the displaced piece moves to the source slot. This allows the player to sort their storage without playing pieces onto the board.

Build Phase — Detailed

The Build phase is the heart of the game. It's where the player repairs damage, extends fortifications, and manages their limited resources (pieces, spackle, trash, storage). The Build phase runs on a timer and has specific sub-sections that execute in order.

Build timer scaling: The base Build timer decreases linearly from 35 seconds (Campaign 1) to 22 seconds (Campaign 32). Formula: 35 − (campaign − 1) × 0.42, rounded. This timer can be extended by the Time Rollover System (unused Fight time from the previous sortie, at 50%) and Stonwryt Alter Time purchases. Boss battles use 25 seconds (fixed). See Phase Timing for full analysis.

Build Phase Sub-Sections (In Order)

1. Cleanup Wipe (Spackle + Wall Repair + Castle Recovery + Area Denial Cycle)

A right-to-left screen wipe plays across the board (the opposite direction of the Deploy→Fight transition, helping the player instantly recognize the phase change). During this single animation, four things happen simultaneously:

— Spackle removal: All spackle blocks disappear (temporary patches from the previous sortie).

— Wall repair: Any wall block below 25% HP is removed, all blocks at 25% HP or above are fully repaired to 100%. All wall HP bars disappear.

— Castle HP recovery: Each castle that was enclosed at the end of the previous Fight recovers 1/3 of its total HP. Castle halo segments update visually (green shimmer VFX on recovery, gold pulse on full recovery). Never-enclosed castles are unaffected. This recovery uses the same end-of-Fight snapshot as cannon Lock-In 1.

— Area denial cycling: Fire zones from Fire Ships, Siege Ships, and Bombers advance one step in their lifecycle: fire → holes → cleared. A grid that was on fire last Build becomes a hole (blocks building and movement). A grid that was a hole last Build is cleared to open ground. A grid dropped this sortie arrives as fire for next Build. This cycle is the only way area denial resolves — it is NOT instantly cleared by the wipe.

The board shows pristine white walls with no visible damage. The player starts each Build phase looking at clean, fully-repaired fortifications (minus destroyed blocks, removed spackle, and any area denial holes/fire that haven't cycled out yet).

2. Pre-Build Cannon Bonuses (Lock-In 1)

Before the player touches anything, surviving cannons receive their Pre-Build bonuses (rebuild + enclosure based on end-of-Fight state). HP bars update immediately. This is the reward for protecting cannons during the previous Fight. See Cannon Health & Performance for full bonus values.

During Build: As the player builds walls, any cannon that becomes enclosed shows a projected health bar increase in real-time — previewing the Post-Deploy bonus (Lock-In 2) that will be applied after Deploy. This preview updates dynamically as walls are placed or removed around cannons.

Post-Deploy preview: Enclosed with castle: +6%. Enclosed without castle: +4%. Survived exposed: +1%. (The move bonus of +5% to +0% depends on distance moved during Deploy, so it is not previewed during Build.)

The cannon's health bar should always show the latest projected value based on current enclosure state. As the player builds walls around a cannon, the preview bar grows. If they break the enclosure, it drops back.

Note: Destroyed cannon rebuilds happen later (step 5), after the player has had a chance to enclose them.

3. Player Builds (Timed)

This is the core gameplay. The player places wall pieces, spackle patches, and uses trash/storage. A countdown timer runs. Fight debris is still on the board — fragments, craters, burn zones from the previous Fight remain as obstacles. The player must work around this damage.

Selection refresh: At the beginning of each Build phase, the Wall Rack is filled with new pieces — different from whatever was in the Wall Rack at the end of the previous Build phase. The player always starts a Build phase with a fresh draw. This refresh also prevents hoarding — a player cannot hold a favorable piece in the Wall Rack across sorties. Use it, store it, or lose it.

Wall placement rules: Wall pieces do NOT need to be attached to other walls. A piece can be placed anywhere on the grid, even if it touches nothing. Isolated wall segments are valid placements. However, isolated blocks (touching 0–1 other blocks) will be cleaned up in step 4 — so the player should plan connections or accept the loss. Walls cannot be placed on Rocks, existing walls, cannons, or castles. Walls CAN be placed on spackle — the wall replaces the spackle. The spackle is not reclaimed; it is permanently consumed. This lets the player upgrade a temporary spackle patch to a permanent wall. Walls CAN be placed adjacent to Cliffs (which count as exterior wall for enclosure). Wall pieces can overlap tree grids — the tree is clear-cut at hard lock (not on placement). While soft-locked, the tree shows a preview of being replaced. If the piece is moved before hard lock, the tree remains.

4. Isolation Cleanup (After Player Builds)

This Happens AFTER the Player Builds — Even If Zero Pieces Were Placed

A text banner appears: "Cleaning up exterior wall fragments" with a visual wipe from right to left across the board (same direction as the Build phase cleanup wipe, reinforcing the Build phase visual identity). During the wipe, isolated blocks are removed in order.

All Build sub-steps (4, 5, 6) always execute regardless of whether the player placed any pieces. Even with zero pieces placed: isolation cleanup runs (removing any blocks made isolated by previous Fight damage), destroyed cannon rebuild runs, and the enclosure check fires. If the required castles are not enclosed after step 6, it is defeat — the player cannot skip building and survive.

After the player's build time ends, the game runs automatic cleanup. Any wall block touching 0 or 1 other wall blocks, castles, or cliffs on its sides (4-way adjacency only — diagonals do NOT count as touching) disappears. This clears dangling fragments from Fight damage AND any newly-placed isolated walls that the player didn't connect properly.

Single-pass, no cascade: The check runs once. If removing a block would cause its neighbor to become isolated, that neighbor is NOT removed in the same pass. Only blocks that are isolated at the moment of the check are cleared.

Interior wall collection: After isolation cleanup, the game checks for wall blocks completely inside an enclosed perimeter but not touching the perimeter wall, any castle, cannon, or terrain feature. These are "floating interior walls" — functionally the same as isolated blocks, just inside the perimeter. They pulse blue with a ω symbol (Stonwryt icon) and disappear with a collection animation, converting to Stonwryts at 4 blocks = 10 Stonwryts (rounded down). Floating interior walls and exterior isolated blocks follow the same rule — the only difference is location: interior (blue with ω, earns Stonwryts) vs. exterior (red with X, removed).

During the Build phase (step 3), cleanup preview for step 4 is shown in real-time: Exterior blocks that would be cleaned up pulse with a red outline and X overlay (will be removed). Interior enclosed blocks that would convert to Stonwryts pulse with a blue outline and ω overlay. This updates as the player places pieces, teaching the cleanup rules through gameplay.

5. Destroyed Cannon Rebuild

Cannons that were at 0% modifier AND are now enclosed within walls (after the cleanup) are restored to 50%. This happens after cleanup so the enclosure check uses the cleaned-up board state, not the mid-build state.

6. Enclosure Check

The game checks whether the required number of castles are enclosed within continuous perimeters formed by walls, cliffs, spackle, and castle edges. This check runs in real-time during Build (step 3) to show tiled-floor previews, and formally at the end of Build (step 6) and Deploy (Lock-In 2) to determine victory/defeat and cannon bonuses.

Enclosure Detection Algorithm — Implementation Spec

Perimeter elements (block the flood): wall blocks, cliff grids, spackle blocks, castle grids (the 3×3 footprint). These are the only things that form perimeters. Cannons are NOT perimeter elements — they do not block the flood fill and do not contribute to enclosure. A cannon sitting in a gap between two walls does not seal that gap. If a castle's 3×3 footprint is part of the perimeter wall, the castle is considered enclosed because it IS part of the enclosure — the castle simultaneously encloses and is enclosed. Troops hunting for castles use weighted A* pathfinding and can bypass an enclosure if an alternative path (e.g., climbing a cliff or going around) is calculated as faster than breaking through the wall.

Adjacency rules for perimeter connections:

— All perimeter pairs (wall↔wall, wall↔cliff, wall↔spackle, wall↔castle, spackle↔cliff, spackle↔castle, castle↔cliff, castle↔castle): 4-way side-to-side only. Two wall blocks touching diagonally do NOT form a closed perimeter — there is a gap between them.

— Exception — cliff↔cliff: Two cliff grids touching diagonally DO form a closed connection. Cliffs are large, irregular terrain features — their diagonal overlap seals the gap. This is the only diagonal closure in the game.

Map edges do NOT enclose. A wall touching the map boundary does not create an enclosed space. Only closed loops of perimeter elements form enclosures. The map edge is "outside" — it is never part of a perimeter.

Algorithm: Exterior Flood Fill

Step 1 — Define the search area. Only the friendly zone is checked. All grids on or beyond the DMZ line are excluded (marked as "outside" immediately). This eliminates roughly 40–60% of the 3,072-grid board.

Step 2 — Mark perimeter cells. Every grid containing a wall block, cliff, spackle block, or castle (the 3×3 footprint) is marked as BARRIER.

Step 3 — Seed the flood. Every friendly-zone grid on the map edge (row 0, row 47, col 0, col 63) that is NOT a BARRIER is added to the flood queue. Every friendly-zone grid adjacent to a DMZ grid (4-way) that is NOT a BARRIER is also added — the DMZ boundary is "outside" just like the map edge.

Step 4 — 4-way flood fill. Standard BFS from all seed grids. The flood spreads to each 4-way neighbor that is (a) in the friendly zone, (b) not a BARRIER, and (c) not already flooded. Cliff diagonal block: when the flood attempts to move from grid A to 4-way neighbor grid B, check whether a pair of diagonal cliffs blocks the passage. Specifically: if moving horizontally (same row, adjacent column), check whether the two grids diagonally "above" and "below" the passage are both cliffs — if so, the passage is sealed. Same logic for vertical movement. (In practice: moving from (r,c) to (r,c+1) is blocked if grid (r-1,c) and (r-1,c+1) are both cliffs, OR grid (r+1,c) and (r+1,c+1) are both cliffs.)

Step 5 — Classify grids. After the flood completes: every friendly-zone grid that is NOT flooded AND is NOT a BARRIER = ENCLOSED. Every flooded grid = OPEN. BARRIER grids are part of the perimeter, not enclosed space.

Step 6 — Check castles. A castle is enclosed if at least one of the 16 grids surrounding its 3×3 footprint (the ring of grids immediately outside the castle on all sides, including corners) is marked ENCLOSED. This means a castle touching two separate enclosures is still enclosed — both enclosures are valid. A castle with no ENCLOSED neighbors is exposed.

Performance: The board is 64×48 = 3,072 grids. After excluding enemy territory (~1,500 grids), the flood fill visits ~1,500 grids maximum — trivial for modern hardware. BFS is O(n) on grid count. The algorithm runs on every piece movement during Build (soft-lock, drag, rotation) to provide real-time tiled-floor feedback. At 3,072 grids, this is sub-millisecond on any iOS device.

Correctness over speed: This algorithm must be perfect. A false positive (claiming enclosure when the perimeter has a gap) causes the player to lose unexpectedly when the gap is exploited during Fight. A false negative (denying enclosure when the perimeter is closed) causes an unfair defeat. Both are game-breaking. Use the simplest correct implementation and test exhaustively.

Tiled interior (real-time during Build step 3): As the player builds, any space that becomes ENCLOSED immediately shows a tiled floor. The tiled floor only displays over open-space grids. Trees, rocks, cliffs, and other terrain features retain their original appearance — the tile does not overlay them. If a spackle-enclosed area (gray tiled) is then completed with permanent walls, the gray tiling upgrades to dark tiling in real-time. This gives instant feedback during Build.

During Fight: If an enclosure is breached (wall destroyed), the tiled floor reverts to normal ground instantly. The player sees the breach's impact immediately. The enclosure algorithm re-runs after each wall destruction event.

Spackle-enclosed = gray tiled. Permanently enclosed (no spackle in the perimeter) = dark tiled.

If the required castles are enclosed in the Last Stand Build: the battle is immediately won. In a normal sortie: play continues to Deploy. If NOT enclosed: defeat.

Post-Deploy cannon bonuses (Lock-In 2): After the Deploy phase ends, cannons receive their second round of bonuses based on enclosure status at that moment. This is when the +6%/+4%/+1% enclosure and +5% to +0% distance-based move bonuses lock in. See Cannon Health & Performance for details.

Combined with the Pre-Build bonuses (Lock-In 1), a cannon enclosed with a castle and unmoved for two consecutive sorties earns +8% (pre) + +6% (post enclosure) + +5% (post unmoved) = +19% per cycle toward the 200% cap. An aggressively protected cannon can reach 200% in approximately 6 sorties of perfect play.

Time Rollover System

Unused Time Cascades Forward

Time is the player's most valuable resource. Unused time rolls forward through phases within a sortie, and partially between sorties:

Build → Deploy: If the player ends Build early (Ready button), unused Build time rolls to the Deploy phase.

Deploy → Fight: If the player ends Deploy early, unused Deploy time rolls to the Fight phase.

Fight → next Build: If the player destroys all ships before the Fight timer expires, 50% of remaining Fight time rolls to the next sortie's Build phase.

Final Fight → Last Stand: On the final sortie, 100% of remaining Fight time rolls to the Last Stand Build phase (not 50%). This is the culmination of the earned time system — every second saved throughout the battle contributes. The player can also use Stonwryt Alter Time to extend the Last Stand by up to 30 seconds.

Time does NOT roll past battle boundaries. Each battle starts fresh.

Loss prevention: If ending a phase early would result in an immediate loss, the Ready button is disabled (grayed out).

Spackle Walls

Spackle walls are temporary wall patches that fill holes during the Build phase but disappear after the Fight phase. They are the player's emergency repair tool — plug a critical gap to survive this sortie, but rebuild permanently next time.

Spackle size: 2×2 in early campaigns (Cam 1–16). Upgrades to 3×3 in later campaigns (Cam 17+). No rotation — spackle is always placed in its default orientation.

Placement

Spackle has a ghost frame identical to wall pieces — always a 4×4 ghost regardless of spackle size (both 2×2 and 3×3 spackle use the same 4×4 frame). The drag anchor is the same as wall pieces (lower-right for RH, lower-left for LH). Cannons also use this same frame style, with the cannon centered in the middle of the frame. This is consistent drag language across all placeable items.

Spackle can be placed anywhere on the map, but only valid empty ground grids fill. Any part of the spackle where a wall could NOT be legitimately placed (rocks, existing walls, castles, cannons, enemy territory, off-map) shows as red with X overlay — same invalid-placement language as walls and cannons. Valid grids show normal spackle color. If ALL grids are invalid, the entire spackle is wasted. Once dropped on at least one valid position, spackle locks immediately — no soft-lock. Spackle CAN be placed on fragile grids — spackle is lighter than permanent walls (plaster, not stone). Since spackle disappears after the Fight phase anyway, fragile-grid HP damage per sortie is irrelevant.

Behavior

During Fight: Spackle blocks function identically to regular walls — HP, block enemies, take damage. Enemies can't tell the difference.

After Fight: All spackle disappears during the next Build phase (step 1).

Enclosure: Spackle DOES count for enclosure checks. A perimeter with spackle IS "enclosed." Spackle DOES count for cannon enclosure bonuses — it is treated as a regular wall for all enclosure calculations. Spackle-enclosed areas show a gray tiled floor (vs. dark tiled for permanent), indicating the enclosure is temporary and will be lost when spackle is wiped next Build. Wall pieces CAN be placed on top of spackle. The wall replaces the spackle — the spackle is not reclaimed. It is permanently consumed. This lets the player upgrade a temporary spackle patch to a permanent wall without removing the spackle first.

Isolation cleanup: Spackle blocks are treated exactly like wall blocks for isolation cleanup. A wall block touching spackle is NOT isolated (the spackle connection saves it). Spackle blocks themselves follow the same rule — an isolated spackle block (touching 0–1 other blocks) is removed during cleanup. Rule of thumb: spackle behaves identically to a wall block in every way except: (a) no cannon enclosure bonuses, (b) removed after the Fight phase.

Count

Spackle per battle = sorties in that battle. A 7-sortie battle gets 7 spackle. Counter: "🩹 7" → "🩹 6" → etc. Pulses at 1 remaining. Grays out at 0. Spackle does NOT roll over between battles. Each battle starts with its full allotment. Unused spackle from a previous battle is lost.

Pieces can have player-given nicknames that appear in the Wall Rack and catalog. Gold text below the piece ID indicates a nickname.

All wall pieces fit within a 4×4 grid maximum. This constraint keeps pieces manageable on mobile screens and ensures every shape is mentally rotatable. No conditional or fragile pieces — just shapes. All pieces are side-connected (every block touches another block's side, except the 1×1 monomino). Pieces are one-sided polyominoes — unique under rotation but NOT under reflection (no flip). Piece Selection Algorithm

Deterministic Fairness — Not Pure Random

The two selection slots are drawn independently but with fairness constraints. The algorithm does NOT consider what is in storage — storage is a protected player space.

Core rules:

1. No duplicates: The same piece (by ID, e.g., "C3") cannot appear in both Wall Rack slots simultaneously.

2. Guaranteed distribution: If a group has X% probability, then within every 50 piece draws (per slot independently), that group is guaranteed to appear at least once. Example: A-pieces at 2% → guaranteed at least 1 A-piece every 50 draws per slot. This prevents streaks where a group technically has 2% but doesn't show up for 200+ draws.

3. Anti-streak rules (scale with available groups):

— 1–4 groups available (Tiers 1–7): Pure percentage-weighted random draw. Pool is small enough that variety is natural.

— 5–6 groups available (Tiers 8–11): No 4 consecutive draws from the same group in the same slot. Three E-pieces in a row is okay; four is blocked. With more groups available, the constraint relaxes to allow natural clustering while still preventing extreme streaks.

— 7–8 groups available (Tiers 12–16): No 3 consecutive draws from the same group in the same slot. Two E-pieces in a row is okay; three is blocked.

4. Within-group selection: Once a group is selected, the specific piece from that group is chosen uniformly at random. All pieces within a group have equal probability.

Implementation: Each slot maintains a short history (last 1–2 groups drawn). The draw function: roll against the percentage table → check anti-streak → if blocked, re-roll (up to 3 attempts) → if still blocked, pick the least-recently-drawn group. The guaranteed-distribution counter resets per battle.

Part IV — Deploy Phase

Deploy Phase — Detailed

The Deploy phase configures offensive firepower. The bottom panel shows: on the left side, the number of cannons available to deploy (e.g., "3 to deploy"). The horizontal carousel displays all unlocked cannon types with unique sprites in light blue. Not-yet-unlocked types appear grayed out with a lock symbol.

Selection: When a cannon is selected (tapped in the carousel or on the board), it shows the universal selection frame — same visual language as wall pieces. First-sortie cannons have a bright frame with swap icon; previous-sortie cannons have a dimmer frame with no icon.

Deploy timer: The Deploy phase timer scales from 12 seconds (Campaign 1) to 15 seconds (Campaign 32), increasing with cannon count. Formula: Math.round(12 + (campaign − 1) × 3/31), rounded. Deploy time INCREASES as campaigns progress because the player has more cannons to manage — more new placements, more repositioning decisions, and potentially type changes. The 12-second minimum ensures even early campaigns have enough time for board recognition (~5 sec) plus thoughtful placement. Unused Deploy time rolls forward to the Fight phase. Boss battles use 25 seconds (fixed). The wind compass is NOT shown during Deploy (wind does not affect cannon placement).

Ready button: The Ready button is available during Deploy, identical to Build. Tapping it ends Deploy immediately — any unused Deploy time rolls forward to the Fight phase via the Time Rollover System. The player is not required to place any cannons. If all cannons are trashed or none placed, the Fight proceeds with no offensive firepower (no shots, no bonuses). This is a valid but desperate strategic choice.

Cannon persistence: Cannons placed in a previous sortie remain in their positions for the next sortie's Deploy phase. The player does not need to re-place them. They can move, swap, or trash cannons during Deploy, but the default state is "everything stays where it was." New cannon slots (earned from Fight performance) appear empty in the carousel for placement. This makes Deploy progressively faster — the player only needs to place new cannons and optionally reposition existing ones.

Locked cannon preview: Long-pressing a locked cannon in the Deploy carousel shows a preview tooltip: cannon name, stats (damage, range, fire rate, accuracy), special ability description, and the unlock threshold ("Unlock at 50★ — you have 42★"). This creates anticipation ("I'm 8 stars from the Mortar!") and informs strategy before the unlock.

Cannon Slots & Earning

Each battle defines a starting cannon count and a maximum cannon count. Starting cannons are derived from the campaign number. Maximum cannons factor in the campaign, sortie count, and fleet size (a battle with more ships needs more firepower):

Starting cannons: min(2 + floor(campaign / 6), 7) — scales from 2 (Cam 1) to 7 (Cam 32). Boss battles: 6 (fixed).

Maximum cannons: min(startCannons + sorties + floor(totalShips / 5), 12) — factors in how many sorties the player has to earn slots AND how many ships they face. More ships = higher cap = more firepower available. Boss battles: 12 (fixed).

Unplaced cannon slots roll over to the next sortie. Cannon slots do NOT carry over between battles — each battle starts fresh.

Cannon Placement Rules

Cannons occupy a 2×2 grid footprint. All 4 grids of the 2×2 must be open ground — no rocks, walls, castles, other cannons, DMZ grids, ground damage, or terrain features. Cannons CAN be placed inside or outside enclosures. They CAN be placed adjacent to cliffs. They CANNOT be placed on any fragile grid (ice, crust, crag, bog, fissure, or fract) — the ground is too unstable for cannon weight and recoil.

Cannon type deployment limits: Each cannon type has a per-battle deployment limit — the maximum number of that type the player can place. This simulates production capacity: the Stonwryts haven't optimized mass production of advanced cannon types yet.

Early campaigns (1–6): No type limits. The Deploy carousel shows an ∞ symbol next to each cannon type. The player can fill all slots with Standard Cannons if they want.

Campaign 7+: Type limits appear. A countdown counter in the upper-right corner of each cannon card in the carousel shows how many of that type remain (e.g., "3" = 3 more available). The counter grays out at 0. Limits start generous (e.g., max 5 of any type) and tighten as campaigns progress. When a new cannon type is first introduced (star milestone unlock), it starts at limit 1–2 — limited production. As the player earns more stars, the limit increases. This creates a natural diversity incentive: the player CAN'T fill all slots with Lightning cannons because only 3 are available. They MUST use a mix.

Formula: Base limit per type = max(2, floor(totalStars / unlock_threshold_for_that_type)), capped at 5. Example: Lightning unlocks at 75★. At 75★, limit = floor(75/75) = 1 (just unlocked). At 150★, limit = 2. At 375★, limit = 5 (max). Standard Cannon (always available): limit = ∞. This means early-unlocking cannons reach max limit faster than late-unlocking ones.

First-Sortie Cannons (Just Placed This Sortie)

First-sortie cannons appear brighter than established cannons — a vivid light blue glow that distinguishes them. When selected, the bright selection frame with swap icon appears. The player has full flexibility: place, move, drag on and off the board freely (off-board = dropped anywhere in the tray area), and change its type by tapping a different type in the carousel while the cannon is selected. Only cannons with the bright glow can have their type changed in-place.

Once the Deploy phase ends, cannons are locked in place for this sortie's Fight. However, cannons CAN be moved in subsequent Deploy phases — the player can reposition any surviving cannon. Moving a cannon incurs a distance-based penalty to the move bonus (see Cannon Health & Performance): keeping a cannon in place earns +5%, while moving it reduces or eliminates that bonus based on distance. While dragging a cannon from its previous position, the game displays a light frame outline at the cannon's original position and a dashed line from the current drag position to the origin. This visual indicator disappears when the player releases the cannon.

Previous-Sortie Cannons

Cannons placed in a previous sortie can be moved to any open ground on the board during Deploy — no movement limit or range restriction. To change type, the player trashes the cannon and places a new type next sortie — the slot is consumed this sortie, so the cannon misses this Fight. The player CAN voluntarily trash a previous-sortie cannon by selecting it and tapping the cannon trash bin. Trashed cannons are visible inside the trash with their HP bars; the player can recover them (same type) before Deploy ends.

Cannons on invalid positions at the start of Deploy (e.g., ground destroyed beneath them) show red blocks with X on the invalid grids. They are NOT automatically moved anywhere — the player can move them to valid ground. If still on invalid ground when Deploy ends, they are destroyed (not trashed — gone permanently, slot freed next sortie).

No cannon requirement: The player is not required to have any cannons for the Fight phase. If all cannons are trashed or none are placed, the Fight proceeds — the player simply has no offensive firepower (no shots, no bonuses). This is a valid (if desperate) strategic choice.

Why allow voluntary trashing: A previous-sortie cannon at <25% HP from the last Fight was rebuilt to at least 50% in the Pre-Build lock-in. If it survives the next Fight above 25%, it's back to at least 100%. The math naturally discourages trashing healthy cannons — the rebuild system rewards keeping them. But giving the player the choice respects their agency.

Cannons with performance bonuses carry forward to the next battle within the same campaign. At the start of a new battle, carried cannons appear in the cannon carousel in their normal type slots. Part V — Fight Phase

Fight Phase — Real-Time Tick Combat

Countdown & Fight Timer

Every Fight phase begins with a 3… 2… 1… FIGHT! countdown (2.5–3.0 seconds). The countdown IS the Map View → World View transition — they are the same animation, not separate events. On "3": the flat 2D map dims, warm lamplight glow at edges. On "2": camera pans up from the map to a window revealing dark sky. On "1": fly out the window into darkness. On "FIGHT": crossfade into the 2.5D isometric battlefield with the first cannon firing. The player watches their flat planning map transform into the living battlefield in one continuous sequence. The Fight phase has a countdown timer scaling from 25 seconds (Campaign 1) to 90 seconds (Campaign 32). Boss battles use 90 seconds (fixed). See Phase Timing for full analysis. If all enemy ships for that sortie are destroyed before the timer expires, the Fight phase ends immediately and unused fight time carries over (50% to the next sortie's Build phase). If the timer expires with ships still alive, those ships remain at their current position and HP for the next sortie's Fight phase (new ships may also spawn from the spawn points).

Tick-Based Combat System

The Fight phase runs on a real-time tick clock, not discrete rounds. The clock ticks at ~2 ticks/sec (500ms). Enemy ships and troops move, pause, and fire on their own schedules. Cannons fire based on reload speed (ticks). The player directs fire via touch.

Deterministic Battles — This Is a Puzzle Game

Every Battle Is Identical Every Time

The order of ships coming out of each spawn point is fixed. Which ships come from which spawn point is fixed. The troops aboard each Transport are fixed. This is not a skills game — it is a puzzle game. The player can study a battle, learn the spawn order, and develop a precise strategy. On replay, the same ships appear in the same order from the same spawn points with the same troops. The "surprise" of troop types only exists the first time the player encounters a sortie.

Ship Spawn Points

Defined Per Battlefield in the Enemy Overlay

Spawn point locations are defined in the Enemy Overlay (Layer 3), not the base map. They vary from battlefield to battlefield within the same theater. The Enemy Overlay also determines the number of ships, their spawn order, and which ships appear in which sortie. Each ship type uses deterministic AI to find its best tactical position.

Earlier battlefields: Spawn points are at the far edge of the waterways. Ships have a long approach, giving the player time to prepare.

Later battlefields: Spawn points can be placed closer to the DMZ. Ships spawning closer to the friendly zone are harder to detect and respond to — especially in fog of war theaters. The closer the spawn point, the more "stealthy" the approach feels, even without a stealth mechanic.

Only one ship spawns per point at a time; ships queue. Spawn speed scales with Layer B.

Spawn grid: Each spawn point is a 1×4 grid centered within a 7×6 staging area. Ships exit from the long side (4-grid edge) in either direction, but must exit into a valid enemy ship location. If the 1×4 is at the edge of the battlefield, ships can only exit in one direction. Once spawned, ships move at fast speed until completely out of the 7×6 staging box, then resume normal speed. Ships cannot fire while in the staging area, but they can be hit by cannon fire.

The DMZ in Combat

The DMZ separates friendly zone and waterways. Only Transports and Bosses cross it. The DMZ advances 1 grid per sortie after Deploy ends (except Coastal, River, Swamp). See Part II — The DMZ for complete rules on advance mechanics, protection zones, and fragile grid interaction.

Player Targeting — How the Player Directs Cannons

Touch-to-Target with Auto-Fire Fallback

Cannons have three firing modes during the Fight phase:

Auto-fire (default — no touch): When the player is NOT touching the map, all cannons independently auto-target the nearest enemy in range. Each cannon picks its own target based on proximity. Auto-fire has a -15% accuracy penalty and fires slightly slower than directed modes (the cannon "decides" where to aim — processing time). Auto-fire ensures defenses are never idle.

Hold-fire (touch and hold): The player touches and holds a point on the map. All cannons in range concentrate fire on that location. A faint accuracy circle appears around the player's finger — this circle represents the spread area where shots will land based on the combined accuracy of all firing cannons. Close targets = tighter circle. Farther targets = wider circle. The player can drag their finger to track a moving ship. Full accuracy (no penalty).

Touch-lock (tap on a ship): A quick tap on an enemy ship locks onto that ship for 5 seconds. A bright crosshair appears on the target and all cannons in range fire at it. The crosshair fades from bright to nothing over 5 seconds. The player can tap a second ship to lock a second target simultaneously. Third tap removes the first lock (cycling). Up to 2 simultaneous locks.

While touching (Hold or Touch-lock), faint range circles appear around ALL cannons, with an arc (or line) from each cannon to its range circle.

Shot trajectory: The cannonball takes time to travel. Higher trajectory = longer distance but slower travel time. Lower trajectory = shorter distance but faster arrival. More powerful cannons (Long, Doom) fire lower trajectories and hit sooner. Less powerful cannons (Standard, Scatter) use higher arcs and take longer. Cannons never fall short — their "range" stat is arrival speed, not reach. All cannons within range will hit; the question is when.

Two-finger targeting: Two fingers simultaneously create two Hold-fire target points. Cannons split between the two points based on range.

Hitting bombers: All cannons can hit bombers. Cannon fire aimed at bombers goes "only up" on the arc — steep vertical shots. The altitude means shots take longer to arrive (half normal arrival speed) but every cannon can reach.

Enemy Movement & Behavior

Ships move continuously from spawn points toward their AI-determined best tactical position. All ship movement is grid-locked — ships move from grid to grid, not smoothly between them. This represents the player's tactical map knowledge of ship positions. If a cannon hits a grid, it hits any ship on that grid. Ship movement may appear jerky — this is intentional: this is a tactical map, not a simulation. Ships fire OVER troops, not through them — ship projectiles arc above ground units. Ships prefer to target walls (their goal is to breach the perimeter). Troops prefer cannons and castles (their goal is to destroy defenses).

Every enemy must pause before shooting — fast enemies pause longer (catching breath), slow enemies pause briefly (already aimed).

"Crazy Ivan" evasive maneuvers: Triggered when a shot hits within 1 grid of the ship (near miss or direct hit). There is a slight delay before the evasion. The ship makes a sudden lateral move. More common for lighter ships.

Ship stacking: Two or more ships can occupy the same grid. They simply pass through each other. The disadvantage: a single cannon shot hitting that grid damages all ships in it. This is also an enemy tactic to hide numbers — only the first ship into the grid is displayed; additional ships are "hidden" behind it. All ships in the same grid act, react, and fire normally regardless of stacking. The player discovers the true count when the front ship is destroyed or the stack separates.

Ship AI — Deterministic Tactical Positioning

Implementation Spec — Grid-Scoring Functions

Every ship evaluates valid enemy-zone grids using a deterministic scoring function specific to its type. The ship moves toward the highest-scoring grid each tick. Ties are broken by lowest grid index (row-major: row × 64 + col) — this ensures identical positioning on every replay.

Ships recalculate their target grid every 4 ticks (2 seconds) — often enough to react to wall breaches and cannon destruction, infrequent enough to look deliberate (not jittery). Between recalculations, the ship moves toward its current target grid along the shortest path. Rocks can appear in the waterways — ships must navigate around them. Ship pathfinding uses simple obstacle avoidance: if the direct path is blocked by a rock, the ship routes around it via the nearest unblocked adjacent grid. Rocks in waterways create natural chokepoints that constrain ship approach angles, adding another layer to the map designer's toolkit.

Valid grids: A grid is valid for a ship if (a) it is in the waterways, (b) it is outside any staging area, and (c) the ship's full footprint fits (1×2 or 2×2 or 2×4 depending on type). Ships CAN share grids (stacking).

Movement Behavior

Ship movement toward target grid: Ships move 1 grid per tick × their speed stat along the shortest grid-locked path to their target grid. Ships CAN pass through each other (stacking). A ship that reaches its target grid stops and begins firing after the pause-before-shooting delay.

Repositioning: If the board state changes (wall destroyed, cannon destroyed, ship sunk), the next recalculation (every 4 ticks) may produce a new target grid. The ship begins moving toward the new target. This creates organic repositioning — ships shift to exploit new breaches without scripted behavior.

Target fallback: All ships switch to secondary and tertiary targets when their primary target type is completely destroyed. Walls → cannons → castles (for Warships, Scouts, Fire Ships, Torpedo Boats). Cannons → walls → castles (for Mortar Ships). Castles → cannons → walls (for Artillery Barges). If all walls, cannons, AND castles are destroyed, ships stand down — there is nothing left for them to do. (The player has already lost if all structures are gone.)

Hit response: When a ship takes a hit, it immediately recalculates (ignoring the 4-tick cycle) with a temporary +50 bonus to dDMZ (flee impulse) that decays over 4 ticks. This creates the Crazy Ivan evasive behavior — the ship briefly retreats, then returns to its optimal position.

Battering Ram and Siege Ship: These are classified as troops, not ships. They spawn in the waterways and move toward the DMZ using ship movement (grid-locked, toward DMZ). Once they cross the DMZ into the friendly zone, they switch to the troop pathfinding algorithm (weighted A*) to reach their targets.

Accuracy & Range Mechanic

Closer = More Accurate, Faster Fire Rate

Accuracy and fire rate are core combat mechanics that create meaningful positioning decisions for both ships and cannons:

Range falloff (accuracy): The closer a target, the more accurate the shot. At point-blank range: near-100% accuracy. At maximum range: significantly reduced accuracy. This applies to both player cannons AND enemy ships.

Proximity fire rate bonus: Ships and cannons that are closer to their target fire slightly faster. At minimum range: +15% fire rate. At maximum range: base fire rate. The scaling is linear between min and max range. This is a subtle bonus — not enough to dramatically change balance, but enough that ships are incentivized to push closer for damage output. This creates the natural tension: ships WANT to get close for faster shots, but closer means the player's cannons hit more accurately too.

Ship accuracy penalty (movement): Ships are less accurate while moving. A ship that has been stationary for several ticks becomes increasingly accurate — it settles, aims, and fires with precision. This creates a risk/reward: a stationary ship is a better shooter but also an easier target for the player.

Hit disrupts accuracy: When a ship takes a hit, it wants to move (evasion/repositioning). Moving resets its accuracy buildup. The player can disrupt a well-aimed ship by landing a hit, forcing it to reposition and re-aim.

Cannon accuracy advantage: Player cannons are more accurate than ships because they're stationary. Ships compensate with numbers and positioning.

Design consideration — wind affecting fight shots: Should wind blow cannon and ship shots off course during Fight, forcing both sides to adapt? This would add a skill layer (compensate for wind drift on shots, not just piece placement) but may be "too much" complexity. Recommend testing: if wind affects Fight shots, it should be a subtle effect (±0.5 grid) at High wind only, introduced as a Layer D mechanic in later campaigns. Low/Medium wind should NOT affect shots.

Ballistic Arcs & 3D Visual Style

Every Shot Has an Arc — 3D Perspective

All ship cannons and player cannons fire with ballistic arcs. The farther the target, the higher (and longer) the arc. This should be animated with a 3D perspective effect: the projectile appears to get closer to the camera as it climbs (growing larger) and farther from the camera as it falls (shrinking). At the peak of the arc, the projectile is at its largest on screen. This creates depth and drama, especially for long-range Mortar and Doom Cannon shots.

Visual style target: The entire battlefield should have a 3D isometric look reminiscent of Age of Empires — elevated terrain has visible depth, walls have height, castles have towers, and cannons have visible barrels that track their targets. This is a visual polish goal, not a gameplay requirement — the game plays on a 2D grid, but renders with 3D depth cues.

Ship Rollover & Victory

Ships not destroyed by fight-end roll over to the next sortie at current HP and position. Victory requires destroying ALL ships across all sorties (plus castle enclosure). Troops do NOT count toward the win condition — only ships. New ships may be added each sortie from spawn points.

Ship locations during Build & Deploy: Rolled-over ships are visible on the map during the Build and Deploy phases as solid red grid blocks at their last known positions. All ship types use the same generic red block — the player must remember which ship type was at each location. This gives the player strategic information for cannon placement without revealing full intelligence.

Spawn point visibility: After the first sortie in which a ship has emerged from a spawn point, that spawn point is marked during Build and Deploy as a light yellow-orange area with cross-hatching across the 1×4 grid. This marking only appears for spawn points on the edge of the map — the player can see the map edge and infer the spawn location. Stealth spawn points (those placed closer to the DMZ in later battlefields) are never marked — the player must discover them during Fight. On first play, all spawn points are unknown until ships emerge.

No ship preview: There is no preview of upcoming ship types or counts. Ships are too far away to identify before they spawn. The edge of the map is as far as the player can see. On replay, the player's advantage comes from memory, not from UI previews.

Hospital Ship — Full Healing Mechanic

Hospital Ship (2×2, Very Slow) — Heals Other Ships

The Hospital Ship is a support vessel that distributes its own HP to heal other ships at the end of each Fight phase. Multiple Hospital Ships can appear in a sortie — all distribute healing evenly across the fleet, combining their HP pools.

Healing algorithm: At the end of the Fight phase, if any Hospital Ship is alive, it distributes HP from itself to all other surviving ships evenly. Ships cannot exceed 100% HP. Ships at 0% HP cannot be resurrected — dead ships stay dead. The Hospital Ship reduces its own HP by the total amount distributed. If it distributes all its HP, it is destroyed.

The Hospital Ship does NOT heal itself. It is purely selfless — it sacrifices its own HP pool to keep the fleet alive.

The Hospital Ship does NOT count toward the win condition. If the Hospital Ship is the only ship remaining (all combat ships destroyed), it leaves the battlefield — there is nothing left to repair. The player wins. Hospital Ships are support vessels, not combatants.

Distribution rules: Health is split evenly among all surviving ships. If the even split is not perfectly divisible, the remainder goes to the weakest ship (lowest HP%). If a ship reaches 100% before all allocated health is applied, the overflow redistributes to remaining ships that haven't hit 100%. This cascading distribution ensures no health is wasted.

Example: 4 ships at 95%, 94%, 55%, 45% + Hospital Ship at 26%. Even split: 5% each → 100%, 99%, 60%, 50%. Remaining 6%: 1% each to the 3 non-full ships → 100%, 100%, 61%, 51%. Remaining 3%: 1% to 61% and 2% to 51% (weakest gets more). Result: 100%, 100%, 62%, 53%. Hospital Ship at 0% (destroyed — gave everything).

Damage reduces healing capacity. A Hospital Ship at 80% can distribute up to 80%. At 20%, only 20%. The player should prioritize damaging or sinking the Hospital Ship to limit fleet recovery. Even hitting it without sinking it reduces its healing impact.

Troop Transport & Ground Combat

Transport Ships — Deterministic Deployment

A single Transport Ship type carries all ground troop types (except Bombers). The Transport docks on the friendly-zone grids just beyond the DMZ. Once docked, its "fire rate" is the disembark rate. Continuous until destroyed. Transports never empty. Once docked, a Transport does not move again unless the DMZ shifts.

Docking at a wall: If the Transport docks adjacent to a wall, troops disembark into the surrounding grids of the dock point and immediately attack the wall. Troops can exit from any side of the ship's 1×2 grid — even backward. They fan out to adjacent grids and attack whatever wall is blocking them.

Troop composition is fixed per sortie: The specific troop types aboard each Transport are predetermined and identical every time the player plays that sortie. The "surprise" only exists the first time the player encounters that sortie — after that, the player knows exactly which troops are coming from which Transport and can plan accordingly.

Transport sunk mid-deployment: Sinking the Transport stops further troop deployment, but troops already on the ground continue their mission.

Troops reset every sortie. All troops cleared at sortie end. Only ships persist. Troops do NOT count for the win condition.

Aircraft Carrier & Bomber Mechanics

Carriers and Bombers — Linked HP System

Aircraft Carriers launch Bombers from the waterways — no DMZ touch needed (Bombers are aerial).

Shared HP: Each Bomber's HP = Carrier HP ÷ number of Bombers. A Carrier with 5 Bombers → each Bomber has 1/5 the Carrier's HP.

Bomber flight path: Bombers fly in a straight line toward a castle but can deviate 1 grid on either side (Crazy Ivan). Which castle is targeted is determined when the bomber takes off from the Carrier — it is purely random among all surviving castles. If the targeted castle is destroyed while the bomber is in flight, the bomber immediately turns around, lands on the Carrier, and is reassigned to a different random castle on its next launch.

Carrier launch rate: The Carrier's "fire rate" is really how fast the bombers fly — a faster carrier means bombers arrive sooner. A landed bomber takes 4 ticks (2 seconds) before it can be launched again.

Carrier exposure: When a Carrier launches a bomber, the Carrier is fully exposed for ~6 seconds (relevant in fog of war theaters).

Bomber visibility: Bombers are troops, so they are always visible — even over enemy territory. Fog of war does not hide them.

Bomber size: 1×2, with the 1-grid side as the leading edge (nose-forward flight).

Bomber grid sharing: Multiple bombers can occupy the same grid but do NOT stack — each takes damage independently. No 4× stacking bonus like ground troops.

Cannon fire vs bombers: All cannons can target bombers. Cannon shots aimed at bombers fire steeply upward. If the shot hits, the arc stops at the bomber (impact). If the shot misses, the cannonball falls back to earth — it may hit something below (wall, troop, open ground). All bombers fly at the same altitude. If a bomber's flight path crosses a cannon shot already in the air, the shot can hit the bomber — but only if the shot is "at altitude" (the upward portion of the arc, not the falling portion). This creates incidental hits on bombers flying through active fire zones.

Bomber area denial: Bomblets hitting open ground do NOT create area denial on the first hit. The same open ground grid must be hit 4 times before it becomes a crater (area denial). Bomblets hitting walls or castles cause direct damage as normal.

Castle targeting: Which castle a bomber targets is determined at launch — purely random among surviving castles. RNG may send 2 sequential bombers to the same castle. If the targeted castle is destroyed mid-flight, the bomber turns around, lands, and is reassigned to a different random castle on its next launch.

Carrier sunk, bombers airborne: If the Carrier is destroyed while Bombers are deployed, the Bombers complete their current bombing run but then crash at the Carrier's sunk location when they attempt to return. One last strike, then they're gone.

All bombers destroyed → Carrier destroyed: If ALL Bombers are killed, the Carrier is also destroyed. Killing Bombers IS killing the Carrier.

Troop Combat — Adjacency & Stacking

How Troops Deal Damage

Troops cause damage to structures they are side-adjacent to (4-way). A troop adjacent to multiple walls damages ALL of them — a troop touching 3 or 4 walls damages all 3 or 4 simultaneously. Walls are the impediments troops must get through; they attack every surface they contact.

Wall-targeting troop (Battering Ram): This troop specifically targets walls to make them hard to repair. It prefers multi-layer wall sections and attacks only the 4 diagonal walls (not side-by-side walls). After destroying a wall block, the Battering Ram prefers to move diagonally to its next target and likes to destroy every other wall block — leaving a checkerboard of gaps that creates maximum isolation-cleanup vulnerabilities. The goal is to leave walls touching diagonally instead of side-by-side, making the player's repair work as expensive as possible.

Troop stacking (up to 4×): When troops of the same type occupy the same grid, they merge:

— 1 troop: 1× damage, 1× HP

— 2 troops: 2× damage, 1.5× HP

— 3 troops: 3× damage, 2× HP

— 4 troops: 4× damage, 2.5× HP — maximum stack

Stacking happens anywhere on the map when same-type troops occupy the same grid. A single cannon shot hitting a stacked grid damages the entire stack — making stacks dangerous but fragile (a well-aimed shot eliminates 4 threats at once). Troops arriving at a full stack (4) queue behind and seek an alternate route or different target, bunching up at chokepoints.

Speed/HP variation by type: Fast troops (low HP, low damage, flood breaches) vs. heavy troops (high HP, high damage, one breach is enough). The Transport deploys a mix — the player never knows what's coming.

Troop Pathfinding Algorithm — A* with Weighted Terrain

Implementation Spec — Deterministic Weighted A*

Every troop uses A* pathfinding with a weighted cost table to find the lowest-cost path to its target. The path considers terrain difficulty, obstacles that must be destroyed, and other troops. The algorithm is deterministic — same board state produces same path.

Target selection priority: Troops path toward enclosed (non-grey) castles (nearest first). If no enclosed castles exist (all grey), fallback: cannons (nearest), then walls (nearest). Bombers always target castles (see below).

Re-Evaluation Frequency

The goal is every troop re-evaluates its path every tick. With the board at 64×48 and A* only searching the friendly zone (~1,500 grids), a single pathfind is fast. However, late-game battles may have 15–20 troops on the ground simultaneously.

Adaptive batching: The game attempts all troop pathfinds per tick. If the per-tick pathfinding budget is exceeded (target: 4ms total across all troops), troops are split into rotation groups:

— ≤8 troops: All re-evaluate every tick (full responsiveness).

— 9–16 troops: Split into 2 groups. Group A re-evaluates on even ticks, Group B on odd ticks.

— 17–24 troops: Split into 3 groups. Each group re-evaluates every 3rd tick.

— 25+ troops: Split into 4 groups. Each group re-evaluates every 4th tick.

Between re-evaluations, a troop follows its last computed path. The batching simulates command confusion — more troops means slower coordination. This is a feature, not a compromise: large troop swarms should feel chaotic and slightly uncoordinated.

Group assignment is deterministic: troops are assigned to groups by spawn order (troop 0 = group 0, troop 1 = group 1, etc., wrapping). This ensures identical replay behavior.

Deadlock Fallback (No Path Available)

If A* returns no path (e.g., troop is completely boxed in by rocks, area denial, and unstackable troops on all sides), the troop uses a greedy 8-grid fallback:

Step 1: Find the 8 neighboring grids (including diagonals). Pick the passable grid closest to the target (straight-line / as the crow flies).

Step 2: If that grid is blocked by another troop of the same type, treat it as temporarily passable — that troop will likely move on its own tick, clearing the grid. Move toward it (the troops coordinate implicitly because same-type troops move at the same speed and typically have similar targets).

Step 3: If the best grid is blocked by a different-type troop (truly impassable), try the next-closest grid among the 8 neighbors.

Step 4: If ALL 8 neighbors are truly impassable (surrounded by rocks, AoD, and different-type troops on all sides), the troop does not move. It waits until a grid is cleared (troop moves away, AoD cycles out, wall is destroyed by another troop). The troop re-evaluates every tick (or on its batch tick) and will resume movement as soon as any neighbor becomes passable.

Bomber Targeting

Bombers fly over walls and target castles only. They do not use A* — they fly in a straight line toward their assigned castle with ±1 grid Crazy Ivan deviation. Never-enclosed (grey) castles are ignored. If all castles are grey: fallback to cannons, then walls. See Aircraft Carrier & Bomber Mechanics for full bomber behavior.

Troops adjacent to a cannon: While troops deal damage to a cannon, the cannon's shots cause 3 HP damage per shot to all adjacent troops — the cannon's blast and recoil hurt nearby ground units. This makes cannon-adjacent troops take attrition even without direct player targeting. A stack of 4 infantry (125 effective HP) adjacent to a cannon firing every 4 ticks dies from recoil alone in ~167 ticks (83 seconds) — slow but meaningful in long boss fights.

Uniform damage: Troops deal the same damage per tick to all structure types — walls, cannons, and castles. The damage values in the Troop Types table apply regardless of target. The TTK differences come from structure HP: walls (100 HP) fall faster than cannons (150 HP) which fall faster than castles (500 HP).

Friendly Fire & Collateral Damage

Introduced at Campaign 11 (First BC Combo) — Not Before

Friendly fire is OFF in Campaigns 1–10. It is introduced at Campaign 11 (the first BC layer combo — advanced enemies + hard pieces). During the 3-2-1-FIGHT transition for every battle in Campaign 11+, a message appears overlaid on the transition: "Watch out for friendly fire, [Commander Name]." The 2.5–3.0 second transition is long enough for this message to be read.

Targeting tutorial prompt (Campaigns 1–2): During the first Fight phase of Campaign 1 and Campaign 2 battles, a pulsing prompt appears after the first enemy ship reaches firing range: "Tap a ship to focus fire!" The prompt disappears after the player taps any ship (or after 5 seconds). This teaches the targeting mechanic even when auto-fire would suffice — the player learns that targeting is faster than auto-fire, a skill that becomes essential in later campaigns.

Enemy ships firing over own troops: Ship shots arc over troops — they do NOT pass through troop clusters. Ships target walls and structures behind their own troops without hitting them. However, area-of-effect damage IS an exception. If a ship's shot has an area of effect (e.g., Mortar Ship splash damage) that overlaps friendly troop positions, those troops WILL take damage. Similarly, if a bomber's attack hits its own troops, those troops take damage. The player can exploit this — positioning walls to force enemy bombers and AoE ships to damage their own troops is a valid tactical strategy.

Player cannon collateral: Cannon shots that land on a wall block, another cannon, or a castle cause ½ damage to those friendly structures. A cannon does NOT damage itself. Shooting enemy troops stacked against your wall damages both — an intentional tactical tradeoff.

Strategic implication: A skilled player could deliberately shoot their own walls to create a planned breach — a legitimate (if desperate) tactic.

Area Denial & Ground Damage

Ground Damage Visuals & Behavior

Fire Ships, Siege Ships, and Bombers leave area denial effects on the map: burn zones, craters, rubble. These block both wall placement AND cannon placement. Key rules:

Carry-over damage: If ground damage persists into the next Build phase, the damaged grids show as holes (dark pit visual) or holes with fire (if from fire-based attacks). Interior holes look like breaches in the floor. All carry-over damage is impassable (troops, enemies cannot cross). Damage that does NOT carry over is cleared during Build Step 1 (the cleanup wipe) along with spackle removal and wall repair.

Troop blocking: Troops cannot walk over ground damage — not in the current Fight phase, and not in the next Fight phase if the damage persists. Area denial affects both sides: it blocks the player's building AND blocks enemy troop pathing.

Enemy rock collision: Enemies (ships and troops) cannot travel over rocks. Rocks are impassable terrain for everyone. This makes rock placement on the base map a strategic element — rocks near spawn points funnel enemies into kill zones.

Ships Targeting Castles

Ships can damage castles directly. While most ship fire targets walls (their goal is to breach the perimeter), ships WILL target exposed castles — particularly if the enclosure has already been breached. Castle damage from ships follows the same HP system as wall damage. This makes wall integrity even more critical: a breached perimeter exposes castles to direct naval bombardment.

Boss Retreat Behavior

When a Mystarch boss is attacking a castle and takes cannon fire, it will attempt to retreat to avoid continued attack. The boss moves away from the cannon that hit it, seeking a position outside that cannon's effective range while maintaining proximity to its target castle. If multiple cannons hit the boss, it retreats toward the direction with the least cannon coverage. The retreat is not permanent — after 4–6 ticks (2–3 seconds) without taking fire, the boss resumes its attack approach. This creates a push-pull dynamic: concentrated cannon fire drives the boss back, but the player must sustain pressure or the boss returns. Spread cannons across multiple positions to keep the boss permanently suppressed, or focus fire from one direction to create a predictable retreat path.

Last Garrison — Castle Desperation State

When a Castle Falls Below 5% HP

When any enclosed castle drops below 5% HP during the Fight phase, it begins to glow with a pulsing amber light. This is the Last Garrison state — the troops stationed inside the castle abandon their posts and man the nearest cannons in a desperate attempt to save their home.

Effect: All player cannons receive a +100% fire rate boost (double speed) but suffer a -20% accuracy penalty. The garrison is firing frantically — more shots, less precision. The screen subtly shakes. The music shifts to an urgent, percussive variant. The haptic pulses in a rapid heartbeat pattern.

Duration: Last Garrison persists until either the castle is destroyed (0% HP → defeat if below required count) or the Fight phase ends. If the castle survives the sortie and is enclosed in the next Build phase, it heals normally.

Does not stack. If two castles are below 5% simultaneously, the fire rate boost is the same as one. The desperation effect is binary — it's on or it's off.

5% chance of cascade: When Last Garrison triggers, there is a 5% chance (1 in 20) that the castle drops to 0% and is destroyed. The garrison's desperate defense may not be enough — the structural damage was too severe. This adds a moment of genuine dread when the glow appears: "Will it hold?"

Audio: When Last Garrison triggers: alarm bell + urgent horn. Pvt. Reyes: "The castle is falling! EVERYONE TO THE CANNONS!" (text only, localized). When the castle survives: relief horn. When destroyed: collapse rumble + silence.

Wind Direction

Wind uses 8 compass points: N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW. Cardinal directions (N, S, E, W) appear in easier campaigns. Diagonal directions (NE, SE, SW, NW) are introduced in harder campaigns (Campaign 10+), adding an extra layer of complexity because the player must compensate in two axes simultaneously.

The wind is shown as a compass with a directional arrow. The arrow points in the wind direction. The arrow's length indicates speed and its color indicates drift:

Desert and Frozen base drift: These two theaters get +1 permanent base drift added to whatever the battle's wind strength is. A "Calm" wind in Desert is still 1-grid drift. This is a theater-specific mechanic, not a Layer D weather effect — it applies even when Layer D is off.

What drifts: Wall pieces shift on placement based on wind direction and strength. Cannons are too heavy to drift. Drift is applied at soft lock — the moment the player lifts their finger, the piece slides to its drifted position. The ghost preview during drag already accounts for drift (shows where the piece WILL land, not where the finger is). Every time the player picks up a soft-locked piece and drops it again, drift applies again. Moving a piece in the wind means accepting repeated drift. This makes wind a genuine puzzle: place once, carefully.

Drift-stop collision: A drifting wall piece stops at any obstacle that would make the placement invalid. Walls, castles, cannons, rocks, cliffs, DMZ grids, waterway grids, and map edges all halt the drift. The piece comes to rest at the last valid position before the collision. This means existing structures act as windbreaks — placing a piece adjacent to an existing wall on the windward side prevents drift entirely. Skilled players exploit drift-stop to place pieces precisely even in High wind.

Spackle does NOT drift. Spackle is an emergency patch — a panic-mode, last-ditch decision. Adding drift to spackle would feel punishing and unfair. Spackle goes exactly where the player drops it, regardless of wind. This is a deliberate exception to the drift rule.

Victory & Defeat

Victory Condition

The player wins a battle when all required castles are enclosed at the end of any Build phase (including the Last Stand). That's it. Enclosure is the sole win condition.

Ships do NOT need to be destroyed for victory. Surviving ships remain as ongoing threats — they damage walls, land troops, and pressure the player's defenses — but the battle ends in victory the moment the enclosure requirement is met at the end of a Build phase. The star system rewards ship destruction as one of three performance levers, driving the player to clear the battlefield even though it isn't strictly required.

Boss Finale: The boss's HP must reach 0 AND all required castles must be enclosed. This is the only battle type where killing a specific enemy is required for victory.

Last Stand Build Phase

After the final sortie's Fight phase, the game gives the player one additional Build phase — the "Last Stand." This is their final opportunity to enclose castles and repair walls. There is no Deploy or Fight after this. The Last Stand timer = 100% of remaining Fight time from the final sortie (shown throughout the battle as "Earned: +XX sec"). The player can also spend Stonwryts on Alter Time to extend the Last Stand by up to 30 seconds. Unused Last Stand time does NOT contribute to star ratings. The player should use every second to enclose more territory — improving the Area Enclosed star lever — rather than receiving it as a passive bonus.

Short circuit: If all required castles are already enclosed when the Last Stand begins, the timer is skipped — instant victory. The earned time wasn't needed.

Defeat Conditions

The game ends in defeat as soon as the player reaches an unwinnable state. The game does not force the player to play out a lost cause:

Enclosure failure: Required castles are not enclosed at the end of any Build phase (including Last Stand). The game ends immediately — no Deploy or Fight.

Castle destruction: A castle's degradation counter reaches 3/3, the castle is destroyed, and remaining castles drop below the required count. Immediate defeat.

Ships remaining after final Fight: Ships remaining is NOT a defeat condition (ships don't need to be destroyed for victory). However, surviving ships continue to damage walls and land troops, making the Last Stand Build phase harder. The battle proceeds to the Last Stand regardless of ship count.

Boss timeout: In Boss Finale battles — all sorties completed, boss still alive. Immediate defeat.

Mid-Build detection: If during the Build phase, it becomes mathematically impossible to enclose the required castles (e.g., a castle was just destroyed and the remaining castle count is below the requirement), the Build phase ends immediately in defeat. The player is not forced to waste time building walls around a lost cause.

There is no "health bar" for the player. The walls ARE the health bar. As long as you can enclose castles and sorties remain, you're in the fight.

Enemies

All enemies move across the map from the waterways toward the friendly zone. Movement is continuous during the Fight phase, not turn-based. Every enemy must pause before shooting — faster enemies have a longer pause (catching their breath), slower enemies pause briefly (already aimed). This gives the player a visible warning.

Troops are deployed from Transport Ships (or theater-equivalent transports). Bombers are launched from Aircraft Carriers. Killing a transport before it drops troops prevents those troops from spawning. Killing a carrier before bombers launch prevents that group of bombers. Ships across all theaters share recognizable silhouette profiles with theater-appropriate skins — the player learns to identify enemy types regardless of theater.

Balance Relationships — Quick Reference

How Everything Interacts

Cannon vs Ship: Standard Cannon kills Warship in 5 shots (~10 sec). 4 cannons focused-fire kill a Warship in ~2.5 sec. This means a 4-cannon defense can handle 2–3 Warships simultaneously. Late-game fleets of 6–8 ships require 6+ cannons or smart use of Chain/Scatter.

Ship vs Wall: A single Warship takes ~18 sec to destroy one wall block. A wall perimeter of 20 blocks survives a single Warship for ~6 minutes — far longer than any Fight phase. But 4 Warships focused on the same wall section break through in ~4.5 seconds. Concentrated fire is the threat, not individual ships.

Troop vs Castle: Infantry deals 5 dmg/tick to castles (500 HP). One infantry trooper takes 100 ticks (50 sec) to destroy a castle alone. But a transport drops troops continuously — 3 infantry on a castle deal 15/tick = 33 seconds to destruction. Transports must be killed before docking.

Sapper vs Wall: A Sapper reaches the wall in ~17 ticks (8.5 sec) from DMZ. A Standard Cannon kills it in ~5 shots (10 sec). Tight timing — the player must prioritize Sappers immediately or lose a 2×2 section. This is the designed tension for Sapper encounters.

Battering Ram vs Wall: Once a Ram reaches the wall, it deals 40 dmg every 3 ticks (100% accuracy). A wall block dies in 8 ticks (4 sec). A Ram chews through a wall section alarmingly fast. But the Ram has 200 HP and moves at 0.6 speed — the player has time to kill it during approach (8 Standard Cannon shots = 16 ticks during a ~20 tick approach).

Part VI — Castles

Castle & Wall Health

Wall Block HP

Every wall block has HP. An HP indicator only appears on wall blocks that have been hit — undamaged walls show no health bar, keeping the board visually clean. As a wall block takes damage, its sprite shows progressive cracking.

Below 25% HP: The wall block is unrepairable. It still functions during the current Fight (blocks enemies, has remaining HP), but is automatically removed during the next Build phase's cleanup wipe (step 1). Visual: heavy cracks, crumbling appearance, red-tinted.

At 0% HP: The wall block is destroyed immediately during Fight, creating an instant gap in the wall perimeter.

Castle HP

Castles have high HP. Castles can only be damaged by enemy troops and bombers. Ships cannot damage castles directly.

Castle HP display: A 3-segment halo ring surrounds the castle. The halo IS the HP bar. Segments can be partially filled after Fight damage. Halo color: green above 66% (2+ segments), yellow above 33% (1+ segments), red below 33%. Visual damage on the castle sprite is progressive: cracks, crumbling towers, collapsing battlements.

Boss castles: On every base map, the starting 4 visible castles serve as the Boss castles during the Boss Finale. They are the same castles the player has been defending since Campaign 1 — no special markings or different appearance from the other 6 regular castles that appear later in the progression. During the Boss Finale (which uses only 4 castles total, all required), these starting 4 are the ones that appear. The emotional weight comes from familiarity: the player has invested dozens of sorties defending these specific castles and now must defend all 4 with no spare.

Castle Degradation — 3-Strike Rule

Castle Degradation & Enclosure Healing

Castle degradation only begins after a castle has been enclosed at least once. A never-enclosed castle sits as grey and ignored during Fight. Once enclosed and subsequently exposed, the countdown begins:

3/3 (full): Castle is healthy. All 3 halo segments filled.

2/3: First sortie exposed. Cracks appear. Warning indicator.

1/3: Second consecutive sortie exposed. Heavy damage. Urgent flash.

0/3: Third consecutive sortie exposed. Castle destroyed. Removed permanently.

Enclosure restores HP: Each sortie the castle is enclosed, the castle recovers 1/3 of its total HP. A castle at 1/3 that is enclosed recovers to 2/3. Two enclosed sorties: 1/3 → 3/3. Troop/bomber damage during Fight can partially reduce a segment — the halo visually drains as damage is taken. Green shimmer VFX on recovery; gold pulse on full recovery.

Example: A castle at 55% HP (damaged by troops) that is enclosed receives +33% HP → 88%. A castle at 30% enclosed → 63%. The 1/3 recovery is based on total HP, not the current segment display.

Destroyed Cannon Recovery

Cannon Ruins, Enclosure Rebuild & Permanent Ruin

Cannons at 0% modifier that are enclosed after Build cleanup → restored to 50%. This is the ONLY window to save a destroyed cannon — it must be enclosed in the Build phase immediately following its destruction.

If a 0% cannon is NOT enclosed in that Build phase and the player does not trash it during the subsequent Deploy phase, it becomes a permanent ruin. A ruined cannon can never be restored. It remains on the board as a 2×2 obstacle — treated like rocks (blocks wall placement, permanent obstruction). Ruins look like gray rubble on the cannon's 2×2 footprint. The player must plan their Build carefully: enclose destroyed cannons immediately, or trash them during Deploy to recover the slot.

Trash limits: The player's cannon trash budget per battle = ⌈total_sorties / 2⌉ (ceiling). A 6-sortie battle gives 3 cannon trashes total. A 5-sortie battle gives 3. A 3-sortie battle gives 2. Once the budget is spent, a destroyed cannon that isn't enclosed WILL become a permanent ruin. This creates genuine stakes: protect your cannons, enclose them if destroyed, or spend your precious trash to clear them before they become permanent obstacles.

Design consideration: At 50%, a rebuilt cannon is weak but functional. The player must decide: invest sorties nurturing it back to health via enclosure bonuses, or trash it and deploy fresh. Both strategies have merit. The permanent ruin consequence for inaction ensures the player must make a deliberate choice — doing nothing is the worst option.

Loss & Replay

Defeat Display

When the player loses, a popup displays "Defeated" along with the reason for defeat (e.g., "Castle enclosure failed," "Ships remaining — no sorties left," "Castle destroyed (3/3 degradation)"). The popup also shows near-miss metrics (see Defeat Replay) and the player's remaining strikes.

Three-Strike Drop-Back Rule

3 Losses = Drop Back One Level

Each battle has a 3-strike counter. If the player loses the same battle 3 times, they drop back to the previous battle in the campaign. The strike counter is visible at the start of each battle (3 shield icons, one breaks per loss) and on the defeat popup (shows remaining strikes).

Strike 1: "Keep trying! 2 attempts remaining." Retry button is primary.

Strike 2: "One more chance!" Retry button is primary.

Strike 3 (drop-back): "Dropped back to Battle [N-1]. Regroup and try again." The player is returned to the previous battle within the same campaign. If the player is on Battle 1 of any campaign, there is no drop-back — they simply retry until they win (the 3-strike counter still shows but Strike 3 just says "Try again" instead of dropping back). Strikes reset when the player returns to the battle they were dropped from.

Avoid drop-back: On Strike 3, the player can avoid the drop-back by spending 142 Stonwryts OR watching a 30-second rewarded video ad. The option appears on the defeat popup: "Stay on this battle? (142 Stonwryts or Watch Ad)." If chosen, the strike counter resets to 0 and the player retries the same battle. This gives the player agency — they can choose to accept the drop-back (free) or spend resources to persist. Ad-free players always have the Stonwryt option.

Flow state purpose: The 3-strike system prevents frustration spirals (losing 10 times to the same battle) while keeping the "I want to immediately retry" impulse intact. The drop-back feels fair — it's not a punishment, it's a tactical retreat.

Defeat popup buttons: "Retry" (large, primary, bright) — reloads the same battle immediately (3-2-1 countdown into Build). "Home" (smaller, secondary) — returns to the home screen. Retry is always the default action. On Strike 3 (drop-back), the Retry button changes to "Go to Battle [N-1]" and the Home button remains.

Concede ("White Flag")

At any time during a battle, the player can concede via the X button menu (see Save/Pause). This triggers a confirmation popup: "Concede?" with Yes / No buttons. The player must confirm — no accidental surrenders. Conceding follows all normal loss rules (drop-back, star preservation, etc.). The UI should use universal iconography (white flag icon) rather than text.

Drop-Back Rule

Active campaign loss: The player drops back ONE battle from the one they just attempted. The campaign boundary is the floor — the player NEVER drops below the first battle of their current campaign.

Completed campaign loss: Replaying a completed campaign — a loss has no consequence. No drop-back. Stars don't decrease.

Replaying Battles

The player can always return to any previously completed battle to earn more stars. Earned time for the Last Stand resets when replaying a battle — it starts at zero and must be re-earned. Replaying does not affect current campaign progress unless the player is actively within that campaign.

Tactical Hints

If the player loses the same battle 3 times in a row, the game offers a brief tactical hint. The game never auto-skips or reduces difficulty. Respect the player's ability to learn.

Boss Battles Are a Separate Level Type

The Boss Finale is NOT a regular campaign — it is a distinct game mode unlocked by completing Campaign 32. All five layers are active plus a unique boss per theater. Castle Requirement Progression — Game Theory

Escalating Commitment (Kahneman)

Castle requirements follow the escalating commitment pattern. Early campaigns: 1 of 4 castles = easy triage, maximum freedom. As the player invests sorties defending specific castles, the endowment effect makes them emotionally valuable. The game gradually demands more (1/4 → 5/6 → 9/10), forcing harder triage with higher stakes. Crucially, the player never has to enclose ALL visible castles (except Boss 4/4) — there is always a strategic choice about which to sacrifice.

1/4 (Cam 1–3): Maximum freedom. 4 castles on the map, only 1 required. Player experiments freely.

2/4 (Cam 4–6): First real strategic choice. Which 2 castles? Investment begins.

3/6 (Cam 7–10): 6 castles now visible. Triage deepens — player commits to a region.

4/6 (Cam 11–13): Two-thirds of visible castles needed. Significant wall investment required.

5/6 (Cam 14–16): Only one castle can be sacrificed. Mistakes are expensive.

6/8 (Cam 17–20): 8 castles now visible. Six required — the map demands long wall routes.

7/8 (Cam 21–26): Near-total commitment. One spare among eight.

8/10 (Cam 27–31): All 10 castles now visible. 8 required — only 2 can be sacrificed.

9/10 (Cam 32): Full Stack. One spare. Zero margin for error.

4/4 + Boss (Boss Finale): Compact boss map. 4 castles, all required + boss HP = 0.

The War System — Full Reset

War II and Beyond

After completing the Boss Finale, the player can begin War II. EVERYTHING resets to the starting state: the player returns to Campaign 1, Tier 1 pieces, no unlocked cannons (except Standard), no storage slots, no trash/spackle, difficulty levels reset, and Stonwryts do NOT carry over. It's a complete fresh start — nothing transfers between Wars.

Stonwryt isolation: Stonwryts are tracked per-War and per-difficulty-level. A player's Stonwryt balance in War I Recruit mode is separate from War I Commander mode, which is separate from War II Commander mode. This prevents easy-level farming — a player cannot grind Recruit difficulty for Stonwryts and spend them in Legend difficulty. Each War × difficulty combination is its own economy.

What changes in War II: 10 new base maps (different battlefields per theater) + 190 new environmental overlays, adjusted castle requirements (tighter progression — maybe starting at 2/4 instead of 1/4), different ship counts and compositions, tighter Build timers, and potentially wider DMZ (2+ grids). The campaign structure and layer matrix remain identical.

The appeal is nostalgic replay: "start again, but do it better this time, knowing what I know now." Blowing through the first few campaigns with mastery of the Build phase is a fun diversion — but the tighter parameters ensure the mid-game and endgame are genuinely harder. The player brings skill knowledge, not stat advantages.

War III, IV, etc. follow the same pattern with progressively tighter tuning. Each War is a complete product: 32 campaigns + Boss Finale on fresh maps.

Final Rating Calculation

Average the three levers, round up. Example: Ships = ★ (1), Cannons = ★★ (2), Enclosure = ★★★ (3) → average = 2.0 → final = ★★. Another example: Ships = ★★ (2), Cannons = ★★★ (3), Enclosure = ★★ (2) → average = 2.33 → rounds up to ★★★.

★★★! requires excellence: To earn ★★★!, at least 2 of the 3 levers must be at ★★★! and the remaining lever must be at least ★★★. Simple averaging cannot reach ★★★! — it demands near-perfect performance across the board.

★★★! is hidden. It does not appear in any UI as a goal. When earned, it is a surprise reward — the exclamation mark flies in with a special animation. The player cannot predict or target it because the ! thresholds are not shown.

Frequency target: A skilled player should earn ★★★! roughly once every 15–20 battles. Server-tuned thresholds target this frequency.

0★ — The Pacifist Easter Egg

It is possible to earn 0 stars. If the player kills no ships AND no cannons survive, but the minimum enclosure requirement is met (castles enclosed = victory), the result is 0★. The first and only time a player achieves 0★ in each campaign, they earn a Stonwryt bonus: 10 Stonwryts per campaign level. Campaign 1 = 10 Stonwryts (trivially easy). Campaign 32 = 320 Stonwryts (extremely difficult to survive without killing anything or losing all cannons). This is an undocumented easter egg — no hint in the UI. Players discover it organically or through community sharing. The reward only triggers once per campaign — replaying for 0★ yields nothing.

Star Display During Gameplay

The info panel at the top of the screen shows the current projected star rating for each lever in real-time. Three small star indicators update as the battle progresses. Long-press on any star opens a graph overlay showing what the player needs to achieve for each star level in that category. The ! threshold is NOT shown — it remains hidden.

Adaptive Star Thresholds

Star thresholds are not permanently fixed. Server-side analytics track completion rates per battle for each of the three levers independently. If a lever's 3-star rate is too high (>40% of players), its threshold tightens. If the 1-star rate is too low, it loosens. Changes are gradual. Offline play uses the last-fetched thresholds.

Last Stand time does NOT contribute to stars. The player should use that time to enclose more territory (improving the Area Enclosed lever) rather than receiving it as a passive bonus.

Star Earning Examples & Battle Score

A player who focuses on ship killing but neglects their cannons and barely encloses: Ships ★★★ (3) + Cannons ★ (1) + Enclosure ★ (1) = avg 1.67 → ★★. A player who builds a massive enclosed fortress but ignores ships: Ships ★ (1) + Cannons ★★★ (3) + Enclosure ★★★ (3) = avg 2.33 → ★★★. The system rewards balanced play without punishing specialization — any two levers at ★★★ guarantees ★★★ overall regardless of the third.

Battle Score — The Raw Performance Number

The Battle Score is the unrounded sum of the three star lever percentages:

Battle Score = ships_destroyed_% + area_enclosed_% + avg_cannon_health_%

Example: 80% ships + 45% area + 110% cannons = 235. Maximum per battle = 100 + 100 + 200 = 400. Battle Score is the universal performance metric used for leaderboard ranking, Weekend Race movement, and server-side analytics. Unlike the rounded star rating (which collapses detail into 0–3★), Battle Score preserves the full resolution of the player's performance. Two players with identical ★★★ ratings may have Battle Scores of 287 vs 312 — the leaderboard knows who played better.

Daily Challenges — Skill-Building Game Packs

Daily Challenges unlock after Campaign 1 completion. All challenges are designed to build core gameplay skills — there are no mini-games outside the game's mechanics. Every challenge uses the same walls, cannons, ships, and terrain the player encounters in campaign battles.

14-Day Game Pack Cycle

7 Game Packs × 2 Days Each = 14-Day Rotation

The daily challenge system runs on a 14-day repeating cycle. Each 2-day window features one Game Pack — the same board and challenge type with 5 progressive difficulty levels. The player starts at Level 1 and must complete it to unlock Level 2, and so on. The highest level completed without losing determines the reward package.

Package mechanics: Only one package can be opening at a time. The timer starts when the player taps "Open" on a completed package. The purpose is to bring the player back to the game a few hours later — a natural retention loop. 3 package slots: The daily challenge screen has 3 slots that hold earned packages. This means 6 days of play can accumulate (3 game packs × 2-day windows). Pack 1 (Days 1–2): Speed Enclosure. Build walls to enclose as large an area as possible within a time limit. Same board every attempt. Difficulty levers: time limit (shorter = harder), rock density (more rocks = less build space). Teaches: fast wall placement, spatial planning under pressure.

Pack 2 (Days 3–4): Efficient Enclosure. Enclose a target percentage of the board using a limited number of wall pieces. No time limit. Same board. Difficulty levers: required enclosure percentage (higher = harder), rock density. Teaches: piece efficiency, maximizing coverage per piece.

Pack 3 (Days 5–6): Castle Defense. Protect a castle from waves of attacking troops. Build walls, then watch troops attack — hope your walls keep the castle enclosed. Same board. Difficulty levers: build time (shorter = harder), number of troop waves. Teaches: defensive wall design, anticipating breach points.

Pack 4 (Days 7–8): Ship Hunting. Use pre-placed cannons to sink approaching ships — like duck hunt. No building. Difficulty levers: number of cannons available (fewer = harder), number and type of ships. Teaches: cannon targeting, understanding ship movement patterns and weaknesses.

Pack 5 (Days 9–10): Wind Mastery. Build walls in high wind conditions. The board has minimal obstacles but strong, shifting wind. Same board. Difficulty levers: wind strength and direction changes, time limit. Teaches: drift-stop exploitation, windbreak placement, adapting to wind mechanics.

Pack 6 (Days 11–12): Full Siege. A compressed full-loop battle: one Build, one Deploy, one Fight. Unique constraints per difficulty (pentominoes only, no spackle, gale-force wind, all fragile ground). Same board. Difficulty levers: piece restrictions, enemy composition. Teaches: complete gameplay loop under unusual constraints.

Pack 7 (Days 13–14): Cannon Endurance. Pre-placed walls and castles. The player deploys cannons and must survive multiple increasingly difficult Fight waves without rebuilding. Cannon health carries between waves — protecting cannons is critical. Difficulty levers: number of waves (more = harder), starting cannon budget. Teaches: cannon placement optimization, type selection, enclosure strategy for cannon preservation.

Replay Rules

Progressive — Complete or Lose, Then Decide

The player progresses through levels 1→2→3→4→5 sequentially. A loss at any level ends the run — the package earned corresponds to the last level completed. Level 1 loss = no package. The player can replay the entire pack from Level 1 within the 2-day window, but replaying resets their package to the new result. This creates tension: "I got Level 3. Do I try again for Level 4, risking losing it all?"

Leaderboard Scoring

Leaderboard Score = Accumulated Battle Score

The leaderboard uses the Battle Score — the raw sum of the three star lever percentages (ships_% + area_% + cannon_%). No division, no rounding. This produces large, differentiated scores with minimal ties.

Example: 80% ships + 45% area + 110% cannons = 235 Battle Score. Max per battle = 400. Scores are additive across all battles — a player's total leaderboard score grows with every battle played.

Each leaderboard entry shows three sub-scores alongside the total: Ships (accumulated), Area (accumulated), Cannons (accumulated). This lets players see where they're strong and where competitors beat them. A player with 50,000 total might have 20K ships + 15K area + 15K cannons — another player at 50,000 might be 15K + 20K + 15K. The breakdown reveals different playstyles.

Leaderboard Structure

No ranking for daily challenges. Daily challenges earn packages and streak progress but do not contribute to leaderboard scores.

Sortable columns: Every leaderboard can be sorted by any column — Total score (default), Ships sub-score, Area sub-score, or Cannon sub-score. Tapping a column header sorts the board by that column descending. This lets players find the best ship hunters, the best fortress builders, or the best cannon protectors independently. A small sort arrow indicator shows which column is active.

1. Overall Leaderboard: Accumulation of scores for all campaign battles at all difficulty levels. The ultimate ranking. Each entry shows: total score, ships sub-score, area sub-score, cannon sub-score, difficulty badge, streak shield. Broken down by region (global, country, state/province).

2. Per-Level Leaderboards: Score for all battles within a specific difficulty level (Recruit, Commander, Veteran, Legend). Shows the same three sub-scores. Broken down by region.

3. Per-Campaign Leaderboards: Score for all battles within a specific campaign at a specific difficulty level. Allows players to compare performance on the same set of 5–10 battles. Broken down by region.

4. Friends Leaderboard: Shows friends' scores with normalized scoring — adjusted for difficulty level so a Legend player's score is comparable to a Recruit player's score. Difficulty badge shown next to each entry. All three sub-scores visible.

Regions & Skill Matching

Geographic Regions + Campaign-Based Skill Matching

Leaderboards use 20–50 pre-defined geographic regions worldwide, with natural boundaries (e.g., US Northeast, US Southeast, UK, Germany, Japan, South Korea, Brazil). The player's region is determined by their App Store country + time zone. Regional filtering applies to the Overall, Per-Level, and Per-Campaign boards.

Normalized scoring (Friends board): The Friends board adjusts for difficulty so players at different levels compete fairly. A Legend player's raw Battle Score is divided by the Legend difficulty multiplier; a Recruit player's by the Recruit multiplier. This means a Recruit player who maxes every lever can rank above a Legend player who plays sloppily — rewarding skill relative to chosen difficulty rather than raw difficulty selection.

Stonwryt — In-Game Currency

Stonwryts are named for the ancient Stonewright guild — the master craftsmen who built every wall, tower, and fortification in Shoreland history, including Rivenkeep itself. In the old guild's language, a Stonwryt was a unit of trust: the assurance that a wall would hold, earned only through demonstrated skill. You could not buy a Stonwryt — you earned it by building something that stood. The Commander revived the term for his garrison's currency: skill proven in battle, tradeable for the craftsmen's aid. Stonwryts are earned through gameplay only — they can never be purchased with real money. This is a core integrity rule that protects leaderboard fairness and honors the guild's principle: trust is earned, never bought.

Challenges — Meta-Achievement System

Challenges are long-term goals that span sorties, battles, campaigns, and wars. They scale all the way to the very last battle. Completing a challenge earns a reward package (same timer mechanic as daily challenge packages — smaller rewards open faster, larger rewards take hours). Package rewards (not direct Stonwryts): Challenge rewards are delivered as packages with timers. Small rewards (under 100 Stonwryts) open in 15–30 minutes. Medium rewards (100–500) take 1–4 hours. Weekend Race — 48-Hour Competition

Every weekend, 50 players are matched into a 48-hour race. The race runs from Friday 6:00 PM to Sunday 6:00 PM (local time). Players compete by playing campaign battles — daily challenges do NOT count. Only campaign battle Battle Scores contribute to race movement.

How It Works

Race Mechanics

Matchmaking: Players are grouped into pools of 50 based on their current campaign progress (±3 campaigns). A Campaign 15 player races against Campaign 12–18 players. This keeps races competitive.

Movement: Each campaign battle won moves the player forward on the race board. Movement = the battle's Battle Score (ships_% + area_% + cannon_%). A battle scoring 235 moves the player 235 spaces. This uses the raw performance numbers, not rounded stars — every percentage point of ships destroyed, area enclosed, and cannon health matters.

Stonwryt Pool: As players advance, they contribute Stonwryts to a shared pool:

— Every 500 spaces moved: player adds 10 Stonwryts to the pool (automatic, not deducted from player's balance — bonus Stonwryts generated by the race).

— The pool grows as all 50 players advance. A competitive race might generate a pool of 2,000–5,000 Stonwryts.

Anti-cheat: Only legitimate battle completions count (server-validated). Replaying a previously-completed battle counts — but movement is based on the player's best Battle Score for that battle. Team Color & Enemy Color

Team color is a 1pt solid-color outline drawn around the player's walls, castles, cannons, and avatar at ~80% opacity. The outline is rendered in code — no additional sprites needed. Enemy color uses the same outline technique on enemy ships and troops. Default = Red. Purchasable enemy colors let the player customize the enemy appearance while keeping gameplay readability (the outline is always high-contrast against the waterway background).

Naming Rights Stacking (2 Slots)

A piece can have 2 naming slots: the current name (active) and the next name (queued). If a player wants to name a piece that already has a name, they can purchase the "next" slot. When the current name expires, the queued name automatically becomes active (its duration clock starts at that moment). If no one queues, the piece reverts to its default name. This prevents a popular piece from ever going unnamed while still allowing naming turnover. A player cannot queue behind an existing queue — only 2 slots total (current + next).

Approval Process

3-day waiting period: After purchase, the name enters a 3-day review queue. The 3-day wait is NOT subtracted from the naming duration.

AI screening: Automatic filter for profanity, slurs, hate speech, sexual content, trademark infringement.

Community reporting: Any player can report a name (must provide reason). Reported names flagged for manual review.

3 chances on rejection: Rejected? The player gets a notification with the reason. They can resubmit — up to 3 total attempts. If a name is rejected, the naming duration clock is still running. The home screen shows an action item badge ("⚠ Rename piece C3") prompting the player to resubmit quickly. All 3 rejected = purchase forfeited, no refund. Policy shown before purchasing.

Once per day, any player can upvote or downvote a custom piece name in their Wall Rack. Thumbs-up/down icon appears next to custom names during Build. Votes are tabulated every 14 days (Friday to Friday, 6 PM, aligned with the game pack cycle). Top-voted names receive a free 7-day extension.

Wall Stacking — Double & Reinforced Walls (War II Feature)

Rare Pieces That Overlay Existing Walls

Wall stacking introduces two new piece variants that can be placed on top of existing walls:

Double Wall (bluish tint, Type 1): Can only be placed overlapping an existing wall. Where the stacking piece overlaps existing wall blocks, those blocks become double-strength (2× hit points, visually bluish instead of white). Where the stacking piece extends beyond the existing wall, nothing is created — those cells are wasted. This rewards precise overlap. The player must find an existing wall segment that matches the stacking piece's shape as closely as possible.

Reinforced Wall (brighter tint, Type 2): Same overlay mechanic, but where the piece overlaps existing walls, those blocks become double-strength AND where the piece extends beyond existing walls, single-strength walls are created. This is strictly better than Type 1 — it both reinforces and extends. Much rarer draw.

Stacking rules:

— Cannot stack on a block that is already doubled. Double walls are the maximum — no triple-stacking.

— Stacking pieces use the same 4×4 ghost preview and rotation mechanics as regular pieces.

— Stacking pieces appear in the Wall Rack with a distinctive blue/bright border and a "⬆" overlay icon.

— Double walls show a different texture (bluish stone with reinforcement lines vs. standard cream/white).

Draw frequency: Stacking pieces should be rare enough to feel special but common enough to plan around. Suggested: ~5% of all draws in War II are stacking pieces (roughly 1 in 20 draws). Type 1 (Double) at 4%, Type 2 (Reinforced) at 1%. This means in a typical 6-sortie battle with 12 total piece draws, the player sees approximately 0–1 stacking pieces. Receiving one is an event — the player immediately thinks "where can I best use this?"

Impact analysis:

— Does it break the game? No. The 5% draw rate means stacking is a bonus, not a strategy. The player cannot depend on receiving stacking pieces — they must still build competent walls from regular pieces. The stacking piece is a windfall that rewards good existing wall design.

— Does it complicate gameplay? Slightly. The player must learn one new concept: "blue piece goes on top of walls." The UI makes this clear with the distinctive border and overlay icon. The 4×4 ghost shows which cells will double (blue highlight) and which will be wasted (gray). Placement feedback is immediate and visual.

— Does it affect balance? Yes, positively. Double walls create anchor points that the player can trust. This opens up riskier wall designs in War II — thinner walls in some areas, knowing a double section can absorb the damage. It adds a layer of strategic depth without changing the core Build→Deploy→Fight loop.

— Cannon enclosure interaction: Double walls count the same as single walls for enclosure. No bonus for enclosing with double walls. The benefit is purely defensive (hit points).

Tutorial & First-Launch Experience

On first open of the app, the player enters a video-guided demo on a scaled-down battlefield. The design philosophy: watch first, then do. The player can interrupt any video at any time to try it themselves, and can return to the video whenever they want. No text-heavy instructions. No modal popups blocking gameplay. Just "here's how it works — now you try."

Demo Battlefield

A 1/4-Size Playground

The demo battlefield is 1/4 the size of a standard map (32×24 grids). It contains:

— 1 castle (labeled "Castle" with a floating tag)

— 1 spawn point (labeled "Enemy Spawn")

— DMZ line (labeled "DMZ — No Building")

— Rocks, trees, and a cliff positioned near the castle (teaching terrain from the start)

All tray zones are labeled: "Wall Rack," "Trash," "Spackle," "Storage." Labels fade after the player interacts with each zone.

Phase Bar (Tutorial + All Gameplay)

Across the top of the screen in both the tutorial and all regular gameplay: Build → Deploy → Fight with arrows between them. Each word has a unique icon. The active phase is bright with its timer directly below it. Inactive phases are dimmed. When a phase ends, the timer slides to the next phase word, which brightens as the previous dims. This creates a constant spatial awareness of "where am I in the loop."

Video → Play → Video (Seamless Loop)

On first launch, a guided video begins playing over the demo battlefield. The video shows:

1. A piece being dragged from the Wall Rack to the board.

2. The piece being rotated (tap to rotate 90°).

3. Pieces being placed to enclose the castle — the tiled floor fills in as the enclosure closes.

4. A piece being placed near a rock (turns red, invalid) and then moved to a valid position.

5. Spackle being used to patch a gap.

6. A piece being dragged to storage and saved for later.

7. The enclosure completing — golden flash, "Castle Enclosed!" text.

Player interruption: At any time, the player can tap anywhere on the screen. The video disappears instantly and the real demo battlefield is displayed — fully interactive, all tray zones live, spackle and storage available. Resume video: A "Continue Demo" button appears in the lower-left corner while the video is paused. Tapping it resumes the video from where the player left off. If not enclosed, the game auto-animates placing the minimum pieces needed to complete the enclosure — the player watches their fortress finish building itself. Deploy Phase Demo

A second video plays, showing how to deploy cannons: dragging from the carousel, placing on valid ground (inside the enclosure, next to the castle), and the difference between enclosed vs exposed placement (bright vs dim frames). Save, Pause & the X Button

The X Button — Universal Exit

An X button is always visible in the upper-right corner of the screen during all gameplay (tutorial, battles, daily challenges). Tapping X immediately pauses the game and presents a menu:

— "Resume" — returns to gameplay with a 3-2-1 countdown from exactly where the player left off.

— "Quit to Home" — exits to the home screen. The game state is saved.

The X button works identically during Build, Deploy, and Fight — including boss battles. There is no separate "pause" button — the X IS the pause.

Auto-Save

The game saves the exact game state whenever:

— The player taps X (pause/exit).

— The app is minimized, backgrounded, or closed.

— The app crashes.

— Every phase boundary (Build→Deploy, Deploy→Fight, Fight→Build).

The save captures: board state (all walls, cannons, terrain, damage), phase and timer position, sortie number, earned time bank, Wall Rack contents, storage contents, trash/spackle counts, enemy positions and HP, and all RNG seeds for deterministic replay. When the player returns, the game resumes with a 3-2-1 countdown from the exact saved state. No progress is ever lost.

Mid-Phase Resume

If the save happened mid-Build: resume with remaining Build time. Mid-Deploy: resume with remaining Deploy time. Mid-Fight: resume with remaining Fight time, all enemies at saved positions/HP. The 3-2-1 countdown gives the player a moment to re-orient before action resumes.

iCloud Sync

Automatic Cross-Device Progress Sync

All game progress syncs to iCloud automatically in the background. The player does not need to take any action. Sync triggers:

— Every phase boundary (Build→Deploy, Deploy→Fight, Fight→Build) — the same moments as auto-save.

— After every star improvement (new best rating on any battle).

— After any purchase (team color, avatar pack, piece naming, ad-free).

— When the player closes or minimizes the app — ensures the iCloud state matches the local auto-save exactly. The player should return to the exact same state when reopening, even on a different device.

— At campaign completion milestones.

Sync is silent — no loading spinners, no "syncing..." messages. If the network is unavailable, changes queue locally and sync on next connectivity.

Conflict resolution: If two saves exist (e.g., the player played on two devices), the save with more total stars wins. In a tie, the save with the higher campaign progress wins. The losing save is discarded. No manual merge.

What syncs: Campaign progress, star ratings, cannon unlocks, storage contents, Stonwryt balance, commander name, avatar, team color, all purchases, challenge progress, and settings. The current in-progress battle does NOT sync (too volatile) — only completed state.

Offline play: The core campaign is fully playable offline. Server features (daily challenges, weekend race, leaderboard, piece naming, star threshold tuning) require connectivity but degrade gracefully. Offline players use default star thresholds. Queued purchases sync when connectivity returns.

Fight Phase Screen — 2.5D Perspective Shift

The Dramatic Transition: 2D Planning → 2.5D Combat

The Build and Deploy phases use a top-down 2D map view — clean, precise, and optimized for spatial puzzle-solving. The player thinks like a strategist looking at a war map.

The Fight phase uses a 2.5D isometric perspective — walls have height, cannons have depth, ships move through space with visible masts and sails, cannonballs arc through the air with smoke trails, explosions have volume. The player feels like a commander watching the battle unfold from a hilltop.

The 3-2-1-FIGHT transition: A dramatic crossfade sequence that transforms the flat planning view into the living battlefield. On "3": the 2D map dims slightly, a warm lamplight glow appears at the edges — we're looking at a map on a table. On "2": the camera pans up from the map to an open window, revealing a dark sky with distant flashes of cannon fire. On "1": we fly out the window into the darkness, the sky fills the screen. On "FIGHT": the darkness crossfades into the 2.5D isometric battlefield — the same positions, the same walls, the same cannons, but now rendered with depth, shadow, and physicality. The first cannon fires. This transition takes 2.5–3.0 seconds total and is the emotional crescendo of every sortie.

2.5D rendering details: Isometric projection at approximately 30° tilt (similar to Age of Empires, the reference art style). Walls render as stone blocks with visible tops and sides. Castles show towers, battlements, and the team color flag. Cannons have visible barrels that rotate toward targets. Ships have hulls, masts, and damage states. Trees are 3D billboards. Cliffs have visible elevation. The DMZ water/lava/terrain has depth. All sprites are pre-rendered 2D assets from 3D models (rendered offline into multi-angle sprite sheets), composited by Flame into the isometric scene — no real-time 3D needed.

Camera controls during Fight: Pan and zoom work the same as Build and Deploy. Pinch to zoom, drag to pan. One-finger touch-and-hold (200ms+) triggers targeting instead of pan. Two-finger pinch zooms. Quick drag = pan, deliberate hold = target. Camera button in corner (screenshot — restarts countdown). X button (pause/exit). 1–2 finger targeting on the map. Range circles appear while touching. Cannon HP bars visible above each cannon (green/yellow/red + light green overlay for bonus). Enemy HP bars. Sortie progress bar. Tick counter.

Return to 2D: When the Fight phase ends (timer expires or all ships destroyed), the camera reverses the transition — smoothly returning from 2.5D isometric to the flat 2D map view for the next Build phase. This creates a visual rhythm: plan flat → fight deep → plan flat → fight deep. The contrast makes both views more impactful.

Haptic Feedback

Light haptic: piece placement. Crisp click: rotation tap. Medium haptic: wall breach by enemy. Heavy haptic: boss appearance. Thud: cannon firing short of range. Pulse: trash/spackle counter at 1 remaining. Haptic feedback replaces the need for large visual indicators on small screens.

Why 3 Separate Timers (Not One Session Timer)

Three independent phase timers (Build, Deploy, Fight) rather than one session timer creates three distinct pressure profiles within each sortie. Build pressure is spatial (can I solve this puzzle in time?). Deploy pressure is strategic (is this the right cannon for this position?). Fight pressure is reactive (which ship do I target NOW?). A single session timer would let the player spend 80% on Build and speed-run Deploy/Fight — destroying the three-phase cognitive rotation that prevents monotony. Separate timers force the player to be competent at ALL three skills.

Map ↔ Battlefield Cinematic — Implementation

The Deploy→Fight and Fight→Build transitions are the game's signature moment. Critically, the two gameplay states never interoperate — the flat 2D Map View (Flutter Canvas) and the 2.5D isometric battlefield (Flame) are fully independent scenes. The connective animation is a fixed, generic cinematic (the commander looking out the castle window at the world beyond) that plays the same way every battle and does not depict the player's specific fortress. There is no live sprite morphing and no shared coordinate space between the two views.

Two-path build: (1) First-view cinematic — a pre-rendered video asset played via the video_player plugin, crossfading into and out of the live scenes for maximum polish. (2) Skip path — a scripted quick crossfade with the 3-2-1 overlay, used once the player has seen the full cinematic (same skip pattern as Victory Skip). Because the cinematic is generic and shared, it ships as one small asset regardless of theater or fortress.

Star Rating Criteria

Baseline Star Formula (Server-Tunable Per Battle)

Star criteria are shown on the battle page (before starting) and during the Build phase as a collapsible panel. The player always knows what they're aiming for.

★ (1 Star): Victory — required castles enclosed. Completing the battle is always worth 1 star. Ships do not need to be destroyed.

★★ (2 Stars): Victory + ≥50% walls remaining + ≥50% earned time unused. Reward for efficient play.

★★★ (3 Stars): Victory + ≥75% walls remaining + ≥75% time unused + all cannons alive. Requires strong defense and time management. Should be easy to achieve in early campaigns and challenging but doable in later campaigns.

★★★! (Exceptional): Victory + ≥90% walls + ≥90% time + all cannons alive + no castle degradation during the entire battle. A total surprise — the player should never be able to predict it mid-battle. Target frequency: 1 per 15–20 battles for a skilled player. Server-tuned thresholds adjust per battle to maintain this frequency.

All thresholds are server-side tunable per battle. The baseline above is the default. The server can tighten or loosen criteria based on aggregate player data to maintain the target difficulty curve and ★★★! frequency.

Competitive Analysis

Market Position — The Chess of Tower Defense

Rampart (1990, Atari): The inspiration. Arcade build-and-defend with tetromino walls. Rivenkeep inherits the core loop but adds: deterministic puzzles, 10 theaters, 198 piece shapes, boss encounters, and modern mobile UX.

Kingdom Two Crowns (2018): Build-and-defend, 2D side-view, real-time. Similar strategic planning but different spatial dimension (horizontal vs top-down). Rivenkeep's top-down → 2.5D transition is unique.

Clash Royale (2016): Real-time mobile combat, portrait mode, PvP. Similar intensity but multiplayer-focused. Rivenkeep is single-player deterministic — the puzzle is the opponent, not another human.

Tetris (1984): The spatial puzzle under time pressure archetype. Rivenkeep extends this into defensive architecture — pieces aren't just placed, they protect something.

Plants vs Zombies (2009): Tower defense with humor and personality. Lane-based, not grid-based. Rivenkeep offers deeper spatial complexity with full 2D grid enclosure.

No direct competitor combines all three: spatial puzzle (Tetris-style piece placement) + real-time combat (tower defense targeting) + deterministic replay (chess-like puzzle solving). Rivenkeep occupies a unique market position as the "strategic fortress defense puzzle game."

Layer 3 — Client-Side Architecture

The app accesses all backend services through a thin Dart service layer. No direct plugin/Firebase calls from game logic — all backend interaction goes through service classes (abstract interfaces with concrete implementations), so the Core/ logic layer stays pure and testable.

Art Direction

Riquochet Studios — 2.5D Isometric Castle Defense

Studio: Riquochet Studios (sole proprietorship).

Target style: Clean, semi-realistic 2.5D isometric — inspired by Age of Empires II's tilt-shift miniature aesthetic. Walls, castles, and cannons should feel like handcrafted tabletop game pieces — tactile, solid, with visible stone texture and subtle depth. The reference castle image establishes the tone: warm sandstone walls, slate-gray roofs, moss-covered rocky foundations. Not pixel art. Not flat vector. Not cartoonish. Miniature diorama realism — as if the battlefield is a beautifully built scale model.

2D vs 2.5D: Build and Deploy phases use flat top-down sprites (clean, readable, optimized for spatial puzzles). Fight phase uses isometric 2.5D pre-rendered sprites from the same 3D models (rendered offline into multi-angle sprite sheets; Flame composites them into the isometric battlefield). The transition from flat to isometric is the visual highlight of every sortie.

AI-generated assets: Art production relies heavily on AI generation with human curation. Sprites are generated at high resolution, then downscaled and cleaned for pixel-perfect rendering. Theater-specific skins are unique assets (not palette swaps) — each theater has its own wall texture, tree style, terrain, and atmosphere.

Sprite resolution targets: Walls at 24×24px per grid at default zoom (2D). Castles at 72×72px (3×3 grids). Cannons at 48×48px (2×2 grids). Ships at 24×48px (1×2) to 96×48px (2×4 carriers). All sprites have 4 rotation states for 2.5D view.

Accessibility

WCAG 2.1 AA for UI — Visual Game Acknowledged

Rivenkeep is an inherently visual spatial puzzle game. Full VoiceOver gameplay is impractical — the player must see the grid to place pieces. However, all non-gameplay UI must meet WCAG 2.1 AA:

VoiceOver: Accessible labels on all buttons, menus, navigation tabs, settings, store items, campaign cards, and battle page elements. VoiceOver reads: button names, star counts, campaign progress, Stonwryt balance, and all popup content. In-game: VoiceOver reads phase names, timer values, and sortie progress on request.

Reduced Motion: Setting that disables particle effects, shortens phase transition animations, replaces the 2D→2.5D camera transition with a fade, and reduces cannon fire visual intensity. Required by Apple's Human Interface Guidelines.

Dynamic Type: All non-gameplay text (menus, settings, store, campaign cards, popup text) scales with the system Dynamic Type setting. In-game grid text (labels, HP bars) uses fixed sizes for readability.

Colorblind modes: Already specified in Settings — Protanopia, Deuteranopia, Tritanopia adjustments. High Contrast mode increases border thickness and brightens text.
