What Deploy Is — and Why It Carries the Sortie

Build decides where you can fight from. Deploy decides what your fort will do without you. It is the only phase whose entire output is instructions to somebody else — you will not touch a gun in the Fight except to correct it, so everything your guns do for the next ninety seconds is written here.

The four things Deploy is
  • The commitment phase. Build gives you ground; the Fight gives you the verdict. Deploy is where a read of the fleet becomes a set of standing orders you then have to live with.
  • The highest-leverage seconds in the game. A Build mistake costs one wall. A Fight mistake costs one moment. A Deploy mistake — wrong ammunition, wrong ground, wrong stance — is wrong for the whole sortie, and only one slow, costed verb can reach in and fix it. The sortie is won or lost in Deploy; the Fight is where you find out.
  • The place the learning is spent. The Fight is where you learn the fleet. Deploy is where that knowledge cashes in. This is the mechanical home of the Edge of Tomorrow touchstone: you do not get stronger between attempts, you get better orders.
  • Defaults on orders, never on placement. The game will hand you a sensible AoR, a sensible domain, a sensible stance — scaffolding so you are never staring at a blank coverage map. It will never place a gun for you. Placement is the decision; orders are the scaffolding around it.

Scope: this doc owns the lived experience and the phase-level rules of Deploy — its tempo, its economy, its screen, its boundaries, its handover. It owns no combat mechanics. Group adjacency, AoR falloff, the three verbs, the nurture curve and the tower roster belong to the Cannon & Tower doc; walls, pieces, enclosure and triage geometry belong to the Build doc; the moment-to-moment of the sortie belongs to the Fight doc. Where a rule lives elsewhere it is restated here only far enough to be readable without leaving the page, and then linked.

The phase law this establishes

Because Deploy's leverage is high and its duration short, Deploy is the most dangerous clock in the game — the one place where a few seconds of hurry can decide ninety seconds of outcome. That is exactly the arcade line The Why §2 forbids crossing. So the Deploy clock is held to the same red line as Build's: if a sortie is ever lost because you could not finish stancing your groups in time, the timer is mistuned for that board. See §10, The Clock — it is the largest open number in this doc.

1The Deploy Beat — the Phase's Tempo

Like the sortie tempo in the Fight doc, Deploy has a beat structure. Naming it makes the phase checkable: every beat is either a read (free, no commitment) or an act (costs a slot, a bonus, or ground). A healthy Deploy is two reads, then two or three acts, then one read.

#BeatKindWhat happens
1HandoverreadThe Build wipe clears left-to-right (the mirror of Build's right-to-left entry, so the phase is recognisable at a glance). Walls are frozen; the piece rack is gone; the tray reloads as the cannon carousel + cannon trash. Same tray footprint, different contents — the swap is the phase transition.
2The state readreadWhat survived, what it cost. Every cannon shows its bar and its exact percentage (Cannon §8) — "that Standard is at 147%, that Piercer is at 40%." Fresh slots, if any, are announced. The next DMZ line shows as a ghost. This is the beat where a plan is chosen, before anything is touched.
3SitingactPlace new guns and towers; relocate or trash old ones. This is the phase's real decision and the only one with no default (§3). Every placement is simultaneously a coverage act, a grouping act, and a territorial act.
4OrdersactPer group, fill the order card: nudge the AoR, load the domain, set fire-control, order the tower stack. Fast, because every field arrives pre-filled with a sensible default — the work is editing, not authoring.
5The seam readreadStep back and look at the composite: where do the circles fail to reach, which fringe is naked, which approach is answered by the wrong ammunition. The coverage-holes toggle serves this beat. Optional, and the mark of a good player.
6Lock-in—Clock expires or the player commits. Lock-In 2 reads enclosure and move-distance into nurture; the DMZ advances; Map View crossfades to World View on the 3-2-1 camera tilt.
Why the read/act split matters

It is the same anti-plate-spinning promise the Fight doc makes, one phase earlier: the beats that cost you something are few and deliberate, and the beats that are free are where the thinking happens. A Deploy that degenerates into six rushed acts under a clock has become an execution test. A Deploy that is two long looks and three confident taps is the phase working.

2The Cannon Economy REBUILT — slots now derived from load

The economy is rebuilt, and the slot count is no longer a formula in campaign number — it is derived from the load the board actually fields. The upstream input (peak simultaneous contacts per approach) and the throughput model it feeds are owned by Victory §3 and Cannon §5. This section owns what Deploy does with the answer: how many slots you get, how they grow, and the frictions around filling them.

Why the old numbers died. They were inherited from the pre-AoR design and this doc adopted them because they were homeless, not because they were still right. They are not still right: the defender's model changed from targeting to allocation, and that changes how many ships a battle needs, which changes how many guns, which changes how much wall, which changes every phase timer. This section now holds the derivation and the open input, and refuses to state numbers it cannot yet derive.

The model change underneath it

Under the superseded model the player tapped a ship to focus fire. One hull was a real engagement, because engaging it was an act. Under the current model guns auto-fire into a zone, and the player's only lever is where the zones are and what they are loaded with. That is an allocation problem — and allocation problems do not exist below a certain population. Two ships cannot pose a coverage dilemma; twelve can. Three independent parts of the design now converge on the same conclusion:

  • Coverage-vs-concentration needs a crowd. The whole tension in Cannon & Tower §3 — a few big circles versus a line of small ones, scalloped gaps along a coastline, density versus fit — is only a decision when there are enough simultaneous contacts that you cannot cover everything. Under-population makes every layout correct, which makes none of them interesting.
  • Resolve needs a population to propagate through. Fleet Memory's resolve network, fear contagion, and cascade tipping fractions are statistical mechanics. A cascade across three hulls is not a cascade; the intensity dial has nothing to act on. The fleet's mind was designed at a scale the old ship counts do not reach.
  • Pressure comes from concurrency, not attrition. Ships retreat to spawn and re-emerge smarter, and the pool is drained by performance rather than by kills. So the fleet is not a stack of HP you grind down — it is a rate of arrival you must survive. Rate of arrival is the design input, and it has to be high enough to matter.

The dependency chain — one input, four number-sets

This is the structural finding, and it is the reason this section cannot be patched in isolation. The four number-sets that looked independent are one derivation with a single upstream input:

simultaneous contacts → cannons needed → courtyards & wall needed → Build / Deploy / Fight timers

The bracket the sizing session must work inside

Ship count has a floor set by allocation and a ceiling set by legibility, and the ceiling is the dangerous one. Below the floor, coverage stops being a decision. Above the ceiling, the board stops being readable — and The Why §6 names legibility-under-load as the single point of failure, the one loss that loses everything at once. Raising ship count loads that failure mode directly, and it also worsens a known problem: the board is already several times larger than the screen (128×80 as derived — Victory §3), so more hulls means more decision-critical events happening off-viewport (Fight §5). So the sizing session is run against legibility, not against difficulty. The right number is the smallest one that makes coverage a genuine allocation problem — not the largest one the fleet's mind could theoretically support.

What survives the rebuild — the shapes, minus the numbers

These are structural and independent of the count. They stand; only their magnitudes are open.

  • Slots, not purchases. You are granted capacity, you do not buy guns.
  • Slots grow both across the campaign and within a battle, earned by Fight performance. Defender progression inside a battle is what stops a long battle from being a war of attrition you are losing by default — and it matters more under higher ship counts, not less.
  • Unfilled slots roll over. Unspent capacity is capacity, not a loss.
  • A per-archetype type limit, expressed as a fraction of your slots rather than a flat cap — so it scales with whatever the count turns out to be. It exists to stop the strongest archetype filling every slot and collapsing the 8×3 matrix into one column. Red line: the cap must never be the reason a board is unbeatable.
  • Carousel, trash, recover. Full-width carousel with arrows, scrollable both directions, every unlocked type, progressive edge fade with a half-visible gun still selectable. Selection frame (not Build's magnify halo — the two trays must not blur), frame brightness distinguishing new from persisting, plus a swap icon carrying the same information by shape for colour-blind legibility. Tap the bin to view and recover anything trashed this sortie.
  • Changing a gun's type costs a sortie. Trash the old and the slot reopens next sortie, not this one. Deliberate friction: an archetype pivot should be a considered decision, and it is the counterweight that makes keeping a gun (and growing it into a veteran) worth something.
  • Invalid guns auto-trash visibly — a gun whose ground stopped being valid, or which the advancing DMZ overran. Visible so the loss is legible rather than silent.

The slot count — derived, not indexed new ·

The old formulas (start = min(2 + floor(cam/6), 7), max = min(start + sorties + floor(ships/5), 12)) were indexed on campaign number and pool size. Both are the wrong inputs: what a fort needs is set by the threat arriving at once on each bearing, not by how far into the War you are or how many hulls sit in reserve. The replacement:

The rule

For each live approach, the fort is granted the gun count n whose throughput reaches the designed supply ratio against that approach's peak contacts — and it is rounded down:

solve TKP(n) = S × contacts on this approach, then take ⌊n⌋
TKP(n) = 0.45·n1.5 · · S ≈ 0.55 under perfect allocation — derived from the locked star thresholds, not chosen (Victory §3)

Rounding down is the design, not an accident. Integer gun counts are coarse at these sizes, so the fort opens a notch below even its already-under-supplied target, and the in-battle earned slots carry it upward across the sorties. That gives the "slots grow within a battle by Fight performance" rule an actual job: it is the fort's own progression curve, walking you toward a supply level you never quite reach. You are always fighting slightly short — at the start of a battle most of all.

The fraction rule stands and is unchanged in shape: per-archetype cap = max(2, ⌈slots ÷ 3⌉) — "at most a third of your guns can be the same idea," with a floor of 2 so doubling up is always available. Against the table above that reads: 2 in the opening battles (i.e. unlimited in practice, which is correct for the on-ramp), 2 at mid-War, 3–4 late. Red line unchanged: the cap must never be the reason a board is unbeatable.

Contradiction resolved. Three incompatible type-limit rules were in circulation: this fraction rule, an archived "no cannon type limit — variety comes from encounter design," and an older max per type = floor(totalStars / unlock_threshold), cap 5. The fraction rule is canonical because it is the only one that scales with a slot count that is itself derived; the other two are superseded.

Honest status — what is settled and what is a target

Settled: slots are derived from fielded load, rounded down, grown in-battle, capped at 12 for a stated reason, and bounded by a fractional type limit. k₁ = 0.45 is now derived rather than guessed — twice over, from sources that share no quantity (Victory §3) — and S is a consequence of the locked star thresholds rather than a dial anyone chose, so neither is a free parameter this doc can drift. Both flags this section previously carried upstream are now closed: AoR area scaling is ruled linear, fixing TKP's exponent at 1.5, and the sortie-count normalisation is withdrawn, because the three star levers already police each other across a 3-to-10 sortie range. What remains open is the rebuild tax — which sets battle length and therefore how many slot-growth opportunities a battle offers — and the falloff rebuild, which feeds mean hit probability into k₁'s bottom-up route. Either would move the integers in the table above. Neither would change the shape, which is that the fort's gun count is a function of the wave, in that direction and not the reverse.

Confirmed goal — the first couple of battles are deliberately simplified

Ruling: the opening battles are simplified so the player is not overwhelmed. That is a design intent, not a concession, and it resolves the old "thin early Deploy" worry by reframing it — an early Deploy with two guns, one group and one domain is not a broken phase, it is a small one, on purpose.

The rebuilt economy sharpens why this matters. If coverage only becomes an allocation decision once there is a crowd to allocate against, then the early game — which cannot open with a crowd, since Introduce → Isolate → Combine forbids it — is a stretch where the core tension structurally does not exist yet. That is fine so long as it is designed as an on-ramp rather than happening by accident: the early battles teach placement, reading the ground, and the order card one field at a time, and the allocation problem arrives at the same time as the fleet that creates it. The proposed shape — introduce one order field per early battle: placement, then the AoR nudge, then domain, then fire-control, then the tower stack — is now confirmed by the derived slot table above — the opening battles field one approach, 2–3 contacts and 2 slots, so allocation genuinely does not exist yet and the order-field ladder is what fills the phase in the meantime. The ladder itself belongs to Campaign's toolkit teaching order.

3Placement — the Physical Act, and Its Three Consequences

Siting a gun is the only Deploy decision with no default, and it is three decisions wearing one gesture. Where a gun sits decides what it covers, what group it belongs to, and what ground you hold. Players will feel the first immediately, the second late, and the third almost never unless the interface says so.

The one terrain rule

A cannon or tower is a 2×2 footprint needing solid open ground. It cannot sit on a fragile grid (ice, crust, crag, bog, fissure, fract) — the ground will not bear the weight and recoil. This is a property of the terrain, not the gun: no archetype is ever locked out of a board, some ground just cannot hold a gun. It is the only placement constraint (Cannon §3). Invalid targets render as the universal red-blocks-with-X, shared with walls and spackle so the "can't go there" language is one language everywhere.

A gun holds ground — it does not cure it corrected v1.1.0

A cannon suppresses what is under and around it only for as long as it stands there. A fragile grid beneath a gun is held stable and the DMZ is pinned at its line — and the instant the gun moves or is destroyed, both resume: the ground goes fragile again, the line starts advancing again. There is no permanent terraforming; an earlier draft of this section wrongly claimed there was. Two reasons the correction is right beyond being the ruling. First, it makes the forward gun an ongoing garrison commitment rather than a one-time free upgrade — you are not buying ground, you are occupying it, and the moment you need that gun elsewhere you give the ground back. Second, permanent terrain-curing is already somebody else's job: the Pioneer is the land-lineage deep cut precisely because it can bridge and stabilise the fragile ground your cannons cannot stand on. Handing the same power to a cannon would have flattened an enemy's entire identity.

The forward gun is a stake in the ground

The DMZ advances after Deploy, not after Build — and cannons and castles hold it back permanently. A gun placed forward is therefore a territorial act: it pins the line where it stands, protecting everything behind it from the squeeze for the rest of the battle.

That gives placement a third axis with a clean, honest tension:

So the forward gun trades growth and safety for ground and reach. Neither is dominant, both are legible, and the choice re-poses the game's central coverage-versus-concentration question one layer down — which is the signature this design keeps returning to.

Holding position — a bonus for having placed well, not a tax for moving polarity corrected v1.2.0

Read it the right way round. An earlier draft called this a "move tax," which inverted it. It is a bonus for having sited a gun correctly in the previous sortie — you placed it where it turned out to belong, so it stays, and it grows. The "unmoved" bonus decays with Manhattan distance from its start-of-Deploy position (+5% unmoved, sliding to +0% at 9 or more grids — the table lives in Cannon §8); the in-world reason is that the Stonewrights spend the between-sortie hours hauling instead of improving. A gun that never needed moving is a gun you read the ground correctly for, and the bonus is the reward for that read. This applies to physical relocation only — nudging an AoR is always free (§6), and the Fight's AoR crawl never touches nurture. Nor is the foregone bonus the only consequence: because a gun holds ground only while it stands there, moving it also releases the DMZ and un-stabilises the fragile grid beneath it. So relocating has three consequences at once — the bonus you forgo, the coverage you lose during the change, and the ground you give back. The forward gun's real cost is that it is pinned by its own job.

The friction is the point — cannons are placed assets, not draggable units

An earlier draft flagged this as a risk — "it taxes the exact act of folding in a lesson" — with a red line saying that if players stopped re-siting, the penalty should be cut to zero. That was wrong on both counts, and the red line is withdrawn. The friction is doing deliberate work: without a cost, guns become units you shuffle every sortie, coverage becomes something you redraw rather than commit to, and the whole "Deploy is the plan you live with" premise dissolves. A gun you can move for free is not sited, it is parked.

The correct reading of the cluster: holding position is rewarded; moving is permitted and consequential. Nothing prevents a re-site — you can always answer a lesson — you simply do not collect the good-placement bonus on a gun you had to move, and you hand back the ground it was holding. A real decision with a legible price on both sides. And it produces the right incentive: the player who read the board correctly in sortie 1 is quietly ahead of the player who is still finding out, which is exactly how a game about reading ground should reward reading ground.

4Grouping Is Emergent, Not Declared

There is no "create group" button, and there never will be. Two guns that share at least one cell-edge are a group; guns touching only at a corner are not, until a third bridges them (Cannon §3). Grouping is read off the geometry of your fort — which means the player authors their command structure with the shape of their walls, one phase before the guns arrive. That is a designed ordering, not a trap: see the box below.

The Build↔Deploy seam — enclosure geometry is command granularity

This is the load-bearing consequence, and it is not written anywhere else. Build's courtyard of coverage rule says every enclosure needs an interior big enough to seat its guns. Grouping-by-adjacency says guns seated in the same courtyard become one group. Therefore: a player who wants two independently-commanded zones — Sea here, Air there — must build two separated seats to hold them. One large courtyard buys density and one tower buffing many guns; two small courtyards buy independent domains and independent AoRs. The Build triage puzzle was always a coverage puzzle; it is also a command-structure puzzle. That is the same principle expressed one layer up, and it should be taught explicitly in a Build drill (Mini-Games) — not because the player is being ambushed, but because it is the highest-value thing in Build that is not visible on its surface.

Walls come first, and you have enough to build them with corrected v1.1.0

An earlier draft treated "you author your command structure before you know the fleet" as a problem. It is not. By the time you are placing walls you already know where the DMZ is, where the spawn points are (always visible, every sortie, exempt from both fogs), what the terrain lets anything do — the fragile ground, the cliffs, the water — and therefore where an attacker can come from and where it wants to get to. You also know which guns you will be placing: your slot count and your unlocked archetypes are settled before Build opens. That is the whole input set a wall plan needs. Building walls before siting cannons is the correct order and always has been — you are not guessing, you are reading the ground, and the read is available. What you do not know is the fleet's mind — which approach it commits to, what it has learned, what it is loaded to punish — and that is exactly the knowledge the Fight teaches and the next Deploy spends (§7).

The requirement this creates: Build must show the player their cannon slate — slot count and available archetypes — on screen during Build, not just in Deploy. A wall plan seating four guns is a different plan from one seating seven, and a player cannot make the courtyard decision this section describes without knowing which they are building for. Owned by the Build doc; flagged here because Deploy is where the consequence lands.

The group outline — how a group tells you it exists new v1.1.0

Grouping is invisible unless the fort renders it, so it renders it directly. Every cannon carries a coloured outline around its own 2×2 footprint. When two cannons come to share a cell-edge, their separate outlines resolve into one outline tracing the perimeter of the whole group — the union of every footprint in it, drawn as a single unbroken boundary. A group is therefore not a label or a menu state; it is a shape you can see. And it composes: a third gun bridging two diagonals collapses three outlines into one, a tower bridging two groups does the same, and losing a gun in the Fight redraws the perimeter around what is left.

The AoR changes at the same instant, and that is half the signal. A lone cannon's zone is a lone cannon's zone; the moment it joins, the outline merges and the AoR re-forms as the group's larger zone (or, for a Lone Wolf pairing, visibly tightens instead of growing). Two channels move together at the moment of grouping — a perimeter and a coverage circle — which is what makes the event impossible to miss without either channel having to shout. It also means the player learns the adjacency rule by watching it happen rather than by being told it.

This supersedes an earlier draft's JOIN / FUSE / NEW text labels. The outline is strictly better: it is diegetic, it needs no reading, it carries the shape of the consequence rather than a word for it, and it works identically in the Fight where text would be noise. Words in a corner were a worse answer to the same problem. (Canonical render spec lives in Cannon & Tower §3, which owns grouping mechanics; Deploy owns the pre-commit preview below.)

Fusion — intentional, flagged, and undoable new v1.2.0

Fusing two groups is a real play, not just a hazard. A bridging cannon converts two medium zones into one large one with more barrels and a bigger AoR, and one tower then buffs the whole thing — sometimes exactly what a board wants. So fusion is a tool the player reaches for deliberately, and the design's job is not to prevent it but to make it unmistakable and reversible.

Unmistakable is the outline plus the AoR change (above): the perimeter redraws around all of it and the coverage circle re-forms, two channels moving at once, so nothing fuses quietly. The pre-commit version is simply that same outline shown early:

  • While dragging, the ghost carries its own outline, and any outline it would merge with previews the merged perimeter — the boundary you would end up with, drawn before you commit.
  • The merged AoR previews with it, so the player sees the single zone they would get — including the fact that it now loads one domain.
  • Towers are covered by the same preview, including grayed-out backups. Physical touch merges groups whether or not a tower is active, and that is the least intuitive rule in the system — so it is the one that most needs the perimeter drawn for it.

Committing is therefore always informed. This is a legibility requirement, not a nicety: emergent grouping is elegant precisely because it removes a menu, and it only stays elegant while the geometry it depends on is drawn.

The fusion undo — one level, tied to the bridging gun

Reversible is a short, specific memory rule. When a placement fuses two or more groups, Deploy remembers the AoRs those groups had.

  • Remove that same bridging cannon → the previous groups come back with their previous AoRs restored. Not the spread-default, not a blank zone: the exact coverage you had built before the fusion. Trying a fusion is therefore free, which is what makes it something a player will actually experiment with.
  • Pick up any other cannon → the memory is discarded. The remembered AoRs are forgotten and, if the bridge is removed after that, the un-fused groups take fresh defaults. Once the player has started rearranging the fort around the fused shape, the old zones are no longer the plan they would want back.
  • One level, no stack. There is no fusion history to walk backwards through. The rule exists to make a single "let me see what that looks like" free, not to provide general undo.
  • Deploy-scoped. The memory does not survive the phase — the Fight has no placement, so there is nothing for it to reverse. Lock-in discards it.
Why "the bridging cannon specifically" is the right hinge

It ties the undo to the gesture that caused it, which is the only version of this a player can predict without being taught. Removing the gun you just placed obviously means "not that"; picking up a different gun obviously means "I am now doing something else." Neither needs a button, a prompt, or a rule in a manual — and both match the forgiveness-before-commitment language Build already uses. It also makes the accidental case and the experimental case the same case, handled by one rule: the accidental fuse is now cheap to notice (the outline moved) and cheap to reverse (put the gun back in the tray), which is a better outcome than trying to design the accident out.

5The Order Card — What Deploy Actually Outputs

Selecting a group in Deploy raises its order card: four fields, all pre-filled, all editable. This is the phase's entire product. Everything else — the slots, the siting, the geometry — exists to make these four fields answerable.

The controls are the same controls — Deploy teaches the Fight

The AoR drag, the domain slider and the fire-control toggle on this card are the identical widgets the three Fight verbs use. Nothing new appears when the countdown ends; the only thing that changes is that they now cost something. That is a real teaching win and it was free: the player learns the Fight's entire control surface unhurried, with no enemy on the board, every single sortie. It also explains the verb budget from the other side — there are three live verbs because there are three live order fields, and the fourth field (tower priority) is deliberately set-and-forget rather than a fourth verb. Do not add a fifth field.

Command is always of one group. There is no group-of-groups, no multi-select, no "all groups load Sea" — you select and order one group at a time, in Deploy exactly as in the Fight. With auto-assigned defaults doing most of the work, that stays comfortable rather than laborious, and it keeps a fort of six groups from becoming a spreadsheet.

6The Auto-AoR Default and the Nudge

The rule is you nudge an AoR, you never draw one. Every gun and group receives a sensible zone the instant it exists, so the player's task is editing a decent plan rather than authoring coverage from a blank map. Specifying how the default is chosen is this doc's job — it was the item parked out of the Fight work.

How the default is assigned simplified v1.2.0

The rule is deliberately dumb, and that is the design: "somewhere out there, and differentiated from the other AoRs." The default points the zone outward — toward the ground an attacker has to cross — and its only cleverness is not duplicating what another group already covers. That is the whole rule.

  1. Radius from the group's cannon count and its archetypes' AoR contributions (a Lone Wolf shrinks a mixed group's zone; two Lone Wolves tighten accuracy instead of growing area). Mechanics in Cannon §3.
  2. Facing: outward from the group, into the contested ground — the DMZ side, where anything hostile has to come from. Not aimed at a particular corridor, spawn, or predicted lane. Just: out there.
  3. Differentiation: if another group already covers where this one would land, push it off to cover different ground. Groups spread rather than stack. This is the only computation the default performs.
  4. Two sanity clamps: the group's own footprint falls inside its zone (a gun always covers itself), and the punched-out launch-zone dead hole may not swallow the majority of the zone — no default points mostly at ground that cannot be fired on.
  5. Ties break on grid index, deterministically. Every default must be reproducible from the battle seed or replays and leaderboards stop being exact.
Why the dumb default is the right default — it closes the risk by construction

An earlier draft had the default derive its centre from the nearest uncovered approach corridor, and bias toward overlapping the weakest seam when everything was covered. That was clever and wrong twice over. It made the default an opinion about the fleet, which is the player's job. And the seam-overlap bias had the game teaching the overlap technique by performing it — worse than not teaching it at all, because overlap-versus-spread is one of the genuine coverage decisions.

"Somewhere out there, spread apart" is better precisely because it is obviously not optimal. It guarantees the player never faces a blank map and never has a gun pointed into its own courtyard, and it guarantees every board still has coverage work left to do. That retires the standing worry that a good default eats the decision: a default knowing only "outward, and not where the others are" cannot eat a decision it has no information about. The gap between a defaulted fort and a read fort is now the entire read, which is where the value belongs.

The nudge

  • Drag the zone. Soft-lock holds until you lift, so a careful placement is possible; unlike the Fight, the zone arrives instantly — there is no crawl, no degraded fire, no cost. The crawl exists to make live re-tasking a real commitment; in the planning phase it would only be friction.
  • Snap hints at the two positions that matter: centred on an approach corridor, and centred on a seam between two existing zones. Hints, not rails — free placement anywhere is always allowed.
  • Nudging never touches nurture. Only physical relocation of the gun does (§3).
What the default knows, and what it never will

It knows two things: the shape of your own fort, and where your other zones are. It knows nothing about the fleet — which approach the flagship commits to, what arrives first, what a wave is loaded to punish, what it learned last sortie. Those four things are what actually decide where a zone should sit, and all four are knowledge the Fight teaches and the next Deploy spends (§7). So a defaulted fort is playable and a read fort is better — permanently, by construction rather than by tuning.

7Information State — Deploy Gets Smarter Across a Battle

Deploy's decision load rises across a battle (more slots, more groups, more fields) while its uncertainty falls (you have watched the fleet). Those two curves running opposite is what makes the phase feel like it is growing with you rather than piling up.

WhenWhat you knowWhat Deploy is
Sortie 1Terrain, spawn points, the DMZ line. Nothing about the fleet — it has not emerged, and your own fort sits in mind-fog twilight.A hypothesis from geometry. Where can they come from, and what does the ground let me do about it?
Sortie 2+Which lanes were used, which hulls came, what your fringes missed, which wall they taxed.A revision. Same fort, corrected orders — and the first place the cost of changing your mind is felt.
Replaying the battleEverything. The battle is deterministic and the battle is the checkpoint, so the whole sequence is known.A rehearsal. This is where determinism pays out and where the touchstone lands: you are not stronger, your orders are better.
The consequence for the campaign

If Deploy is where knowledge is spent, then a board whose crises can all be answered by one Deploy configuration has spent nothing — the second attempt is the first attempt typed faster. The ≥2-solutions law that governs Build triage therefore has a Deploy twin: a board should admit more than one viable deployment, not just more than one viable wall layout. Owned by Campaign and Build triage; noted here because Deploy is where the failure would be felt.

8The Deploy Read — What Is on the Screen

Deploy runs in Map View: the flat, top-down, colour-coded WWII command-table rendering shared with Build. The 2.5D World View belongs to the Fight. That split is doing real work — the planning phases are a map you reason over, the Fight is a place you watch — and the crossfade between them is the clearest phase signal in the game.

The layers

  • Health, fully exposed. Every cannon shows its bar and its exact percentage ("147%"), with the light-green overlay for veteran surplus. The Fight hides bars until first damage to keep the board quiet; Deploy does the opposite, because this is where protect-and-site decisions get made and the numbers are the decision.
  • Coverage, in the same three-state grammar as the Fight, minus one state. Nothing is firing, so there is no firing state: all groups show resting grid-square outlines, and the selected group shows its full centre-to-edge gradient with its bright core and its punched-out launch-zone hole. Same language, one phase earlier — no new render vocabulary to learn.
  • The DMZ ghost. Because the DMZ advances the moment Deploy ends, the next line renders as a ghost throughout. This is a fairness requirement, not a convenience: without it, a forward placement is a hidden trap, and a gun auto-trashed by an advance the player could not see would be exactly the unattributable loss determinism forbids.
  • Pending nurture, on the card. Each selected gun's card shows the bonus it is currently on track to receive at lock-in — "+6% enclosed, +3% moved 3" — so the enclosure-versus-relocation tradeoff is legible while being made rather than discovered afterwards. Closing this gap is the difference between nurture being a system the player plays and a number that happens to them.

The coverage-holes toggle

One tap dims the board and paints only the uncovered approach ground — the corridors between visible spawns and your fort that no AoR reaches. It serves the seam-read beat, and it is safe here in a way it never could be in the Fight, because nothing is moving and no reflex is involved.

Red line on the toggle — it shows holes, it must never grade the plan

The toggle answers "where does my coverage not reach," which the player could work out by squinting at circles. It must never answer "is my plan good" — no score, no checkmark, no "3 gaps remaining," no highlighting of the hole that matters. The moment it ranks or approves, Deploy stops being a judgement about an unknown fleet and becomes a checklist to clear, and a cleared checklist is the definition of the player having stopped thinking (The Why §2). Coverage holes are not mistakes — a deliberate hole on the approach you have read as quiet is often the correct plan.

9What's Locked — the Hard Boundaries Both Ways

The three phases stay distinct because each one forbids the others' verbs. Stating the boundary from Deploy's side, as the Fight doc does from its own:

In Deploy you CANIn Deploy you CANNOT
Place cannons and towers into open slotsPlace, rotate, trash or store pieces; use spackle; repair or restack walls — all Build
Physically relocate a placed gun (forgoing its hold-position bonus and releasing the ground it held)Draw a fresh piece or touch the rack
Trash a persisting gun (slot reopens next sortie)Fire, target, or engage anything — there is no enemy on the board
Form, split and fuse groups implicitly, by where you siteForm, split or fuse groups in the Fight — grouping is Deploy-only, and you live with your plan
Set all four order-card fields freely and instantlyRe-order the tower stack in the Fight — it is locked for the sortie
And the rule that holds the phase's identity

There is no auto-deploy. The game defaults orders and never placement — not on the first sortie, not on a replay, not as an accessibility convenience. Deploy is a learning phase; a fort that arms itself teaches nothing and removes the one decision the phase exists to pose. The defaults are scaffolding around the decision, which is why they can be generous without being a slippery slope.

10Lock-In and the Handover to the Fight

Deploy ends on the clock or on the player's commit, and four things happen in a fixed order. The order matters, because two of them can invalidate the third.

  1. Lock-In 2 fires. Nurture reads the board as it now stands: current enclosure after Build's repairs and Deploy's moves (+6% enclosed with castle, +4% without, +1% exposed), plus the distance-decayed unmoved bonus. All bonuses stack, capped at 200%. This is the second of the sortie cycle's two windows — the first fired pre-Build, rewarding how each gun finished the last Fight. Full table in Cannon §8.
  2. The DMZ advances one step (except on Coastal, River and Swamp), held back permanently wherever a cannon or castle stands. Anything overrun is auto-trashed, visibly, into the bin.
  3. The board wipes left-to-right — the mirror of Build's entry, so the direction alone identifies which way through the loop you are travelling.
  4. Map View crossfades to World View as the 3-2-1-FIGHT countdown plays out as an animated camera tilt from flat top-down into 2.5D isometric. The plan becomes a place. Then the assembly window opens and the Fight's tempo takes over.
Why lock-in runs before the DMZ advance

Nurture reads the fort you built and ordered, not the fort the map took a bite out of a second later. A gun that was enclosed when you committed keeps its enclosure bonus even if the advancing line strips a wall behind it. The player is scored on the plan they made with the information they had — the same principle as the DMZ ghost in §8, applied to the reward side.

11The Clock RESOLVED — load-scaled, and the contradiction was worse than recorded

Deploy is timed, and the clock is now derived. The headline is not the number: it is that the contradiction in the record was hiding a second, larger one. Both figures on file are void, and so is the model that produced the better of them. This section surfaces all three, then derives the replacement — and explains why Deploy is the one phase whose clock is a formula while the other two are flat numbers.

The contradiction, all three layers of it

Layer 1 — the one that was on file. An earlier pass set Deploy to 20→30 s on explicit human factors: 5–8 s of board recognition plus 3–6 s per cannon. A later consistency sweep normalised it to 12–15 s to match a phase-timing table, without re-deriving anything. The sweep's figure does not survive its own source model — at 7 slots that model gives 26–50 s — so 12–15 s is a table artifact and void. That much was already recorded.

Layer 2 — the 20→30 s figure is void too, on the same arithmetic. It never covered its own model either. 5–8 s + (3–6 s × 7 slots) = 26–50 s, and against the derived slot table (§2), which now reaches 12 rather than 7, the same model gives 5–8 + 36–72 = 41–80 s. So the record held two numbers and neither was consistent with the reasoning behind one of them. The pre-existing note treated 20→30 s as the survivor; it is not.

Layer 3 — and the model itself is void, which is the real finding. "Seconds per cannon" was the right unit for a design where Deploy meant placing every gun, every phase. It is the wrong unit now, by a large factor. Under the current design (§1, §4, §6) Deploy places only the new guns — the rest are already standing and already seated — and it edits one order card per group, pre-filled with defaults. A late fort is 12 guns in 3 groups, and adds roughly 0.4 guns per sortie. Charging per cannon prices a job the phase no longer does.

Worth naming the pattern, because it has now bitten three times in this doc set: the number was not wrong when written. The design under it moved — targeting to allocation, individual guns to groups, authoring to editing — and the number was carried forward by consistency sweeps that check figures against each other rather than against the mechanic. A figure re-derived is a figure re-tested; a figure normalised is only a figure made tidy.

THE DEPLOY CLOCK — load-scaled, because the job varies tenfold inside one battle

The replacement is Model A from the old options list, and the choice is forced rather than preferred. Build takes a flat clock and Deploy cannot, for a structural reason: Build's work is bounded above by a mandatory floor that grows smoothly across the campaign (the rebuild tax — Build §2, §11) and bounded below by the Ready button, so one number covers every case. Deploy's work has a one-time spike at sortie 1, when the entire opening slate is sited at once, and then collapses to near nothing. A flat clock would either starve sortie 1 or waste every Deploy after it.

Deploy = 5 s recognition  +  5 s × (new guns sited)  +  5 s × (commanded groups)   ·   floor 15 s

Every term, and where it comes from. All three constants are taken at the harsh end of their evidence, per the translation law — for a clock, harsh means short.

TermValueDerivation
Recognition5 sBeat 2, the state read (§1). The measured band is 5–8 s; the short end is taken. Sortie 1 has less to read (no damage, no survivors) and later sorties have more, but the read is the same act and the difference sits inside the band.
Per new gun sited5 sArchetype choice off the carousel (2–3 s — a real decision among 8) plus placement (3–4 s). Bounded below by Build: the courtyard was already built for this gun, so siting is a constrained fit, not an open choice. Bounded above by the three consequences every placement carries — coverage, grouping, territory (§3) — plus the fusion preview and the DMZ read. 5–7 s; the short end is taken.
Per commanded group5 sOne order card, four fields, all pre-filled (§5): AoR nudge ~3 s (a drag, and the only field that genuinely wants a look), domain ~1 s, fire-control ~0.5 s, tower stack 0–1 s. This is an editing pass, which is exactly why the number is small — and it is small by design, not by luck: the defaults exist so the clock prices editing rather than authoring (§6).
Floor15 s= recognition + one group + one seam read. Beat 5 (§1) is optional and is the mark of a good player, so the floor's job is to guarantee that beat always physically exists. Without it, a late-battle Deploy with no new guns and one group would run 10 s and the phase would have no room to be played well.

What it produces. New guns per sortie come from the slot table in §2 — slots grow in-battle by Fight performance, so the count is the growth, not the total.

StageGroupsSortie 1 (whole slate)Sortie 2+ (growth + edits)
Cam 1–612 guns → 20 s0–1 guns → 15 s (floor)
Cam 7–221–25 guns → 40 s0–1 guns → 15–20 s
Cam 23–322–39 guns → 65 s0–1 guns → 20–25 s

The old 20→30 s figure was right about the wrong case. Steady-state late Deploy lands at 20–25 s — the band that figure named. It was void not because it was badly estimated but because it was estimated for every Deploy, and Deploy is two different jobs wearing one name.

The pacing consequence — a feature, and it has a shape

The old options list flagged that a load-scaled clock makes phase duration vary within a battle, "which may be a pacing feature or a pacing problem." It is a feature, and the reason is that it runs against the Build curve. Build is flat while its tax grows, so a Build gets tighter as the battle goes on. Deploy shrinks from 65 s to ~22 s over the same span. Net pre-Fight time per sortie:

Late battleBuildDeployPre-Fight total
Sortie 155 s65 s120 s — you are authoring a fort
Sortie 555 s22 s77 s — you are keeping one alive

So the battle accelerates: the planning burden front-loads onto the sortie where the plan is actually made, and the back half of a long battle is quick, tight, and mostly repair. That is the right shape for something that ends in a Last Stand, and it arrives without a pacing rule — it is two derived clocks pointing opposite directions.

The clock is legible, which is what makes a varying clock fair. The player can see what it is priced on before it starts: the cannon slate is on screen through the whole of Build (Build §5), so you know how many guns you are about to site, and you built the courtyards that decide how many groups they form. A varying clock you can predict is a budget; one you cannot is a surprise.

⚠ The consequence that lands upstream — the sortie-1 spike is a slot-table question

Surfacing rather than papering: a late sortie-1 Deploy is a 65 s phase, and it is the single longest non-Fight phase in the game. The formula is right; the input may not be. It falls out of §2's grant of 9 slots at sortie 1 in the late campaign, and 9 guns sited in one phase is a fort authored in one sitting rather than grown.

The alternative is not a clock change — it is a smaller opening grant with faster in-battle growth, which would flatten the spike, spread the siting decisions across the sorties that can afford them, and give "slots grow by Fight performance" more to do. That is §2's derivation to re-open, not this section's, and it is logged here as a cross-reference debt rather than acted on: the slot table is derived from throughput against peak contacts, and re-cutting it to smooth a clock would be fitting the fleet to the phase timer, which is the dependency chain backwards. Flagged for whoever next opens §2.

The red line, unchanged and now measurable

If a sortie is ever lost because the player could not finish stancing their groups in time, the timer is mistuned for that board. Word-for-word the sibling of Build's timer law, and it matters most here: Deploy has the highest leverage-per-second in the game (see the opening section), so a tight Deploy clock is the closest this design comes to the arcade test The Why §2 forbids. Deploy may create urgency; it may never create hurry.

What is new is that the law is now checkable rather than aspirational. Every term in the formula is a priced act, so the clock cannot be short of the work by construction — and the floor guarantees the optional read beat survives even in the emptiest Deploy. The failure mode to watch in prototype is not the total: it is the per-gun constant. If siting a gun on a 128×80 board with pan costs more than 5 s in practice, the sortie-1 spike is worse than 65 s and the slot-table flag above becomes urgent rather than logged.

Retired options — for the record

The three candidate models are resolved rather than carried. A · Load-scaled — adopted, above, with the pacing worry answered. B · Untimed — rejected: it breaks the rollover economy (Victory §5), which is the only mechanism converting saved time into score, and the hazard was never a clock — it was a tight clock. C · Idle-bounded — rejected, but for a better reason than opacity: an extending timer prices activity, and the phase this doc describes is two long reads and three confident taps. A clock that rewards touching things would fight beat 5 directly, which is the beat the floor exists to protect.

Honest status of this doc

A complete first cut of the phase layer, with one section deliberately unfinished. What it settles: the auto-AoR default-assignment rule (previously parked out of the Fight work); the group outline as the render that makes emergent grouping visible, with the AoR change as its second channel; emergent grouping as the Build↔Deploy seam — courtyard geometry decides command granularity, decided in Build from information the player genuinely has; the forward gun as a held territorial commitment (pins the DMZ and stabilises fragile ground while it stands there, releasing both when it moves); the order card and its widget-sharing with the Fight verbs; lock-in ordering. Three legibility gaps closed: the merged-perimeter preview, the DMZ ghost, the pending-nurture readout.

What it does not settle, on purpose: every number in the cannon economy, and all three phase timers. Both are downstream of one open input — how many ships a battle fields — and the old figures were set under a defender model that no longer exists. Inventing replacements would have been worse than the hole: they would have looked settled and been built on. What this doc contributes there is the derivation order, which is the thing that was actually missing. Nearly everything about how the phase feels remains a written target awaiting prototype.
