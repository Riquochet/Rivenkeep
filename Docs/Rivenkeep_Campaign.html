1What This Doc Is

The campaign-layout module. It does not invent the campaign — the GDD already carries the full skeleton (Introduce→Isolate→Combine, the five-layer difficulty matrix across 32 campaigns, the castle-triage ramp, ten theaters, the 190-battle generator, the boss finale). This doc meshes that skeleton with the two things it predates: the blind-mind cognition model (Fleet Memory) and the locked cast (the paired-down 8 cannons + 7 towers and the lineage×axis roster).

Relationship to the other docs

The GDD keeps the full 190-battle generator until it is migrated here — nothing is lost. This module states the campaign design: the difficulty model, the cognition ramp, the roster mapping, the toolkit teaching order, and the foundational tensions the layout must respect. Where it contradicts older GDD text on how enemies get harder, this doc supersedes it.

The one goal everything here serves

Rivenkeep is a thinking game: strategy is the fun. Every layout choice below is judged by a single test — does it keep the player making interesting decisions against a mind, rather than executing a memorised optimisation against a number? That test, not "is it hard," governs the campaign.

2The Inherited Skeleton from the GDD — kept whole

The GDD's campaign structure is strong and stays intact. In brief:

Introduce → Isolate → Combine

No battle ever asks the player to handle more than one new thing at a time. New mechanic → familiar terrain and enemies; new enemy → terrain off. Challenge comes from combining things already understood, never from confusion — the player must always understand why they lost. This teaching law now governs the toolkit too (§6).

Theaters are variety; layers are difficulty

Ten theaters (Coastal, River, Swamp, Forest, Mountain, Volcanic, Desert, Frozen, Sky, Crystal) are freshness — scenery and feel, never punishment. Difficulty comes from five named layers, mixed independently of theater.

LayerWhat it dials
A · TerrainThe theater's environmental mechanic (fire, DMZ advance, sandstorm, lava, ice, fog, decay, bounce).
B · Advanced enemiesBeyond the basics — the roster's harder lineages. Reframed below into breadth (new lineages) + depth (sharper minds).
C · Piece complexityWall-piece pool escalation across 16 tiers (A-series → H-series).
D · Weather & eventsFog, storms, wind, night, monsoon, eruptions.
E · TerritoryEnclosure gets spatially harder — castles spread, walls pre-damaged, obstacles crowd the castles.
The combination matrix — 32 campaigns

The five layers are introduced singly, then every combination is explored: 1 base + 5 singles + 10 pairs + 10 triples + 5 quads + 1 full = 32 campaigns, then the boss finale. Each campaign is a unique layer-combination teaching a distinct interaction. Alongside it, two monotonic ramps rise with campaign number — castle triage (1/4 → 9/10 → 4/4 boss; more required, fewer spares) and piece tier (1 → 16). Difficulty knobs that need no new rules — just tighter margins on decisions the player already makes.

Determinism — the puzzle has a solution

Every battle is deterministic: same spawns, same terrain, same destinations, for everyone. Retrying is learning, not grinding; leaderboards are fair; a solution exists. This is the bedrock — and, as §7 argues, the sharpest double-edge in the whole design.

Boss finale — the ten Mystarchs

A separate mode after Campaign 32: ten boss battles (one per theater — Leviathan, Colossus, Sandworm, Hydra, Magma Titan, Frost Wyrm, Storm King, Ancient Treant, Plague Herald, Crystal Lich), each a promoted flagship chassis dialled to max intensity, on a compact 4/4 map so the boss is the challenge, not the enclosure. Boss set-pieces are their own later step.

3The Difficulty Model, Reconciled supersedes older GDD text

The skeleton escalates the board. The cognition model escalates the enemy. They are orthogonal, and together they are the whole difficulty curve — but the enemy side is not a second parallel track bolted on. It has one axis, and it is the mind.

Two orthogonal difficulty families
  • Board difficulty — combinatorial. The five layers (A–E) combined, plus castle triage and piece tiers. This is situation complexity: what the terrain, weather, geography, and piece pool throw at you. The GDD owns this and it is excellent.
  • Enemy difficulty — cognitive. How well the fleet thinks — the quality of its estimator (fear-reading, sight, contact-verification, anticipation). This is the axis the GDD was missing, and the one that makes "harder later" without a stat treadmill.
Tougher hulls and stronger guns are PART OF the cognitive improvement — not a parallel track

The load-bearing correction, and it supersedes any GDD text implying enemies get harder by inflating HP or fielding faster, stronger guns as a difficulty lever in their own right. There is one enemy-improvement curve and it is led by the mind. A little more hull or a slightly quicker gun may ride inside that curve as minor seasoning — never as a separate, parallel escalation. The moment "the wave got tougher" means "the health bars got bigger" rather than "the fleet outsmarted my old plan," the thinking game has decayed into an arcade grind. Enemy strength is a subordinate component of enemy intelligence, full stop.

The ramp must aim at the TOP of every band, not the middle new ·

The Why now carries a named target for load: the game must feel real, and always just barely overwhelming — which resolves as sit at the top of every legible band, never the middle (Why §4). That governs this doc directly, because a difficulty ramp is precisely the machinery that decides where in a band the player ends up.

What it rules in and out:

  • The ramp’s destination is the measured ceiling, not a safe distance below it. Contacts must arrive at 8 and stay there — not treat 8 as an occasional late peak. Approaches must arrive at 3 and stay. A ramp that tops out at a comfortable 6 has underspent the band and made the late campaign slack.
  • Arriving early is worse than arriving late. The ramp still has to obey Introduce → Isolate → Combine and the one-screw-at-a-time law — so the correct shape is a ramp that reaches the ceiling as late as it can and then holds it, rather than one that reaches a lower plateau sooner. Late arrival at the top beats early arrival at the middle.
  • “Room to grow” is not a virtue here. Any headroom deliberately left in the final campaigns is slack the player will feel as the game running out of things to say. The last campaigns should be at the boundary of what a person can read.
  • It does NOT license raising the ceilings. The bands are measured (tracking research, Rampart, the enclosure geometry) and the law says sit at the top of them — not above them. Pushing past a measured ceiling is the failure this doc’s own subtle but observable constraint forbids, and it is the difference between overwhelmed-and-reading and overwhelmed-and-guessing.

Note the direction this cuts against the instinct of a ramp designer. The natural habit is to leave a margin so the game never feels punishing; this law says the margin is the bug. The protection against unfairness is not headroom — it is the three red lines that already bind every board: no board punishes a player for not watching a contact that gave no reason to be watched, every loss is attributable, and no board is winnable only by placing fast.

Why this is the whole point

Escalating by HP/DPS converts strategy into optimisation: find the damage build, repeat it forever. Escalating by cognition keeps the decision space alive at every level — you are always out-thinking a mind, never out-gunning a number. A plan that beat a naive fleet stops working against a sharper one not because the numbers grew but because the fleet no longer falls for it — so the player must think again. That is the fun, and it is why difficulty lives on the estimator.

4The Cognition Ramp — Fine, Continuous, Felt Not Seen

The enemy mind does not step up in tiers. It creeps — a little sharper each battle, across the whole War, so slowly the player rarely catches the exact moment it changed.

The experience we are designing for

The target feeling is not "a hard wall appeared." It is: "my usual opening isn't landing like it used to… why? What can I do differently?" The fleet that used to keep shelling the gun you relocated now probes the empty spot first. The scouts that never spotted your forward battery now do. The raiders that hit where fear was low now hit where you're about to move it. No banner, no tier-up — just a rising, quiet pressure to adapt. Frustration becomes curiosity, and curiosity is the engine of a thinking game.

How it rises

Cognition is a monotonic per-battle creep, not a combinatorial layer — it does not join A–E (that would wreck the clean 32-campaign math). It behaves like the castle-triage and piece-tier ramps: it advances with battle number, independent of which layers a campaign exercises. Early War (Campaigns 1–~8): naive estimators — short sight, fast fear-decay, trusts stale contacts, reacts to the board as it stands. Mid War (~9–~22): the estimator tightens — slower decay, longer sight, begins to verify. Late War (~23–32): the sharp forms — verification over trust, anticipation over reaction, threading seams it used to blunder. The named apexes from the roster (Farseer, Counter-Battery, Pathfinder, Nightstalker) are the far end of a smooth slope, not discrete unlocks.

The contact ramp — the third monotonic ramp new ·

Two monotonic ramps already rise with campaign number alongside the layer matrix: castle triage (1/4 → 9/10) and piece tier (1 → 16). Fleet sizing adds a third, and like the others it needs no new rules — only tighter margins on a decision the player already makes. It has two sub-dials, and separating them is the whole point:

The board-authoring law the corrected win condition adds

Victory is beating back the attack while maintaining the required enclosure count (Victory §2), so a board carries two independent clocks and both must be satisfiable. The sorties a board's fleet provides must exceed the Build phases its requirement demands, with margin for a lost ring. Too tight and the board is unwinnable regardless of skill; too loose and the back half is an unopposed construction exercise.

Practically, this couples two dials that were being ramped independently: the castle-triage ladder (which sets wall demand, and therefore the deadline) and queue depth (which sets sortie count, and therefore the time available). Raising the requirement without deepening the queue tightens the race; deepening the queue without raising the requirement loosens it. They must be ramped together — a new constraint on the 190-battle generator, and a checkable one, which is the useful kind. Sibling to the ≥2-solutions law.

⚠ CORRECTED — mean formation size RISES across the ramp, it does not fall v2.4.0

This doc previously stated that mean formation size falls across the ramp (≈4 hulls early, ≈3 late) even as hull count rises, on the reasoning that late fleets disperse and dispersal converts hulls into contacts. The measured composition says the opposite: mean hulls per formation is 4.3 / 4.6 / 5.3 across the three stages (Victory §4b). Late formations are bigger, because escort strength follows the real convoy curve and late fleets run several payloads behind one shared screen.

The old claim’s conclusion survives, though, and by a better route. The late War does feel sharper without anything getting bigger — but the mechanism is not shrinking formations. It is that the hull cap now binds before the contact cap, so the fleet physically cannot field its heavy convoys and a wide wave in the same sortie. That splits every late sortie into one of two kinds:

  • Payload sortie — narrow and heavy: 1–2 convoys plus a wolfpack or two, 3–4 contacts, 19–24 hulls, and every contact decision-bearing. The ≤4 law at its ceiling with nothing spare.
  • Preparation sortie — wide and thin: 6–8 wolfpacks, 6–8 contacts, 16–22 hulls, no payload out at all.

So the 8-contact destination is reached on preparation sorties — which is exactly when the player most needs to read wide, because there is no payload in the wave to anchor attention on. Dispersal still spends the attention budget as the old text said; it just does it by changing the kind of sortie rather than the size of every formation. This also makes the existing law “a wave may arrive wide and simple, or narrow and clever — never both” physical rather than authored: the hull cap enforces it with no rule required.

The two enemy counts are not the same dial and must not be ramped together. Contacts are the attention budget and are capped hard and low by human tracking; hulls are the population the fleet's mind runs on and can be several times that. A formation holding shape is one contact whatever its hull count, so raising hulls inside coherent formations costs the player nothing in legibility while giving resolve and fear more to work with. Raising contacts is what actually costs the player. The ceilings — 8 contacts (≤4 decision-bearing), 3 approaches — come from the legibility bound and not from a difficulty judgement, and 8 is where the campaign is meant to arrive rather than a line to stay behind; see Victory §3 and The Why §6.

The choreography law, extended to three screws

Build §5 already holds the law that piece-pressure (C) and cognition (E) never turn together — one screw per campaign, to protect the legibility ceiling. Sizing makes it three screws that must be turned singly: contacts, approaches, and cognition. A campaign that raises contacts per approach and opens a new approach and sharpens the estimator is not a hard campaign — it is an illegible one, and the player will not be able to name what changed. Introduce → Isolate → Combine, applied to the fleet's size as strictly as to its mechanics.

And a fourth screw that is not a screw. Hull count is deliberately excluded from the list, because it is not a pressure dial. A campaign may add hulls freely so long as they arrive inside formations that already exist — that raises the fleet's statistical richness without raising the player's tracking load. Hull count only becomes a screw when the hulls arrive dispersed, at which point they are contacts and count as such.

The corollary for approaches specifically. The spacing law from Fight §6 — multi-threat sequences must be spaced so a planned single-group crawl plus reload fits the gaps — tightens as A rises, because the same commander now has more bearings to walk between. So opening a third approach is not just "one more threat": it obliges wider gaps between arrivals on every bearing. If a board with A = 3 cannot be spaced that way, it should be authored at A = 2 with a higher Ca instead.

The opening battles — the on-ramp, designed as one

At Cam 1–6 the board fields one approach and 2 contacts against 2 slots and one group, which means the allocation problem structurally does not exist yet — there is nothing to allocate between. That is deliberate (Deploy §2's confirmed simplification goal), but it must be an on-ramp rather than an accident, because those battles otherwise teach the player to skip the phase that carries the sortie. The answer belongs to the toolkit teaching order in §6: introduce exactly one order-card field per early battle — placement, then the AoR nudge, then domain, then fire-control, then the tower stack — so Deploy is never empty, only small, and the allocation decision arrives at the same time as the second approach that creates it. What those battles are not is sparse: 2 contacts still carry 6–8 hulls between them, so the fleet has a working resolve network and a real motion-signature read from the first battle (Fleet Memory §7). Simple to read, not thin.

The decapitation route debuts at 5–8 guns — a teaching order the campaign must stage new ·

Falls out of the fort’s own throughput rather than from a decision. Beating a fleet by killing its purpose-bearing cores (Victory §4a) requires deep-centring an AoR over the ground a core will occupy — and that costs an approach, because holding every live bearing already consumes every gun group. At Cam 1–6 the fort fields 2–4 guns: nowhere near enough to hunt a core and cover a bearing. So:

StageGunsHow the attack is beaten back
Cam 1–62–4Annihilation or the wall clock. The pool is only ≈17 hulls, so grinding the whole fleet down is genuinely available — and holding the required enclosure to the deadline is the other route. Core-hunting is not available, and should not be.
Cam 7–225–8Decapitation debuts. A third group appears, so one can be spared to hunt. This is where purpose collapse becomes a strategy the player can discover.
Cam 23–329–12Routes must be combined. 14 cores against a sustainable ≈1.2–1.5 core-kills per sortie exceeds the 10-sortie cap, so hunting alone no longer finishes — the player must also strip screens until the survivors refuse to sail.

This is Introduce → Isolate → Combine applied to the win condition itself, which is a level the pattern had not previously been used at, and the campaign should stage it deliberately rather than letting it emerge. Concretely: the first battle where a fleet has a clearly-screened, clearly-valuable core should sit at the front of Cam 7–22 with only one such core on the board (isolate), and the mixed-route requirement should be signposted by a late board whose core count is visibly beyond what hunting can clear.

Worth being honest about the risk this creates: a player who learns to beat Cam 1–6 by annihilation has learned a technique that stops working, which is the exact experience the cognition ramp is designed to produce (“my usual opening isn’t landing like it used to”) — but here it is the win condition shifting under them, not the enemy’s mind. That is a bigger rug-pull, and it needs the tell to be loud: the first fleet whose cores are worth hunting should look conspicuously different from the fleets that came before it.

The hard constraint: subtle, but observable

Subtlety must never cross into invisibility. The GDD's own law — the player always understands why they lost — still binds. A sharper mind therefore has to show in behaviour the attentive player can read: the fleet that now verifies a gone-quiet fix visibly probes the spot before committing; the anticipating raider visibly strikes the flank you just uncovered. So "why isn't my strategy working?" always has a discoverable answer — "they stopped falling for the bait; they're checking now." If the rise is felt but its cause can never be seen, the game reads as cheating, and the curiosity we are chasing curdles into resentment. Subtle in magnitude, legible in kind.

6Toolkit Teaching Order the counter arrives with its threat

The locked 8 cannons and 7 towers obey the same Introduce→Isolate→Combine law as everything else: each is introduced with, or just before, the threat it answers — taught in isolation, then folded in. A new pressure and its counter arrive as a matched pair, in a battle where nothing else is new.

Cannons
  • Standard — Campaign 1, the baseline every board opens on.
  • Suppressor — early, the first survivability tool once incoming fire mounts.
  • Piercer — with the first Bulwark / heavy hull.
  • Interdictor + Breaker — with the first Breacher / breakthrough threat (kill-at-the-wall and buy-time, taught together).
  • Marksman — with the first back-line support (Quartermaster / Chirurgeon).
  • Splasher — with the first stacked troop clump; Saturation with the first wide swarm or bomber cloud.
Towers
  • Stonewright first — repair is the gentlest amplifier and fits the "fix what broke" loop the player already lives in.
  • Spotter (coverage), then Flare (first fog / stealth), Jammer (first fast juker), Camouflage (first Contact-hunter that punishes exposed guns), Powder and Rally as output and verb-speed begin to matter.
Why symmetry matters

Introducing a threat and its answer together means the player never faces an unanswerable wave and never holds a tool with no purpose. Every new piece — enemy or counter — enters against a background already mastered, so the only new thing to reason about is the interaction itself. That is the teaching law doing double duty, and it is what keeps the widening system legible as it grows.

7Foundational Tensions — The Load-Bearing Risks

An honest read of the foundation, judged by one question: does it keep strategy the fun? The core is sound and mutually reinforcing. These are the tensions that, mishandled, quietly convert the thinking game back into an arcade or a solved puzzle. Foundation, not polish.

1 · Determinism vs renewable strategy — the deepest tension

A deterministic puzzle with one solution stops being strategy the instant it is solved — it becomes execution of a memorised line. The design leans hard on determinism ("retrying is learning"). That is only true if each board has a wide space of viable solutions, not a single dominant one. If boards collapse to one optimal answer, replay is rote and "strategy is the fun" fails after first solve. The cognition ramp and the no-forced-playstyle rule protect this (a memorised line breaks when the mind sharpens; every threat has several answers) — but board design must actively preserve solution-space width. This is the number-one imperative for the north star.

2 · Retry can bypass reasoning

Determinism plus retry has a dark side: a player can brute-force a board by memorising spawn timings instead of reasoning about the fleet — pattern-matching, not strategy. The line between "retry to understand" and "retry to memorise" must fall on the side of understanding, which the cognition creep helps enforce (memorisation ages out) but which also argues against boards whose only solution is a single precise, memorised sequence.

3 · Legibility under load — the make-or-break

The deepest ambient risk. The system is dimensional: cannons as concept×domain, enemies as lineage×axis, memory as three channels, five layers, seven towers, triage, the cognition creep, the Fight verbs. Depth is only strategic if it is legible — the player must reason about interactions and predict outcomes. Past that ceiling, "strategy" degenerates into guessing or guide-following, and the whole design lives or dies here. Two things hold the line: the generators (you don't memorise 112 ships, you read any ship as lineage×axis; you don't memorise 24 cannons, you read them as concept×domain) and the legibility law (every enemy has a tell, the fear grid is visible, outcomes are deterministic). The imperative: as systems combine, protect legibility above all — an illegible interaction is a non-strategic one.

4 · Enclosure must stay a choice, not a dominant turtle

The spine is that enclosed guns are ghosts the fleet can't target. If enclosure were pure upside, the dominant strategy would be "turtle everything" and the game would solve itself. It is saved by real cost and real counters — the Build puzzle makes full enclosure genuinely hard (limited pieces, damage carryover), and breakthrough threats (Breacher, troops that breach and reveal from inside, bombers over the walls, the Sower denying ground) punish the pure turtle. The tension to protect: enclosure stays a tradeoff — safe but spatially costly and coverage-limited — never strictly better, never strictly punished.

5 · The Fight phase must reward reading, not reaction

A thinking game with a real-time phase risks sliding into arcade — plugging holes by fast re-tasking (APM). The AoR-crawl change is the key defence: slow, costed re-tasks turn the Fight into "watch and nudge," deliberate not twitchy, and the Deploy-first philosophy keeps live actions rare. The line to hold: the Fight rewards anticipation and reading the mind, not reflexes. If success ever comes down to speed, the identity has eroded.

The verdict

The core — a blind, deterministic mind whose difficulty is its intelligence; enclosure discipline; castle triage; no forced playstyle; difficulty by cognition, not stats — is genuinely elegant and rare. It is a real answer to the treadmill problem and points squarely at strategy-as-fun. The surface is ambitious and sits near the ceiling of manageable dimensionality; the layer set has mild internal overlap (A/D both environmental; C/E both Build-difficulty; E and triage both enclosure pressure) that a later tightening could address but need not. The whole is elegant if and only if legibility is protected as the systems fold together — that is the foundation's single point of failure, and everything above (the generators, the tells, the determinism, the matched teaching) exists to hold it. Build to keep the player reasoning, and strategy will be the fun.
