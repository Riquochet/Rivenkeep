How To Read This Document

This is the roster sweep the Fleet Memory doc promised — the dream-big list, every lineage × axis expression, developed freely. It is vast on purpose. Nothing here is a shippable cast; it is the generative superset the later joint session cuts down against the cannons.

The generative contract — no cuts here

This pass goes wide. The pair-down — bringing this bestiary to the Cannon & Tower doc and cutting both sides to exactly what works against each other (16 archetypes → 6–8, 6 tower axes → ~6, and this roster to a shippable cast) — is a separate joint session. Do not read any lineage below as a commitment. The job now is coverage of the design space, not economy. A good idea that looks redundant stays in; redundancy is the pair-down's problem, not this document's.

Inherited, settled, not re-opened (from Fleet Memory)

Everything in the Fleet Memory model is foundation. This roster rides it and must never contradict it. The load-bearing constraints every entry below obeys:

  • Blind & deterministic. No unit reads the true cannon roster, AoR radii, loaded domains, HP, or fire-control stances. A ship is a deterministic policy over (seed + its observation history) — nothing more. Every "it knows / it reads / it targets" below means from what it has perceived through the channels, never from ground truth.
  • Difficulty is Cognition first. A "harder" lineage is chiefly a less-manipulable estimator — sharper fear-reading, longer sight, slower decay, more verification. HP and fire-rate may tick up with depth, but only as seasoning; the moment the health bar becomes the main difficulty lever, the model has failed. Durability is one of seven axes and the only HP-ish one — a reason for Piercer/Finisher to exist, not the escalation engine (see The Seven Axes). Smarter, not just tougher.
  • No forced playstyle — this is a spine, not a nicety. The fleet gets harder to exploit; it never gets harder to beat only one way. Every lineage stresses a concrete lever, and buying or using that lever is always a sufficient, straightforward answer. Feinting, baiting, and bait-and-relocate are the elegant path, never the mandatory one — a commander who refuses to feint can still win every board, just with more brute lever and less finesse. Any lineage beatable only by a specific trick is a design bug (see §11).
  • Richness is the point — boredom is the enemy. The reason the roster is this wide is that "oh, another wave of X" is itself a failure state. Lineage × axis exists to keep every wave legibly different — a new mind to read, not a re-skin. Breadth here is not indulgence; it is the anti-boredom budget the pair-down must protect even as it cuts.
  • Ships are AoR-avoiders. The default movement policy is seek low incoming hit-%: loiter in weak fringes, gaps, and behind shields; avoid HOT cells on the fear grid. A handful of lineages deliberately override avoidance (the Bulwark absorbs, the Breacher commits, the Provocateur begs to be shot) — and those overrides are their whole personality.
  • Legibility is a hard constraint. Every lineage's cleverness must be readable — the Fight is a diagnosis. So every entry carries a tell: the on-screen behaviour that lets the commander see which mind they're fighting. A lineage the player can't read is a bug, not a feature.
  • Three channels are the fleet's entire mind. LOS (live vision, gated by occluders + distance) · Contact (hard facts, persistent, revised only on contradiction) · Effect (the soft ~10×10 fear grid, from coarse bearings). A lineage is defined by its relationship to these three (see Channels As Palette).
Every lineage earns its slot by stressing a lever

The counter-web rule from Fleet Memory: the roster exists to make the defender's levers matter. So each lineage below names the cannon and tower it pressures. If nothing on the defender side answers a lineage, that lineage is a free win and gets cut later; if a lineage stresses a lever no other enemy touches, that lever earns its slot in the cannon pair-down. This document is therefore also the input to the cannon/tower cut — the Counter-Web Map is the hand-off table.

1The Generator — Lineage × Axis

The same trick the cannon matrix uses (role × ammunition), one layer out. Ships rhyme within a lineage — a fleet role, a strategy over the channels — and diverge along axes — the growth directions. A small set of base lineages × a handful of axes is the big combinatorial generator; the vast list is what it produces.

roster = lineage (a strategy over LOS / Contact / Effect) × axis (a growth direction) × domain (sea / land / air)
The evolution axes are the counter-web axes — one structure expressed twice

Straight from Fleet Memory: a lineage's growth directions are the escalation of the defender's levers. When a Recon lineage grows along Cognition, the answering lever is the Reveal tower; when a Bulwark grows along Durability, the answer is the Piercer. So the matrix is not two lists — it is one counter-web, read from the attacker's side. Introduce each axis in roughly the order the player earns the answering lever: Introduce → Isolate → Combine. Early boards run one axis at a time against a fresh answer; late boards stack them against a commander who has every lever and must choose.

2The Three Channels As A Design Palette

The deepest hook in the whole roster: a lineage is not primarily a stat block — it is a strategy over the fleet's three channels. Design a ship by choosing which channel it lives on, which it writes, and which it poisons. Everything else is tuning.

LOS lineages — the vision war

Live vision is the fleet's sensor, gated by occluders and distance. Lineages here are about seeing and not being seen: the Recon that extends the fleet's eyes (and dies for it), the Wraith that lives in the gap where the sightline breaks, the Sower that plants an occluder to blind your guns. The defender's answer is the Flare / Spotter Reveal subsystem — which is why a whole family of enemies exists to make those towers precious.

Contact lineages — the memory war

Contact is hard, persistent, and revised only on contradiction — never time-decayed. That single property is a goldmine of enemy behaviour: the Reaver that can only act on confirmed fixes (so exposure is the crime), the troops ashore that manufacture contacts from inside your perimeter, the Mimic that plants false facts the fleet won't revise until contradicted. The player's counter-play is the whole hidden-breach / ghost-structure bait layer, and the Camouflage tower that keeps a group off the contact ledger entirely.

Effect lineages — the fear war

The fear grid is soft, coarse (~10×10), and decays slowly — it can be read, starved, and lied to. The Bombard and Corsair route purely by it (avoiding HOT, exploiting COLD seams); the Provocateur forces you to write to it (draw fire → the flash is banked); the Bulwark absorbs it so soft hulls travel in its cold shadow. This is where hold-fire is a weapon on both sides: your silence keeps a cell COLD; their Provocateur tries to break your silence.

Why designing by channel keeps the roster legible

Because the three channels render in three distinct visual languages (the exposure glint, the fear haze, the decay behaviour — see Fleet Memory's Commander UI), a channel-defined enemy has a built-in tell. A Reaver's fixation on an exposed gun, a Wraith's shimmer at the fog edge, a Provocateur's taunting push — each reads as which channel it's playing, so the Fight stays diagnostic. This is the legibility law paying rent: define the ship by channel, and its readability comes for free.

Axis 3 is the escalation; the rest are texture

The Fleet Memory Cognition axis says "harder as you descend" = a monotonic decrease in how exploitable the fleet's inference is. That is axis 3, and only axis 3, doing the campaign-scale escalation: slower fear-decay, higher fear-resolution, cross-check verification, longer sight, deliberate searching fire. When a late board is harder, it is because the same lineage now reads you better — a less-manipulable estimator — not because it has more health. Every lineage's Cognition variant below is its late-game apex, and it is always smarter, never tougher.

Durability is a lever, not the treadmill — guard this line

Durability is deliberately kept as one axis of seven, and framed as a puzzle, not a health bar: a durable enemy is answered by piercing it (Piercer), culling it once cracked (Finisher, no overkill), or out-killing it (Output towers) — three defender answers, each a real choice. The failure mode the whole game refuses is durability as the escalation ladder ("bigger enemy → build bigger"). So Durability variants exist to give Piercer/Finisher/Output their reason to exist — not to make the campaign a stat race. If a board ever feels hard only because hulls got fatter, that is the treadmill leaking in, and the fix is to move the difficulty back onto Cognition.

The two axes that carry the early game

Also from Fleet Memory: "easy early, hard late" runs mostly on axes 3 and 4 (Cognition and Coordination), not 1. Early ships are blind blunderers on shallow formations that path into your bright cores and die cheap; late ships are sharp, coordinated hunters that avoid cores, find seams, and screen their support. So the axes are also a difficulty schedule: Durability / Firepower / Payload teach the levers early (concrete, legible threats); Cognition / Coordination are where the ceiling lives.

4The Flagship–Boss Spectrum

One role on a spectrum. Every battle has a flagship — the coordination hub and morale anchor, screened in the back line. A boss is a rare, promoted flagship: fanatical resolve plus bespoke mechanics on top of the flagship's coordination role. Not every flagship is a boss — or "boss" means nothing and you have ~190 bespoke centrepieces to build.

Any lineage can be promoted to flagship — the promotion is the flavour

The flagship is not a separate ship; it is a lineage member wearing the command hub. So each lineage below names its flagship-promotion — what changes about the fleet when that role holds the hub. A Recon flagship makes decapitation also re-blind the fleet (a nasty double); a Bulwark flagship is the fanatical tank that forms the natural boss chassis; a Reaver flagship shares its fixes instantly, so killing it wipes the fleet's shared target book. The promotion unifies three systems Fleet Memory already wants: the back-line-targeting cannon (flagship-killer = boss-killer), fleet screening (the screen = the boss's guard), and the resolve spread (boss = the fanatical top of the knob).

The back-line-targeting cannon's reason to exist

The flagship is screened and back-line, so it is the reason the Marksman (Lone Wolf, deletes the highest-value threat) and the back-line target-personality exist at all. Fighting to the flagship means fighting through the screen (Bulwarks, Quartermasters) — the decapitation play is a legible, hard-won objective, not a free snipe. This is also why the Quartermaster and Bulwark lineages matter: they are the flagship's guard, and the fleet protects its flagship (screening behaviour) as an emergent priority.

Boss homes — where the promoted flagships naturally live

Some chassis promote to boss better than others. The Bulwark (fanatical tank) and Leviathan (capital bruiser) are the obvious boss frames — durable, unbreakable, set-piece. The Herald (a fanatical zealot-fleet you must break via the standard, not the hull), the Mimic (the Impostor — a boss that hides which ship is the real flagship among decoys), and the Chirurgeon-Fleet (the Grand Chirurgeon — a fleet that won't stay dead until you kill the healer) are the puzzle-bosses. The GDD's theater Mystarchs (Leviathan / Colossus / Sandworm / Hydra / Magma Titan / Frost Wyrm / Storm King / Ancient Treant / Plague Herald / Crystal Lich) are the shippable capstone list — each a promoted flagship on one of these chassis, dressed for its theater.

Resolve is a PER-SHIP NETWORK — the layer, fully designed (v2.0.1)

Not a global morale unit and not a single scalar: every ship carries its own resolve (internal 0–1, never shown). Each hull's resolve is pushed by three things — (a) its own experience: damage it takes, fear cells it crosses, its own progress; (b) peer influence: it is pulled toward the resolve of nearby hulls — a fleeing hull drags its neighbours down into a rout cascade, a steady cluster stiffens a waverer (panic contagion); (c) an anchor broadcast: top-down support from the flag. Resolve rides the same lagged propagation net as fear and contact (it reuses Fleet Memory's command-confusion batching — not a new system). Fully deterministic: same battle → same cascade → same break points. This makes resolve a true peer of the fear grid — fear is a spatial field of your danger on the map; resolve is a network field of their will on the hulls.

The anchor hierarchy & the field promotion

Anchors rank: flagship (primary — its broadcast strength is the intensity dial) > Herald / Rally-type > Quartermaster / command support > capital ships (Leviathan etc., anchoring nearby via their own high resolve) > peers. Kill the flagship and a surviving hull is field-promoted to flag: it takes the flag icon, gets no buffs (a battlefield promotion, not a purpose-built command ship), and the fleet's anchor personality becomes that hull's personality (its lineage's resolve tendency + intensity). The successor is the most command-capable survivor (the hierarchy above). Consequences: (1) decapitation is not one kill but a succession — the fleet inherits whatever you leave alive, so breaking its will means killing the flag and the tough anchors, leaving only skittish hulls to inherit; (2) every successor is unbuffed → easier to kill than the original → chain-decapitation ratchets coordination and resolve down toward a battle Retreat; (3) the honest risk — a careless kill that hands the flag to a fanatical survivor makes the fleet harder. A Herald boss short-circuits this: it is a resilient standard, so you must break the standard, not chase successors.

The intensity dial — a STRATEGY-SELECTOR, not a power knob

One knob, set per battle by whoever holds the flag (its broadcast strength). Sliding it up, monotonically: lowers each hull's personal rout threshold, strengthens the anchor broadcast, raises recovery, and lowers contagion susceptibility (fanatical hulls resist panic; skittish hulls catch it instantly — so skittish fleets cascade fast, fanatical fleets don't cascade and must be ground down hull-by-hull). Per-lineage resting points: skittish Recon/Corsair/Sower/Quartermaster/Wraith; fanatical Bulwark/Breacher/Leviathan/Provocateur; Herald = pinned max. Fanatical cuts both ways — not "harder," a different problem (anti-treadmill): bluffable = evasive but fragile of will (skirts your guns → hard to kill, but routs → easy to break); fanatical = brave but exploitable by position (ignores the fear grid → drives straight into kill-zones → easy to slaughter, but won't rout → can't be bluffed). Against fanatical you don't bluff — you build the meat-grinder and let their fearlessness walk them in.

Reading it: the MOTION SIGNATURE — no morale bar

You never see a number. Resolve maps deterministically to how a hull moves — that mapping is the entire display (a bar would be bookkeeping; in a real battle you read the line's behaviour). Two orthogonal components, never confusable: LATERAL = fear (reading the fear grid — the hull still faces and nets forward to its objective, just skirts/routes around a HOT cell); FORE-AFT = nerve (resolve). Nerve reads as a rearward gradient: Committed (drives straight forward, ignores fear cells — the fanatical read), Steady (forward + lateral fear-avoidance — the default), Wavering (net motion develops a rearward component — drifts back, hangs behind the objective, lines sag aft), Breaking/Routing (commits to the rearward run — flees to spawn), Gone (reached spawn, off-field). "Advancing around a danger" (lateral) can never be confused with "drifting back toward the exit" (aft). The flag icon marks which hull is the anchor; behaviour tells how close it is to breaking — separate jobs, so the icon does not reopen the "morale bar" door.

What drains & restores it (perception-based)

Drains: cluster / fast visible sinks, esp. high-value (hard — but bluffable in timing: a held alpha concentrates the morale hit); crossing HOT/SCALDING & being turned back (bluffable — fear-projection drains morale: resolve reads the fear grid, and the grid is a belief you write); no progress / stalemate (partly bluffable — deny apparent progress); flagship/anchor killed (hard, discrete, biggest — crater + a forced succession). Restores: progress / breach / breakthrough ("we're winning" → presses harder — so a breakthrough raises the fleet's resolve); anchor rally while it lives; the between-sortie regroup (bounded by recovery). Belief-drains are bluffable; hard-drains you concentrate in time — that split is the whole "break their will" skill.

Outcomes are EMERGENT from the network — locks to the Victory doc

A hull routs when its own resolve crosses its personal threshold → it runs to spawn. REPEL (sortie) = a rout cascade tips the wave: enough hulls rout that the committed remainder loses cohesion and disengages early → banks time. HELD = anchors hold the net above rout until ammo is spent → orderly reload-withdrawal. BREAKTHROUGH = a breach event, independent of resolve. Battle RETREAT = across sorties the net has degraded so far (losses, dead/weak anchors) that a re-formed wave instantly cascades — the fleet stops committing (hulls may remain). Battle ANNIHILATION = pool exhausted. Repels drop the net's baseline (the break-their-will currency); held sorties don't demoralise (the grind currency). Stalemate drains per-hull resolve → guarantees termination, no infinite stalemate.

Deployment, the screen, and the safe launch zone

The flag does not sail first — its convoy does (Jack, v2.5.0). “The flagship does not come out first. Its convoy does. It is the standard bearer. The flagship should be just like any other ship. It has a job to do.” So the flag is a marker on a hull that has its own job, not a command vessel that arrives ahead of the fleet to hold an anchor point. It occupies the command core slot of an ordinary convoy (§9), sails when that convoy sails, and does that convoy’s work while it flies the standard. Three things follow, and all three are improvements. (1) The flag’s lineage now matters twice — once for its flagship-promotion effect and once for what it is physically doing on the board, so a Bombard flag is shelling your wall while it anchors the net and a Runner flag is driving for your coast. (2) Decapitation becomes a target-selection problem inside a live formation rather than a race to catch a lone hull in an opening window — which is the harder and better version of the play. (3) The flag’s convoy is an ordinary formation for queue purposes: field promotion moves the standard to another hull elsewhere in the fleet rather than resurrecting this one, so the convoy is eliminated by the normal rule (Victory §4b) and the queue needs no exception for it. Superseded: the earlier “deploys first, takes station at the back, broadcast dormant until the wave assembles on it” pass. The assembly window survives in relocated form, and is worth keeping: the shakiest, most baitable moment is a convoy forming up at its launch zone before it has coherence, and the flag’s convoy is the most valuable instance of that. An opening alpha there still seeds a cheap cascade — a gamble, never a free snipe.

RESOLVED (Jack, v2.6.0) — the buff stays, and only the first flag gets it

Ruling: “The first flag ship should be buffed. It is the captain of the team, so there is a reason why. If it fails, the battle promotion on the next sortie does not have the buff.”

So the buff is not a consequence of the old lone-arrival opening — it is who the ship is. The fleet’s original commander is a better-built hull with a better crew because the fleet chose it for the job; a hull that inherits the standard mid-battle is a lieutenant standing in, and gets nothing. That reading survives the “just like any other ship” ruling cleanly, because the two rulings are about different things: the flag has an ordinary job (it sails in a convoy and does that convoy’s work) and an extraordinary crew.

What it buys the design, stated plainly: the decapitation ratchet gets a first step that is genuinely harder than the rest. Killing the original captain is the expensive kill; every successor after that is unbuffed, so chain-decapitation accelerates — the fleet gets easier to behead the more you behead it. That is a better curve than a flat one, and it is the mechanical form of a command structure degrading. It also means the honest risk already recorded below still stands and now matters more: hand the flag to a fanatical survivor and you have made the fleet harder to break while making it easier to kill.

The flag is slightly buffed — modest durability + speed only, sub-boss, NOT firepower (a command hull is better-built) so reaching it is a committed play, never a stray-shot kill; this buff is a flat structural fact, NOT a difficulty knob that scales (intensity scales, hull/speed do not — anti-treadmill), and field-promoted successors get none of it. The screen is the other ships — they actively protect the flag (emergent priority; the Quartermaster/Bulwark guard). Every spawn has a safe launch zone: hulls accelerate fast there and cannot be hit (no spawn-camping — it would be unfair to sit guns on the emergence point); in the AoR this shows as a displayed dead zone in the coverage (cross-ref the Cannon & Tower doc for the render). So the flag emerges safe, accelerates out, stations — you engage it only once it clears the launch zone and comes within reach.

Supersedes earlier passes; what remains is sanding

Design facts (locked): the per-ship network; anchor hierarchy + field promotion; the flag-icon marker; the motion-signature tell; fanatical-as-tradeoff; deployment / assembly window / safe launch zone; and the emergent map to Held/Repel/Breakthrough/Retreat/Annihilation. Sanding magnitudes (specified targets; final values tuned in prototype — feelings, not facts): per-hull rout thresholds & broadcast strengths per intensity tier; contagion susceptibility per tier; the cascade tipping fraction (target: once a cascade starts the wave visibly unravels over ~2–3 s); recovery rate; the relative drain weights. Retired & dead: the earlier "global scalar + rally-effort barometer," and the "no-marker, identify-by-deploy-order" pass. The Mimic/Impostor boss is the escalation of the flag-icon tell — it fakes the marker and anchoring behaviour with decoys, so identifying the real flag becomes the puzzle.

Sea 14 · Mimic the fleet baits YOU deep · late · kept open

Channel identity — a lone deceiver that poisons your read of the fleet's mind

The signaling game reversed: the receiver deceiving the sender. The Mimic doesn't fight — it lies. It plants false Contact into your read, exploiting that contact is "revised only on contradiction" and that your whole UI — exposure-glints, fear-haze — is a set of signals it can forge. It is the deliberate on-ramp to the late game: the board where a player who has mastered baiting meets a fleet that baits back. Two hard facts define it — it is a true loner (it never forms up with the fleet; it works alone), and it never shoots (there is no place for a cannon on a hull whose whole job is deception; a Mimic that fired would give itself away).

This channel stays open — we build toward it

The Mimic is a permanent fixture of the design, not a maybe. The exact mechanic is still exploratory; the legibility law decides which version ships, and every candidate must carry a fair, learnable tell. Two lead candidates:

  • Disguise — the Mimic renders as a different ship type (a fat Bombard, a harmless Runner) but behaves as a Mimic. The tell is behavioural: the disguise never does the thing its silhouette promises — the "Bombard" never shells, the "Runner" never beaches.
  • Mirror-projection — the Mimic projects copies of real ships into mirrored positions on the field, multiplying an apparent fleet out of phantoms. The tell is built into the trick: hit any one projection and all of them wink out at once — a real ship doesn't vanish when its neighbour is struck. A single test shot dispels the whole illusion, and learning to spend that shot is the skill the lineage teaches.

Movement: alone, never in formation; a disguise loiters in character, projections drift in unnatural mirror-symmetry. Tell: per the candidates above — a promise never kept, or copies that die together. Resolve: a trickster — deliberately ambiguous, flees once exposed. Stresses: the Lone-Wolf cannons — it never groups, so Marksman deletes the real hull and Piercer/Disabler punish the single target — once you have identified which hull is real — plus the commander's UI-reading skill itself.

7Troops — The Land Domain same mind · their own levers

Troops ride the identical three-channel mind, but land changes what the levers are. A troop is a Contact-writer the moment it lands, it moves by weighted A* pathfinding rather than fear-avoidance, it fights by formation, and it is a guess until it's ashore.

Revealed on landing — the Payload guess resolves into Contact

While aboard a Runner, a troop wave is only the transport's Payload — you don't know its composition, just that a heavy line is driving for your coast. The wave reveals on landing: as each rank disembarks — on the deploy cadence below, so heavies trickle and light infantry pours — it becomes hard Contact for the fleet and a known quantity for you. The composition therefore reveals progressively, rank by rank, not in one flash. So the Runner's approach is a bluff on both sides — you're defending a coast against troops you can't yet see, and the fleet is committing a wave it hasn't yet confirmed will land. Kill the transport and the reveal never happens.

Troops ashore are close-recon — they write Contact from inside your wall

A troop that reaches and strikes a structure confirms it on the Contact channel — from inside the perimeter, past the outer wall that was blocking the fleet's sightlines. This is how a deep fort gets read: the fleet breaches, troops pour in, and the interior geometry you thought was hidden gets written to the ledger. It also means clearing troops each sortie doesn't un-write what they found — the contact persists; only the troops reset.

The land-native levers
  • Target chain — an ordered priority list per lineage, not a single target: Swarm = wall → cannon → castle, falling through as each is cleared; Linebreaker = wall only (idle otherwise); Infiltrator = one locked high-value target. The land mirror of a cannon's target-personality.
  • HP-bias (the tiebreak) — which target inside the current chain tier: weakest-first (guarantee kills, deny the enemy healer its patient) or toughest-first (crack the blocker before it stalls the wave). A per-lineage temperament, and why Finisher and focus-fire matter on land too.
  • Deploy cadence — troops disembark on a rate, not in one lump, and bigger troops deploy slower: a heavy line trickles ashore while light infantry pours. Cadence is the throttle that keeps a wave flowing into contact rather than spawning a wall of bodies.
  • Stacking — troops mass, and ×4 hits harder than ×1 (damage scales with the stack), so concentration is rewarded and the fleet wants to clump — which is exactly what feeds Splasher and Saturation. Formation (below) is how a stack is shaped.
  • Formation — the arrangement a stack holds, which decides which multi-target cannon answers it: clump, dispersed, file, wedge, shieldwall, testudo (the grammar is §9). The Coordination axis made literal on land.
  • Command-confusion batching — the propagation-lag signature, one layer down: more troops → slower coordination, so a big wave reacts as laggy batches, not one mind. The land Standard-bearer tightens this (killing it loosens it).
Cadence × stacking — the "no dead-weight queue" rule

Cadence and stacking are tuned against each other so troops are always doing something. The deploy rate should roughly track the rate the front can absorb, so a wave flows steadily into contact instead of piling into an idle lump behind the breach. Massing (×4 > ×1) is the reward for landing a stack intact — never a licence to spawn a hundred bodies that shuffle uselessly. When troops do back up — a held breach, a wall that won't fall — that stall is a legible fail-state for the fleet (its committed wave can't land) and a win-signal for you, not dead animation time. Bigger-deploys-slower is the honest price of fielding heavies: you get the tougher troop, you wait longer between them.

Deployment — one Transport, a disembark cadence (from the GDD)

A single Transport chassis (the Runner) carries every ground type; it docks on the friendly grids just past the DMZ and, once docked, does not move again unless the DMZ shifts. Its "fire rate" is the disembark cadence — continuous, and it never empties until sunk. Troops fan out from any side of its footprint (even backward) into adjacent grids and attack whatever wall blocks them. Composition is fixed per sortie: the surprise is real only the first time you meet that sortie; after that you know exactly what beaches and can plan for it. Sink the Transport and further deployment stops, but troops already ashore finish their mission. Troops reset every sortie and never count toward the win — only ships and enclosure do.

Stacking — the exact curve (from the GDD)

Same-type troops sharing a grid merge, and the payoff is super-linear on damage, sub-linear on HP:

  • 1 troop — 1× damage, 1× HP
  • 2 troops — 2× damage, 1.5× HP
  • 3 troops — 3× damage, 2× HP
  • 4 troops — 4× damage, 2.5× HP (max stack)

A full stack hits four times as hard but is not four times as tough — and one well-aimed shot damages the whole stack, which is exactly what makes Splasher and Saturation matter: massing is the fleet's reward and its exposure at once. A troop arriving at a full (4) stack queues behind and reroutes, bunching at chokepoints — the visible pile-up the cadence rule above exists to keep meaningful.

Movement — deterministic weighted A* (from the GDD)

Every troop runs weighted A* to the lowest-cost path to its target; same board state → same path, always. The target chain is baked into the search: enclosed (non-grey) castles first (nearest), and only if all castles are grey does it fall through to cannons, then walls. The cost per grid entered is the whole game of it:

  • Open ground 1; damaged tree / cliff 2; healthy tree 4; diagonal moves ×1.414 on top.
  • A wall block costs (hits-to-destroy + 1) — its HP ÷ this troop's damage, plus one to step through. A 100-HP wall vs a 5-damage troop = 21; a 30-HP wall = 7. So the pathfinder naturally flows toward damaged walls and away from healthy ones with no special logic — chip a wall and you redirect the whole wave.
  • Impassable (∞): rocks, the DMZ (troops don't path back over it), area denial (fire, craters, holes), and any unstackable troop (a different type, or a full-4 stack).
  • Because walls have finite cost, there is always a path — the troop just destroys walls along the cheapest one. Rocks are the only true funnels: place them to herd the wave into a kill lane.

Re-evaluation is the command-confusion engine: ≤8 troops re-path every tick; 9–16 split into two groups (even/odd ticks); 17–24 into three; 25+ into four. Between re-paths a troop follows its last route — so a big swarm reacts in laggy batches, and more troops means slower coordination. The chaos is a feature; the land Standard-bearer is the knob that tightens it.

Damage — dealing and taking (from the GDD)

Troops damage whatever they are side-adjacent to (4-way); one troop touching three or four walls damages all of them at once. Damage per tick is uniform across walls, cannons, and castles — the different times-to-kill come only from structure HP (walls fall faster than cannons faster than castles). Exposure cuts both ways: a troop adjacent to a firing cannon takes steady recoil attrition (a few HP per shot) even if you never target it — so your guns quietly grind down the troops clawing at them, and a stack pinned on a cannon dies to the blast alone given time.

Collateral you can exploit

Enemy ships arc over their own troops — normal shots don't clip a friendly cluster. But area-of-effect does: a splash-ship's or a bomber's blast overlapping the fleet's own troops hurts them. So positioning your walls to force enemy AoE and bomb runs onto their own ground troops is a legitimate tactic — you make their firepower do your work. (Friendly fire is off early and switches on deeper in the campaign; once on, it cuts both ways.)

8Bombers — The Air Domain the switch-moment archetype

Air is the domain that most sharply justifies the domain verb. Bombers launch from Skyfall carriers (or fly as airships), fly over walls, and target castles — the launch is the loudest telegraphed switch-moment in the game. Air and land are the two-thirds of the domain triad that drive the switch verb: you hold Sea against the fleet, then switch a group to Land as troops beach or to Air as the decks open. That is the whole reason the verb exists (§10).

Bomber baseline (kept from the GDD, reconciled to the model)
  • Target = a castle, chosen at launch (baseline: random among the surviving castles — RNG can send two in a row to the same one), then a fear-aware run at it: bombers don't A* around walls (walls are nothing to them) but they do read the flak heat-map, biasing their approach away from the hottest anti-air cells, with a ±1-grid crazy-Ivan jitter on top. If the target castle dies mid-flight they turn back, land on the carrier, and are reassigned to a fresh random castle on the next launch; never-enclosed (grey) castles are ignored. So concentrated flak doesn't just kill bombers — it herds them, and a smart cloud threads the gap you left thin.
  • Fragile, fast, agile — by design. A bomber trades hull for speed and a hard jink: easy to kill if you can bring a gun to bear in its short flight window, but it crosses fast and juke-heavy, so slow or ill-aimed flak pays for it. The lone exception is the Zeppelin — slow, tough, unmissable, the airship that dares you to shoot it and can soak the answer.
  • Always the switch cue. The carrier opening its deck is a threat you can see crossing the map — the archetypal reason to switch a group to Air. This is what the domain-switch machinery exists to serve (see §10).
  • Air-density is the open knob. Bombers must be frequent enough that loading Air is sometimes right, rare enough that a commander who never builds dedicated flak isn't punished for it. This is a tuning question the roster hands to the pair-down, not a settled number.
Carrier & bombers — one linked HP pool (from the GDD)

A carrier's bombers share its hull: each bomber's HP = carrier HP ÷ number of bombers (five bombers → each has a fifth). The consequence is the hook — kill every bomber and the carrier dies with them; killing the bombers IS killing the carrier. Carriers launch from open water (no DMZ touch — bombers are aerial); the carrier's "fire rate" is really how fast its bombers fly, and a landed bomber needs a short beat (~4 ticks) before it can go again. Crucially, launching leaves the carrier fully exposed for a few seconds — that is both the switch-moment telegraph and your window to punish the platform. Sink the carrier while bombers are airborne and they finish the current run, then crash on the way home: one last strike, then gone.

Flak — how you actually hit them (from the GDD)

Bombers are always visible — they count as troops, so fog never hides them (a bomber can't go Wraith on you; the air threat is always legible). They do not stack: many can share a grid but each takes damage independently, so a cloud is N separate targets, not one fat one — which is why a wide cloud wants Saturation and a tight stream wants Piercer. Any cannon can engage them: shots fire steeply upward — a hit stops at the bomber, a miss falls back to earth and may clip a wall, a troop, or open ground below. All bombers fly at one altitude, so a bomber crossing a shot that's already in the air can be caught by it — but only on the rising portion of the arc. That's the mechanical reason fear-aware routing pays: a cloud that threads your quiet sky dodges these incidental hits, while one forced through an active fire zone eats them. On the ground, bomblets need four hits on the same open grid to crater it into area denial; hits on walls or castles are direct damage.

Formation coherence IS the contact count — the sizing consequence new ·

The sizing model (Victory §3) counts contacts, not hulls — one coherent object of attention, one coverage decision. That makes the formation grammar above a direct lever on the game's legibility load, and the rule is exactly the grammar read as a count:

  • A formation holding shape counts as ONE contact, whatever its hull count — clump, file, wedge, shieldwall, testudo. It moves, routes and breaks as one thing, and one AoR answers it.
  • A dispersed or picket formation counts as ONE PER HULL. That is precisely what the spacing bought: it beats the splash and it forces separate tracking.
  • Troop stacks count as one (a single shot hits the whole stack — §7); a bomber cloud counts as one, a bomber stream as one per element (§8).
  • The flag is a label on its formation while it is screened, and becomes its own contact only once it is separable — thinned or maneuvering clear of its escort. Corrected v2.5.0. The earlier rule (“always its own contact”) does not survive the arithmetic: the flag plus its escort would consume the entire early contact budget (C=2) every sortie and no payload would ever arrive. Common fate is the measured criterion and it gives the right answer — while the flag moves with its escort it is one tracked object. This also prices the decapitation play honestly: stripping the flag’s escort costs the player an attention slot, so committing to the kill makes the board harder to read at the exact moment you commit.

The perceptual backing, since this rule is doing real work. Group tracking capacity is about four groups and is independent of how many items compose each group; it degrades as the items in a group spread apart, and it degrades when their common fate is disrupted. So all three halves of the rule above are measured rather than assumed: a held shape genuinely reads as one object, spacing genuinely dissolves it, and a formation that breaks mid-approach genuinely costs the player attention at the moment it breaks. That last case is worth authoring deliberately — a wave that fragments under fire is buying itself tracking load at the player's expense, which is a real tactic and not a bug.

The counterpart, and the one not to lose: hull count is a different quantity and is not capped by this. Resolve, fear propagation, contagion and the rout cascade are population mechanics (§4) and want bodies — roughly six to function, eight or more to read well. Because a coherent formation costs one contact whatever its size, a wave of two four-hull clumps is two objects for the eye and eight bodies for the network. Formation coherence is what lets the fleet be statistically rich and perceptually simple at the same time, which is why the opening battles field clumps rather than lone hulls.

So Coordination compresses the count while raising per-contact difficulty; dispersal inflates it. The Coordination axis is therefore the fleet's legibility-load dial as much as its tactical one, and it puts a hard constraint on wave authoring: the contact budget is one budget shared between count and sophistication. A wave may arrive wide and simple, or narrow and clever — never both. That is Introduce → Isolate → Combine applied to the player's attention rather than to mechanics, and it is why the picket, the design's most attention-expensive shape, has no single-shot answer by design (the counter is denying vision, not out-shooting it).

FORMATIONS PERSIST BETWEEN SORTIES — and reconstitute by role new ·

A formation is not assembled fresh each sortie. It is a persistent entity with role slots, and it carries its losses forward. Ruling (Jack): "The target (formation) of ships should persist between sorties. Let's say I have 4 screen ships and 1 troop ship in a formation. If the player kills 3 screen ships, the next sortie will have to look back for 3 more screen ships for the troop transport. If the troop transport is sunk and 2 screen ships remain, they can be reallocated to another screen, but they are probably damaged and soon sunk."

This is the mechanic that makes the whole contact model bite, because it means what you kill inside a formation decides what the fleet looks like next sortie — and the two choices lead to completely different fleets.

Forcing those into one core plus escorts requires inventing a lead hull that exists only to satisfy a rule; letting them sail as coreless formations destroys the coreless-residual read that purpose collapse depends on. It sails on its own account with its own errand, and it is purpose-serving at fleet scale — it strips the gun that would have sunk the transport, blinds the flank the carrier will launch into. And a wolfpack is never mistaken for a coreless residual: it has an errand and it moves with intent, where a residual “moves like hulls with nowhere to be” (Fleet Memory §7).

Screen slots split by behaviour: shields and riders

A screen slot is filled by one of two things, and the difference matters because of how targeting works (see Targeting → why screens work below): cannons fire at the target nearest their group’s AoR centre, so a screen protects by occupying the high-probability position.

  • Shield — interposes. It puts itself where the player’s own targeting preference will send the shells. Bulwark and the Durability-axis expression of most lineages.
  • Rider — travels inside the shape and never interposes. Quartermaster, Chirurgeon-Fleet, Herald, an attached single Recon or Sower.

Both consume a screen slot for refill purposes. Only shields count toward the sortie-refusal threshold (Victory §4a), because only a shield can buy a core anything.

A free consequence worth authoring: a rider-heavy formation looks thick and is not. That is the signaling spine getting a deception at no design cost, and it stays fair because the tells already exist — the Bulwark is the biggest silhouette on the board, and the Quartermaster never fires and never bleeds.

The screen is a live allocation, not a set of stations (Jack) new · v2.5.0

Four rulings that together make the screen a behaviour rather than a formation diagram. All four are legible, and three of them are new information for the player about the fleet’s mind.

  • Tightness tracks the fleet’s confidence in its own map. “An escort screen maybe loose for unknown waters, but tighten up when it thinks it knows the player’s AoR.” A loose screen means the fleet is still guessing; a screen that closes up means it believes it has your coverage pinned. This is the Contact and Effect channels made visible in the shape of the escort — a second read of the fleet’s belief state alongside the fear-haze, and a baitable one: feed a false read and the screen tightens against a zone you have already moved.
  • A screen is not necessarily a ring. “The screen might not be a ring around the payload ship. It might be a picket line closer to the center of the AoR. Their job is to draw fire.” This is the sharpest possible expression of the cont.16 targeting rule: the escort’s job is not to physically block, it is to be the nearest hull to your AoR centre. So a screen can stand well forward of its core, spread across the zone’s middle, and still be a screen. It also means a thick escort does not have to look like a wall — it can look like a line of bait, which is exactly what it is.
  • Escorts are faster than their core, and they reallocate under fire. “An escort ship can go faster than the payload ship, but that doesn’t mean that the screen ship just stay in a specific location, it may move to have stronger ships fill in the screen where a weaker ship maybe failing.” So a screen slot is a role being filled, not a station being occupied: as a shield takes damage, a healthier shield slides across to cover the bearing it was covering. The consequence for the player is that focus-firing one shield does not open a hole — it summons a better hull into that hole, at the cost of thinning somewhere else. The counter is to make two holes at once, which is a coverage-and-concentration decision, exactly the one the design wants.
  • The escort speed differential is a tell. Because escorts can outrun their core, a convoy moving at escort speed is a convoy that has lost its core; a convoy moving at core speed still has one. That falls out for free and is worth authoring into the motion read.

TRANSLATION CHECK — the exponent transfers, the absolute share does not new ·

Per the translation law (real figures are inputs, not authorities — Jack), the convoy data has to be split into the part that carries over and the part that does not.

  • The exponent transfers. 0.775 is a fact about the geometry of protecting a group — ring plus depth plus stations answering bearings. That geometry is the same here, so the curve holds.
  • The absolute share does not. Real convoys ran 12–24% escort because a U-boat pass threatened a few ships. Our fort accounts for roughly 55% of arrivals per passage under perfect allocation — an order of magnitude more lethal per crossing than the threat those ratios were calibrated against. A 12%-escorted formation would simply be deleted here, and the fleet would not sail one. So our screens must be far thicker than history's, and they are.
  • What was scarce, then and now. Escorts were scarce in 1943 because of shipyards. Here the fleet's scarcity is queue depth and the contact budget — so a shield is priced in what it denies the rest of the fleet, not in construction time. That is why attriting screens drains the pool rather than the queue (Victory §4b), and it is the correct game-side expression of a scarce escort.

Worth confronting honestly: our overall fleet runs ≈24% cores, where a merchant convoy runs 76–88% payload. We are far more escort-heavy than trade shipping and far less than a carrier group, and we sit between them because our fleet is both — it carries payloads and screens them by interposition, which no single real formation does. That is a defensible middle, not an unexamined one, and it is the first time this composition has had an external check of any kind against it.

It sharpens the player’s problem rather than diluting it. A shared screen is a concentration of value: getting through it once reaches several cores, so the reward for breaking a heavy screen scales with what is behind it. And screen thickness stays a readable signal — six hulls around one transport is a fleet protecting something it cannot afford to lose; six around three is a fleet running an economy of scale, and a cheaper prize per hull killed.

Three consequences, all emergent rather than authored:

  • Bundling costs the fleet its options. A shared convoy moves at its slowest core’s speed, can take only one bearing, and cannot feint. It is also a Splasher’s dream. The discount is paid for in tactical flexibility, which is the right price.
  • Surplus screens are released as orphans. Kill one of three cores and the formation now wants four shields for two cores but holds six — the surplus two are released and reallocated at reconstitution. So attacking a shared screen core by core reinforces the rest of the fleet. The only good answer is to break the whole thing inside one engagement, which is a genuinely hard problem nobody had to author.
  • Mixed-core convoys are the sharpest tool the domain verb has. A Runner and a Skyfall behind one screen is a two-domain threat inside one contact, forcing a domain decision on a single AoR (see the switch-moment catalogue, §10). Authoring cap: two domains per shared screen — three exceeds the ≤4-decision-bearing law the moment it lands.

Two routes, both designed — the orphan fork and sortie refusal designed v2.5.0

The core/screen structure invites a simple reading — hunt cores, win — and Jack ruled it out from both directions. Both directions are now designed rather than recorded as constraints.

  • Killing the payload does not end the battle. “You shouldn’t be able to kill the payload ship and the battle ends. Those screen ships can do damage too and they might be out for revenge.” Purpose collapse is a resolve pressure, not a kill switch (Victory §4a).
  • Killing enough screens can stop the cores from sailing at all. “Kill enough screen ships and the payload ships may not come out to battle.” A core will not risk an unescorted run. Threshold and tells are owned by Victory §4a.

Ruling (Jack): “If a core ship is killed, the screen could continue as a wolfpack, but it could also retreat — it lost its purpose. Why this is the right balance and not a compromise between two ideas. It means the player’s own prior pressure decides which one they get. So a surgical kill is more dangerous than a worn-down one — which inverts the usual expectation, is honest, costs nothing new, and gives the grind line a genuine payoff it did not have before. What this deliberately is not: a resolve spike. Purpose is an input that holds resolve up (Fleet Memory §7); making it also spike resolve on removal would be a sign inversion on one input plus a new state to author, teach and read. Revenge is a behaviour swap, not a morale event — resolve keeps falling while the board gets more dangerous, which is both of Jack’s requirements at once with no contradiction. It is transient by construction and needs no timer: it ends at reconstitution (absorbed → resumes screening) or at rout (resolve was already sagging). “Briefly” is bounded by the next Build phase, a diegetic boundary.

Trigger on the last core, not any core. While a core remains, surviving screens re-slot onto it (the surplus rule above). Otherwise cracking the first core of a shared convoy would strip protection from the remaining two and collapse the whole thing in a cascade — dramatic, but it would make shared screens too fragile to be worth fielding.

Specified feel: the wave gets louder as it loses — when it still has the nerve for it.

Orphans look battered and shapeless — the legibility answer (Jack: YES)

The risk in the orphan fork is that an aggressive orphan group and a fresh wolfpack are the same thing behaviourally, and the player must be able to tell them apart. They can, and the tell is already in the design: orphans keep their damage and have no shape, so they look battered and shapeless where a wolfpack looks deliberate. A wolfpack arrives at strength in a held formation with a clear line of attack; an orphan group is a scatter of hurt hulls with no geometry. Prototype confirms the magnitude; the kind is settled.

The sniper is what keeps the two routes honest — and the wolfpack is what keeps the sniper honest new · v2.5.0

Ruling (Jack): “I think snipers have the ability to ignore the screens. That is why they are snipers. They should also shoot slower because they are looking for the payload. A Wolfpack will confuse them because there are no payload ships.”

This closes the loop the whole section was missing. The Marksman is no longer “a cannon that prefers high-value targets” — it is the defender’s answer to the screen itself, with a cost and a counter:

  • It ignores the screen. It is not bound by the nearest-to-AoR-centre rule (cont.16) — it selects the core regardless of what is interposed. That is the definition of a sniper and it is why the decapitation route is reachable at all.
  • It pays in rate of fire. It shoots slower because it is looking for the payload — the search is the cost, and it is diegetic rather than a balance tax. So a Marksman is a low-throughput gun: it does not help you with a wave, only with a target.
  • A wolfpack confuses it. With no core in its zone the sniper has nothing to search for, so it dwells and wastes its slow shots. The wolfpack is the anti-sniper formation, and it is not a special rule — it is the sniper’s own targeting logic finding nothing to prefer.

Why this is the best structural result in the pass: it completes the symmetry. Core-hunting is answered by the screen; the screen is answered by the sniper; the sniper is answered by the wolfpack; the wolfpack has no payload, so it cannot win the battle on its own. Every element answers the one before it and none dominates. Why this makes target selection the deepest decision in the Fight

The player now has two genuinely different plans against any formation, and neither is strictly better:

  • Kill the screens. Easier — they are the hulls presented to you. It drains queue depth, thins the fleet over time, and eventually leaves a core naked. But the formation comes back at strength next sortie, and you have spent a sortie to make the fleet slightly shallower.
  • Kill the core. Harder — it is screened, back-line, and reaching it means fighting through the hulls protecting it. But it is permanent: a purpose removed from the fleet forever, plus a handful of damaged orphans that are cheap kills next sortie, plus one step toward purpose collapse and the win.

So the screen is not padding — it is a formation buying time for its core with hulls the queue will have to replace. And the Marksman, the back-line target personality, and every fight-through-the-screen problem in the design now sit on a line that runs all the way to the win condition. Damage is temporary; purpose is permanent.

A second-order effect worth authoring for: because refills are fresh and orphans are damaged, a late-battle fleet becomes visibly patchwork — some formations at strength, some limping with borrowed hulls, some coreless and unwilling. That patchiness is the fading fleet (Victory §4b) rendered as composition rather than as numbers, and it is readable at a glance from formation shape and motion.

Why a formation is not just one big ship — the question that justifies hull count new ·

If a four-hull clump counts as one contact and reads as one object, the obvious challenge is: why not simply ship a single tougher hull and save the entity count? The answer is that a formation and a big ship are different kinds of thing, and the difference is the whole reason hull count is a mechanic rather than decoration. Two reasons, both Jack's.

1 · A formation has internal composition — so it is a destructible structure, not a health bar

A contact is rarely four of the same hull. It is four escorts around a Transport, or six hulls screening a carrier, or a Bulwark front with soft hulls behind. That means the player is not choosing whether to shoot it — they are choosing what inside it to shoot, and the choices lead to different battles:

  • Kill the payload first and the escorts are suddenly an escort for nothing — still dangerous, but no longer an objective. The land wave never happens; the bomber cloud never launches.
  • Kill the escorts first and the payload is exposed but has been advancing the whole time you spent on its screen. You may have won the exchange and lost the race.
  • Kill the anchor (§4) and the formation's coordination and resolve collapse into a field promotion, which is a different lever again.

A single big hull has none of this. It has one HP pool, so the only question it poses is "shoot it or don't," and the only way to make it harder is to give it more HP — which is exactly the treadmill the whole design refuses (§3, Durability). Composition is what makes a formation a puzzle instead of a number, and it is why the answer to "more pressure" is always more structure, never more health.

2 · Every hull has its own mind — so the formation can come apart

Resolve is a per-ship network, not a group scalar (§4). Each hull carries its own resolve, is pulled toward its neighbours', and listens to the anchor's broadcast. So a formation is a coalition that can fail: one hull crosses its rout threshold, its neighbours are dragged toward the same edge, and the shape unravels. A big ship cannot waver, cannot be bluffed, and cannot be broken — it can only be killed. Every mechanic in the resolve layer requires bodies to run through, and a formation is where those bodies live at a cost of one contact.

This is also why the fear grid and the intensity dial have anything to act on. A single hull either advances or it does not; a four-hull clump skirts, hesitates, sags aft, and comes apart in a readable order. The motion signature is only rich because there are several hulls to read it across.

The two reasons meet: FRAGMENTATION as an authorable tactic

Group tracking degrades when common fate is disrupted — so a formation that breaks apart mid-approach converts from one contact into one contact per hull, and does it at the worst possible moment for the player. This is not a bug in the contact model; it is the sharpest thing the model produces, and it should be authored deliberately.

It arrives two ways, and they feel completely different:

  • Involuntary — a rout cascade. You broke them, the shape dissolves, and you are suddenly reading five fleeing hulls instead of one wave. The attention cost is the price of your own success, and it is fair: they are running, so most of that new load is ignorable. But not all of it, because retreating hulls still take fire and a sunk retreater still counts (§4).
  • Voluntary — a deliberate scatter. A coordinated late-War formation can choose to break, spending its mutual support and its splash-resistance to buy the player four extra things to track. That is a real tactic with a real cost on both sides, it belongs to the high end of the Coordination axis (a shallow fleet cannot execute it), and it is the cleanest possible expression of "harder because smarter, not bigger."

Both cases give the player a legible cause and a legible answer, which is what keeps it inside the law that the player always understands what happened. And both are bounded by the same ceiling: a scatter that pushes the board past its decision-bearing budget is a board-authoring failure, not a clever wave (Victory §3).

Coordination is also a Cognition tell

A shallow (early) fleet holds loose, sloppy formations that a single area shot shreds; a deep (late) fleet holds deliberate shapes — it disperses against your splasher, columns through your breach, testudos under your marksman. So a formation's tightness reads as the fleet's cognition tier, keeping the Fight diagnostic: you can see how smart this wave is by how well it's arranged.

10The Switch-Moment Catalogue validates the domain verb

The Cannon doc asks, honestly: does the domain switch earn its rules-weight? The switch carries a lot of machinery — restartable, reversible, third-domain-longest-path — for a lever expected to be mostly set in Deploy. The doc names the Ships session as the validation pass: if the roster can't invent enough telegraphed switch-moments, the mechanic should shed rules. Here is the catalogue. The roster generates enough switch-moments to justify the domain verb's machinery — the bomber run and the transport-dock alone are staple, legible, see-it-coming decisions. The honest counter-case

If prototyping shows players almost always pre-load correctly (because the roster telegraphs so well) and almost never switch live, then the reversible / third-domain-longest-path sub-rules are carrying weight they don't earn, and the mechanic should shed them down to a simple set-in-Deploy loadout with a single expensive live override. The catalogue above justifies the switch; it does not by itself justify every sub-rule. That's a prototype question, flagged for the joint session.

11Cognition Ladders — Harder Because Smarter, Not Just Bigger

The anti-treadmill promise made concrete. For each lineage, the shallow-board and deep-board versions differ chiefly in estimator quality — how exploitable the fleet's inference is. Hull and fire-rate may tick up with depth as seasoning; the point of the rightmost column is that the mind is what actually sharpened, not the health bar.

The whole roster's difficulty curve is one sentence

From board 1 to board 190, every lineage climbs the same Cognition ladder: slower fear-decay, higher fear-resolution, verification instead of trust, longer sight, deliberate searching fire, anticipation instead of reaction. The fog never lifts — the estimator just gets less manipulable. That is a designable, theory-grounded curve, and the spine of it is Cognition — HP and fire-rate are seasoning that may rise a little, never the ladder itself. The Durability axis exists to give Piercer and Finisher a reason to exist — not to be this ladder. Keep the escalation on the mind.

The guarantee this ladder must keep — no forced playstyle

A smarter estimator is harder to exploit, never harder to beat only one way. Every escalation above has a blunt, non-trick answer: the Counter-Battery that defeats bait-and-relocate is itself defeated by simply enclosing your guns — no bait required; the Nightstalker that routes your reveal gaps is answered by more Flare/Spotter coverage; the Wolfpack Lead that reads your re-tasks is answered by simply not re-tasking under pressure. Feint and bait are the cheaper path, never the required one. If any deep-board lineage leaves the player with exactly one viable technique, it is over-tuned and gets pulled back. The promise: skill lowers the cost of winning; it never gates winning.
