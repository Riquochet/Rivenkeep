1What This Doc Is

The mini-games are a major part of the game — the "candy-crush" of Rivenkeep. This is the full design, second pass, rebuilt around Jack's rulings on v1.0.0. Headline changes: mini-games get their own rating currency (Rooks, ♜) completely separate from campaign stars; the unit of play is a group board of levels you climb, not a single drill; the battle rung is cut — battles are campaign-only; the Muster runs a 7-day plan; and there is a Stonwryt grind door that is inflation-neutral by arithmetic.

Why they exist — the on-ramp to the north star

Rivenkeep's north star is strategy is the fun — but that fun is only reachable by a player who can read the fleet, and reading is a learned skill. The mini-games are how a raw player builds that skill until the strategy game becomes intuitive. Recorded as a supporting law in The Why (§4, "skill is taught, not assumed"). They also defend two of the Why's standing tensions — they grow the reasoning that stops retry becoming brute-force, and they train the legibility-reading the system leans on as it combines.

The line that organises this whole doc — Jack, cont.27

"The campaigns should seem to be hard. Edge of Tomorrow took 300–400 days. That is a little extreme, but we do want them to fail. The mini games are the opposite. They might fail the first time, but they won't the second."

Which resolves into one sentence that decides a dozen smaller questions below: the Yard is where you succeed; the campaign is where you fail. That inversion is also what makes the whole prescription system work — a struggling player is being sent somewhere they are going to win. Full treatment in §17.

2The Non-Negotiables

Jack's requirements. These plus everything marked ruled below are the locked content; the rest is derived and correctable.

  • They build ALL the skill needed to intuit the game. Every drill teaches a real skill the campaign will demand.
  • Built entirely from the real game's mechanics. Same walls, cannons, ships, terrain, wind, enclosure as the campaign.
  • Fun, with ZERO downside. No punishment, no loss states that take something away.
  • Endlessly replayable — including in the late campaign, not just as onboarding.
  • The retention engine. "It seems like you are playing a game, but you are learning how to play the campaigns."
  • Coaching, not diagnosing. ruled "The AI isn't just telling you what is wrong, it is coaching you to be better."
  • No battles. ruled A battle is reserved entirely for the campaign.
  • No leaderboard. ruled The Yard is personal; the leaderboard is campaign-only.
  • At least one minute per level ruled — "or it is over too quickly and didn't teach anything." Longer preferred.
  • Progression: ruled beat one level, move to the next.
  • Onboarding IS the mini-games ruled — and skippable. "Who are we to tell them that they are not ready."
  • Never condescending. ruled Always a coach: gentle early, tough later, never talking down.

3The No-Downside Model

Carried from v1.0.0 unchanged in substance — the cont.27 rulings all sit comfortably inside it. Restated tightly.

The distinction the word was hiding: downside is an attempt that takes away something you held; failure is an attempt that misses its target. A drill with no failure signal teaches nothing, so the rule is not "no failure":

Failure is free, immediate, and always attributable. Nothing is ever taken.

The third clause comes from a locked red line — every loss must be attributable (Why §4) — which in the campaign is a fairness guarantee and in a drill is the entire product. A drill that says only "try again" has failed even though it honoured no-downside.

The derivation — the drills are the campaign loop minus loss aversion

The campaign is already deterministic, checkpointed at the battle, and replayable win or lose. Its only real downsides are the Strike-3 drop-back and the campaign floor, both deliberate loss-aversion instruments (Why §7). So the drills are the existing replay rule with the drop-back deleted, and nothing else changed. No new reward philosophy, and the two modes stay recognisably one game.

The GDD's "complete-or-lose" gauntlet is deleted. Two rules make no-downside felt rather than merely true: a drill never shows a defeat screen (that word stays campaign-only — the design already distinguishes "a wound, not a loss" in Victory §2), and no hidden ★★★! in the Yard, because bolting an unpredictable jackpot onto unlimited replays is a slot machine, and Why §7's own guardrail is that the randomness must be over pieces, never over the outcome.

Where the last two downsides went — and both are now deliberate

Two things in this design do take something away, and both were ruled in knowingly. The board reset clears your ♜ (§6) — Jack: "That is okay, this is just the Yard." And missing a day resets the streak in full (§13) — "This is a hard stop." Neither is inside a drill: the first is a voluntary act the player chooses, and the second lives on the calendar. The no-downside rule governs playing, and there it still holds absolutely.

4Rooks ♜ — the Yard's Own Rating

ruled The mini-games get a rating of their own, using ♜ instead of ★. The two systems are completely different: ♜ is personal, ★ is public. Mini-games never touch the leaderboard.

The name

Wanted: simple, one word, sits beside "Stars" without confusion. Three candidates, and the recommendation turns on collision rather than taste:

NameReads asVerdict
Rooks"Four Rooks on that one." "Two Rooks off a clean board."Recommended. Literally what ♜ is; zero collision anywhere in the design; one syllable; unmistakably not a star.
Keeps"Four Keeps."Best fiction fit (Rivenkeep) but collides with the fort itself — "protect the keep" already means something.
Towers"Four Towers."Collides hard — there are seven tower types in the toolkit.

Used as Rooks throughout. Rename freely; it is a label, not a mechanic.

Five Rooks, not three — and the count differs on purpose

Derivation

The campaign uses three stars because a star is a coarse public bucket — it must be comparable at a glance across millions of players, and the full-resolution number underneath it is Battle Score. A Rook is the opposite: private, and its only job is to show you moving. A personal-progress marker wants finer granularity than a public bucket, because "I had three, now I have four" is the entire feeling being sold.

So ♜ runs 1–5. Different glyph, different count, different resolution, different purpose — the "completely different" ruling made structural rather than cosmetic. It also means no line of UI can ever confuse the two.

What Rooks do and don't do

★ Stars (campaign)♜ Rooks (the Yard)

ScopePublic — feeds Battle Score and the leaderboardPersonal only — never feeds any board
PersistencePermanent; best result kept foreverKept until you reset the board, then cleared
GatesUnlocks, cannon carry, progressionOnly the next level, on a first pass (§6)

Rooks are earned against thresholds on that level's own score lever (§9) — never by surviving a sequence. 1 Rook is "cleared" and is all that unlocks the next level; Rooks 2–5 are the reason to come back to a level you already beat. Which is the design instruction §17 makes explicit: the difficulty lives in the Rooks, not in the clear.

Why the split is right and not merely tidy — the Yard is farm-proof by construction

The strongest argument for two doors is what it makes unnecessary. An unlimited drill that paid currency needs a rate limiter, a decay curve, or a daily cap — three mechanisms whose only job is to stop the player doing the thing the design wants. A door that pays nothing cannot be farmed, so it needs no gate at all, which is the one property "endlessly replayable" actually demands.

The honest cost — and cont.27 raised it rather than lowered it

With no currency in the Yard, its retention rests on the ♜ climb and the board reset. v1.0.0 cited Candy Crush as the precedent for currency-free retention. §17 examines that precedent properly and finds it only half-supports us — Candy Crush's engine is repeated failure on one level, and Jack has explicitly ruled the opposite for the Yard. So more weight sits on the reset-and-replay loop than v1.0.0 assumed. Still the first thing to measure; now with a clearer idea of what could go wrong.

6Group Boards — the Unit of Play

ruled The unit is not a drill, it is a group board: one atom, a ladder of levels, getting harder all the way up. "Each of the Atoms should be a full run of minigames where we are making it harder and harder for them."

The lifecycle — three states, and the third is the endless one

  1. First pass — locked and linear. ruled Level 1 is open; every level after it is locked until the one before is cleared (1 ♜). You walk the ladder in order. This is Introduce → Isolate → Combine enforced by the board itself.
  2. Board complete. Every level cleared. The board unlocks as a whole.
  3. Reset — new seed, free roam. ruled The player chooses to reset. It re-seeds (§10) — same 20 problems, different boards — and this time they can skip around freely. The reset also clears the board's ♜. Repeatable forever.
Why the reset clearing Rooks is the right cost — and it is the only cost in the Yard

Jack ruled it and waved it off ("this is just the Yard"), but it is doing real work and deserves defending. A reset that kept your Rooks would make the second pass pointless — there would be nothing to earn, so the re-seeded boards would be scenery. Clearing them is what makes the second pass a game rather than a tour, and it is the mechanism by which "endlessly replayable" becomes true rather than aspirational.

And it is not a downside in the sense §3 forbids, because the player chooses when it happens. Nothing takes your Rooks; you spend them, deliberately, to get the board back. Closer to a prestige reset than a punishment — and the record of what you did survives it anyway, in your highest level reached and in the campaign you can now play.

What the board looks like

A ladder, not a grid — 20 rungs climbing, each showing its ♜ out of 5 and its mind tier. Locked levels are visible but dimmed, so the climb is legible from the bottom (Why §7's Zeigarnik hook pointed at a ladder: "three more and the board is clear"). A completed board carries a single mark; a reset board shows its pass count.

Level count per board — derived, not chosen

20 levels, stepping the campaign's own difficulty range

A group's job is to carry the player from raw to campaign-competent on one atom, so its ladder should span the same difficulty range the campaign does. Campaign difficulty is cognition creep across 190 battles, so the ladder samples that range. At 20 levels each step is ~10 tiers — coarse enough that a step is felt, fine enough that no step is a wall (which §17 requires: the step must be climbable in one retry).

Cross-check from the other side: a 20-level first pass is 20 × 72–180 s ≈ 24–60 minutes, which lands on Why §7's campaign-loop band. A group board is one sitting's commitment — the same size as the thing it is teaching. Not fitted to; it fell out.

Rung 3, specified — Jack's two shapes, and there turn out to be three

ruled "We create a castle, the player deploys and fights. Or we build and deploy, the player fights and builds." The principle underneath: the player plays exactly two phases and the game supplies the rest, which is what keeps rung 3 from collapsing into rung 4. Jack's second shape names a seam v1.0.0 missed entirely, and it is the valuable one.

Seam drillGame suppliesPlayer playsThe lesson
S1 · The CourtyardNothing up front; runs the Fight at the end for you to watchBuild → DeployThe ring you built decides how many guns can sit and how many commands they form. A tight ring is cheap and seats nothing. Deploy §4's granularity premium, paid in wall.

S3 is why cutting the battle rung cost less than expected

v1.0.0 argued for a battle rung because the rebuild tax — the campaign's real late-game difficulty — was undrilled. Jack's Fight→Build pairing drills it directly: the tax is a two-phase seam, and it was hiding in plain sight because v1.0.0 only looked at seams in forward order (Build→Deploy→Fight) and never at the wrap-around.

What is still not drilled, now deliberately: the multi-sortie race — the tax compounding against the queue's drain across 3–10 sorties. That needs a battle, battles are campaign-only, and it is therefore the one skill the Yard hands off. A clean division rather than a gap: the Yard teaches you to pay the tax; the campaign teaches you whether you can afford it.

The timer point. ruled "The build, deploy would have to have some kind of timer, otherwise it is just all 3 phases." Correct, and it generalises: every played phase in a rung-3 drill runs its real clock (Build 54 s, Deploy by formula, Fight 54/70 s). Untimed phases would let the player grind out an optimum — the north-star failure Build §2 names — and would also stretch a two-phase drill until it was indistinguishable from a full run.

Rung 2, specified — why the phase is played twice

Two rulings collide: a level lasts at least a minute, and it should use the real battle clock so the player gets used to it. But a real Build is 54 s — under the minute. Rather than invent a third clock, a rung-2 level is the phase run twice with the mind tier stepped up between passes: 108 s, both clocks real, second pass harder than the first. Better teaching than one long pass, because the step is where the learning is.

"The camera trade is just a larger board" — Jack, and it closes a hole cleanly

v1.0.0's CALL-OUT 6 worried that the camera trade was a genuine skill no drill isolated, and floated a drill where the only decision was when to zoom. Jack's reading is better and dissolves the problem: the trade is not a skill with a technique, it is a consequence of board size. Give a drill a board several times the screen and the trade appears by itself — no drill authored, no new mechanic. It becomes the board-size lever in §10, which is also why board size is a difficulty axis rather than a cosmetic setting.

"Territory / DMZ pinning — not sure what this is"

Fair — it is buried in Deploy §3. In short: the DMZ line advances one grid per sortie, squeezing your buildable ground — and a cannon or castle standing forward pins it where it stands. A forward gun also holds the fragile grid beneath it stable. Both effects last only while the gun stands there: move it or lose it and the line resumes advancing and the ground goes fragile again. So siting a gun forward is a territorial act — it trades growth and safety (an enclosed gun is a ghost to the fleet and collects the enclosure bonus every cycle) for ground and reach.

Why it stays a lever rather than a group: the decision is real, but it is one branch of the placement decision D2 already teaches (where you put the gun), not a separate skill with its own read. Switching the DMZ squeeze on in D2's later levels teaches it where a player will actually meet it. Flagged honestly — this is the atom I am least sure about demoting; see CALL-OUT 3.

It looks like a broken drill — a Fight you cannot fight. That is the point. Rungs 3 and 4

S1 · The Courtyard · S2 · The Plan · S3 · The Tax — specified in §7. Each ends with a consequence trace: a sentence, not a score breakdown — "your ring was 4 cells tighter than the courtyard needed, so the third gun had nowhere to sit, so the east bearing went uncovered, so both transports landed."

R4 · The Sortie — one complete Build → Deploy → Fight under an authored constraint: pentominoes only, no spackle, gale-force wind, all fragile ground, one cannon type. The GDD's Full Siege. The constraint is what makes it a drill rather than a short battle: it forces one specific muscle to carry the whole loop.

Why rung 3 needs the consequence trace, and rungs 1, 2 and 4 do not

Rung 3 is the one rung with no natural engagement loop under it — a seam is not an activity, it is the causal link between two activities, and the game gives that link no moment of its own. That is exactly why the GDD's seed only implied it. So its hook has to be authored, and the obvious authored hook is the seam's own subject matter: the causal line, stated out loud. The other rungs have their own loops (a beat, a phase, a sortie) and need no such prop.

10Levers, Seeds, and Where the Thousands Come From

ruled "We have difficulty levers in the battle. Use them. We can also modify the size of the board. There are so many levers that we should have 1000s of minigames predefined."

Two axes, and only one of them is difficulty

Axis 1 — the mind tier. Difficulty-as-cognition applied to a drill. Campaign difficulty is a fine, continuous, monotonic per-battle creep across 190 battles, so:

tier = battle index, 1 – 190. A group's 20 levels step it by ~10.

No number invented, and three things fall out free: late-game relevance (a Campaign-23 player meets tier-~130 minds and drills at tier ~130, so the Yard is never beneath them ruled); calibratable coaching (the tier means the same thing on both sides of the door — §14); and one honest rating per group, highest level cleared.

Axis 2 — the levers. Not difficulty tiers; variety. Every one already in the game:

LeverRangeWhat it changes
Board size~3 bands, up to 128×80Pan and zoom distance — and therefore the camera trade (F·h). This is why board size is a real axis, not a setting.
Wind4 strengths × 8 directionsDrift and drift-stop. Diagonals force two-axis compensation.
Piece tier16 tiers / 8 groupsWhich polyominoes the draw hardens toward.
Theater10Fragile ground, rock/tree/cliff density, fog, DMZ behaviour, bounce (Crystal).
Constraint set~4No spackle, one cannon type, pentominoes only, friendly fire on.
Where "thousands" actually comes from — and it is not thousands of authored levels

20 groups × 20 levels = 400 authored level templates — a real but tractable job (the campaign needs 190 battlefields by comparison). The thousands come from templates × lever settings: one template instantiated across board sizes, wind states, piece bands, theaters and constraints is dozens of distinct problems, so 400 templates is comfortably five figures of playable levels without hand-authoring one of them.

The important part: this is not procedural generation of difficulty. The tier ladder is authored and the levers are a finite, hand-checkable set — so every level is a designed problem, which is what keeps the ≥2-solutions law enforceable.

Seeds ruled

  • One seed per group instance — the whole board of 20 levels is instantiated from a single seed, so the levels within it are a coherent set rather than 20 unrelated rolls.
  • Reset re-seeds. Same 20 problems, different boards. This is the randomness, and it is the endless axis.
  • Seeds are NOT global. Players do not share a seed. Which is the mechanical reason there could be no Yard leaderboard even if anyone wanted one — there is no common board to compare on. Jack's "no leaderboard" ruling and the seed model agree from different directions.
  • The Muster is the one exception and needs it: today's Muster drill is a single fixed seed, so the day's challenge is a shared thing to talk about. It still feeds no board.
The choreography law, and where it binds

The campaign's law: piece-pressure and cognition never turn together — one screw per campaign, or the combined load blows the legibility ceiling (Build §11, Why §6). Applied here it splits on isolation: on rungs 1–2 a lever and the tier may rise together, because one isolated atom under two screws is still one thing to read. On rungs 3–4 only one screw turns at a time, exactly as in the campaign. Nice property: the upper rungs behave like the campaign because they are the campaign in miniature, and the lower rungs can be tuned harder than the campaign ever would be.

11Durations and the Clock

Level durations — multiples of 18 seconds

The constraint that resolves both rulings at once

Build's clock displays in days at 18 s per day. A drill clock that is not a multiple of 18 s displays as a fractional day — exactly the "fuzzy 3.06" the Build clock moved from 55 to 54 s to avoid. So every drill clock is a multiple of 18 s, and the one-minute floor rounds up to the next legal value, 72 s.

RungClockReads asDerivation

A first pass up a 20-level board is therefore 24 minutes (rung 1) to 60 minutes (rung 4).

The display — the sweep is out, and the fix is an 18-hour day

ruled "The clock should be a big number and a small number. The sweep is too distracting."

Why the sweep existed, and the one change that removes it for free

The sweep came from an arithmetic accident: a siege day is 18 s and a real day has 24 hours, so the hour figure had to tick every 18 ÷ 24 = 0.75 s — faster than a second hand. That was originally read as a feature (a small number visibly racing). Jack's ruling says it reads as distraction, which is the right call for a phase whose whole premise is deliberate reading rather than hurry.

The fix costs nothing but a line of fiction: make the siege day 18 hours long. Then one hour is exactly one second, the small number ticks at the calmest rate a clock can have, and the sweep is gone by construction rather than by tuning.

1 siege day = 18 hours = 18 real seconds · 1 hour = 1 second, exactly

Big number: days, 3 → 2 → 1 → 0 on a Build (4 → 0 on a rung-1 level). Small number: hours, 17 → 0 within each day, resetting as the day ticks. Nothing races, both numbers are legible at a glance, and the siege framing survives — an 18-hour day is free in a fantasy world and not a number any player will interrogate.

⚠ This is a change to Build §2, not just to the Yard

Build §2 currently specifies a 24-hour day and the 0.75 s sweep, with the mismatch called out as deliberate. The ruling reverses that, so Build §2 owes an edit: 24 hours → 18, and the "faster than a real second, and that mismatch is the point" paragraph comes out. Nothing mechanical moves — 54 s is still 3 days, the tax table still reads in days, every derived figure is untouched. Flagged as an owner-change rather than made from here, since the Build doc was not open this session.

Should rung 1 be time-limited at all?

CALL-OUT · Attempt-limits versus time-limits at rung 1

Raising this because the evidence points somewhere the design is not currently pointing. Candy Crush's timed levels were mostly 1:00 or 0:30 (hard ones 1:30; the full range 0:10 to 3:00) — and King removed timed levels entirely in 2018 after negative player reception, judging them difficult and user-unfriendly. Modern Candy Crush is move-limited. So our 72 s floor is well-calibrated against what a level should last, but the mechanism bounding it is the one the genre's biggest title deleted.

There is already precedent inside our own design: the GDD's Efficient Enclosure has no timer and bounds itself with a piece budget. And §18 establishes that a drill may relax a campaign law when isolation is the point. So an alternative rung-1 model exists: bound by attempts, not seconds — n pieces in B1, n calls in F1/F2, n shots in F3 — still averaging 72 s in practice but never cutting a player off mid-thought.

Recommendation is a split, along a line the design already draws: time-limit the drills whose atom is itself time-pressured (B4 The Re-Plug, F3 The Alpha, F5 The Cue — all three are about acting in time), and attempt-limit the pure reads (B2, D1, F1, F2, F4). Rungs 2–4 keep the real battle clock unconditionally, because getting used to it is the point. A real fork rather than a detail: it decides whether rung 1 feels like a test or a batting cage.

The grind door — it works, and it fits in the gap the plan just created

ruled, optional Jack's reference: "I play Brick n Balls and one of the mini games I have to play about 10–15 games to earn 150 coins. The minimum purchase is 200 coin." The shape being described is a grind deliberately just short of a purchase — one session gets you most of the way, so you come back.

Inflation-neutral by arithmetic rather than by promise

The Long Muster: a side door on the Muster screen where any group-board level can be played for Stonwryts, at a flat rate, capped by the day's remaining headroom under 250.

  • The rate: 6 Stonwryts per level cleared. Derived, not chosen — 6 is the cheapest purchase in the game (Reinforce Cannon, +1%), so one level cleared buys exactly one nudge. The cleanest possible statement of what a grind play is worth, and legible without a tutorial.
  • The cap: 100 per day = the 700/week the 7-day plan handed back, divided by seven. So the grind spends headroom the plan created, and the week's ceiling still lands under the GDD's 1,750. Nothing inflates, and the proof is subtraction rather than an assurance.
  • The feel: 100 ÷ 6 ≈ 17 levels ≈ 20 minutes for 100 Stonwryts — against Battle Surge at 93 and Early Cleanup at 235. A full grind session buys one good play, or gets you most of the way to a big one. Compare the reference: 10–15 games for 150 against a 200 minimum. Same order, same shape, same "one more" pull.
The best property of it, and it is accidental

The cap is remaining headroom, so the grind is available exactly when a player wants it. On a rung-1 day, 5♜ pays 100 and leaves 100 to grind. On a rung-4 day, 5♜ pays 250 and there is nothing left. So the grind opens on the short easy days and closes on the big ones — precisely the "some days you are just too busy for a full play" case Jack raised under the streak. The busy player grinds; the invested player musters; both hit the same ceiling.

The one thing to watch

A grind door makes it possible to reach the day's cap without ever playing the day's Muster drill — letting a player farm rung 1 forever and never climb. Two cheap guards, and I would take the first: the Long Muster opens once today's Muster drill has been attempted (attempted, not cleared — no downside, and an attempt is an honest ask). Alternative if that reads as coercive: the grind rate halves on levels below the player's highest cleared rung, so grinding your comfort zone pays worse than grinding your edge. See CALL-OUT 2.

13The Streak

ruled "The streak is just to get them opening the app and playing/completing SOMETHING. There are days where you are just too busy for a full play. A training ground run is just fine." And: "Miss a day and it is a full reset. This is a hard stop."

  • Any completion counts — a campaign battle, a Muster drill, or a single Yard level. The streak measures showing up, not achievement.
  • Miss a day, full reset to Day 1. No decay, no shield, no grace. This reverses v1.0.0's recommendation (decay, on the campaign-floor precedent) — Jack's call, and defensible on its own terms: the value of a streak is exactly the fear of losing it, and a decaying streak is not really a streak. The permanent DiMaggio shield badges already survive a break, so nothing earned is destroyed; only the counter resets.
⚠ The consequence this ruling has, and it lands on the economy doc

The GDD's streak rewards are not only Stonwryts — they include gameplay buffs that compound across the 14-day cycle (+1 s, +2 s, +3 s Build timer; +1% to +5% easier pieces; +1 spackle; +2 s Last Stand bank; cannon enclosure bonuses), stabilising at 2× Day 14 indefinitely.

With a 72-second Yard level qualifying as a day's completion, those buffs become maintainable by any player willing to open the app once a day — which is nearly free. That is not currency inflation, but it is inflation: a permanent +3 s on a 54 s clock is a 5.5% larger Build phase forever, and "+5% easier pieces" reaches into the piece-tier curve Build §7 is carefully choreographing.

The honest reading: if the streak's job is retention rather than achievement — and Jack has now ruled that it is — its rewards should be retention-flavoured, not mechanical. Stonwryts and shields, yes. Buffs to the Build clock and the piece draw, no: those are difficulty dials the choreography law owns. Recommendation for the economy session: move the streak's reward table off gameplay buffs and onto currency, cosmetics and shields. Flagged rather than acted on — the streak table is the economy doc's.

14The Coach — Prescription, the Roster, and Tone

ruled "The AI isn't just telling you what is wrong, it is coaching you to be better." A change of role, not of feature — and it needs no new AI capability.

The machinery already exists

  • The post-battle debrief already reads a structured summary the deterministic sim emits at battle end, is already tone-adaptive off progression state, and is already advisory-only — it cannot touch stars or score.
  • The 3-loss hint trigger already exists (Why §7's anti-frustration table; GDD Tactical Hints).
  • The AI guardrail already draws the line: the logic stays deterministic and authored; the model generates framing only.

So the addition is one field on the debrief — it may attach a starting point on a group board — and a deterministic table picks it while the model writes the sentence. Guardrail intact by construction.

A starting point, not a drill ruled

The distinction is the whole ruling. The AI does not hand over one level; it hands over a rung on a ladder, and the player climbs from there. That is what makes it coaching: a coach does not set you one exercise, they put you into a programme at the right weight.

The entry point sits one tier below the board that is beating you.
Why one tier, and only one

The flow channel (Why §7, Csikszentmihalyi) says keep the player between boredom and anxiety: someone failing at tier N is above the channel, so the entry must sit below it. But the drop need only be one tier, because isolation is itself the large difficulty reduction — the Isolate step of Introduce → Isolate → Combine is the relief. Dropping further puts the entry under the channel and makes it boring, wasting the one moment a player is willing to be taught. And because the entry is a ladder rather than a level, the player climbs back past the tier that beat them, which is the actual repair.

The Roster — where suggestions live ruled

"We should also have a place to store these suggestions so that the player can come back later and see what the AI suggested. The player can either play them or just delete them from his roster."

  • A persistent list on the Yard screen. Each entry: the group, the entry level, one line of why, and the date suggested.
  • Two actions only — play it, or dismiss it. No third state, no snooze, no expiry.
  • Nothing accumulates silently. A group already on the Roster is never re-added; a new loss on the same skill updates the existing entry's level instead. Otherwise a struggling player opens the Yard to a wall of evidence about how badly they are doing — the exact opposite of the intent.
  • Dismissal is permanent and free — no confirmation, no re-suggestion of that entry. The player's judgment about their own weaknesses outranks the table's.

Tone — the rule and the test ruled

"This should always be like a coach. In the beginning, it might be gentle, but in later stages, it might get tough. It should NEVER be condescending."

A register that scales, and a checkable line

The register scales with demonstrated competence — a signal the debrief already receives (progression state, star history, retry count). Early: explanatory and warm — "Your rings held fine. The guns had nowhere to sit — want to look at courtyards?" Late: terse and demanding, the way a coach talks to someone who knows the sport — "You centred forward three sorties running against a screen you'd already read. Deep centre. The Centre, tier 130."

The never-condescending line, made testable: a line is condescending if it explains something the player has already demonstrated. That is checkable against the same progression state the register scales on — so it is a rule the implementation can enforce rather than a tone note in a doc. Two corollaries: critique the play, never the player (the summary describes decisions, so the sentence should too), and never soften a real problem to be kind — a coach who won't tell you what is wrong is not being gentle, they are being useless. Rivenkeep's own working rule on honesty says the same thing about this project, and it should say it to the player too.

Bounds, unchanged: offered once per stall, dismissible, never more than one per battle, never gating. Retry is always one tap away, and a player who ignores every suggestion for a whole campaign has their path unchanged.

15Onboarding — the Mini-Games Are the Tutorial

ruled "Onboarding should be the minigames. If the player doesn't do anything, then we give them a finger that shows them what to do."

A tutorial is by definition one new thing at a time — which is the Introduce step, which is exactly what a rung-1 level is. So the teaching content and the drill content are the same content, and it only gets designed once.

The Induction — a ~10-level sampler ruled

"We should have a small set of each of the rungs that demonstrates the game. Maybe like 10? But the player can skip all of that and start the battles or daily challenges. Who are we to tell them that they are not ready."

Ten levels, ~15 minutes, walking the ladder in miniature so the structure is learned alongside the mechanics. Every level is a real group-board level 1, so nothing here is bespoke tutorial content needing separate maintenance — the Induction is a playlist, not a mode.

Skippable, and it means skippable

A single always-visible skip: no confirmation, no warning, no "we recommend finishing this first." Straight to the campaign or the Muster. And no consequence — the Induction levels stay on their boards, so a player who skips and then struggles finds the same content waiting, and the Coach (§14) will point at it by name.

Worth stating because it is the tempting thing to get wrong: a skip that nags is not a skip. Jack's line is the design rule — who are we to tell them that they are not ready.

The finger — the idle prompt ruled

If the player does nothing, the game shows them what to do. Escalating, and every stage already has a home in the design:

  1. ~4 s idle — the target element pulses (Build's existing ghost-preview and magnify-halo language; no new render vocabulary).
  2. ~8 s idle — a pointing finger traces the gesture: the drag path, the rotate tap, the drop point. It traces it rather than describing it.
  3. ~12 s idle — the AI contextual tutor speaks one line, in the Coach's register. The existing mechanic, which already handles cases like "a player who hasn't rotated in 8 seconds hears: try tapping the piece to rotate it."

The finger is demonstration before explanation — the right order for a spatial game, and the reason it comes before the text. It stops the instant the player acts: a nudge, never a script to sit through.

Why the idle prompt belongs to rung 1 and nowhere else

An idle timer on a timed drill is a contradiction — the clock is already the prompt. So the finger runs only where a player can be genuinely stuck rather than merely slow: the Induction and the first pass of a rung-1 board. From rung 2 up the phase clock does the job, and a player who is idle at rung 2 is thinking, which is the behaviour the whole game exists to reward.

17Failure Philosophy — the Yard Succeeds, the Campaign Fails

One inversion, and it decides a dozen smaller questions

The Yard is where you succeed. The campaign is where you fail.

Which is why the whole prescription system works: a struggling player is being sent somewhere they are going to win. A coach who answers a losing streak by handing you something else hard is not coaching, and a drill as punishing as the thing it prepares you for has no reason to exist. It also explains, retroactively, why no-downside was never in tension with teaching: the Yard does not need loss aversion to make you care, because the campaign has already done that.

Three things it settles concretely: the tier step must be climbable in one retry (which sizes the ~10-tier step in §10); the Coach's entry point sits below the failure rather than at it; and 1 Rook is easy while 5 is not — clearing a level should be the expected outcome of a second attempt, with Rooks 2–5 carrying the real challenge. That last one is a direct instruction for the threshold-setting session, and it is the one to hold onto: the difficulty lives in the Rooks, not in the clear.

What the Candy Crush comparison actually says

Jack asked how long a Candy Crush level is. The answer is more useful than expected, and it cuts three ways.

  • On length, we are well calibrated. Candy Crush's timed levels were mostly 1:00 or 0:30, hard ones 1:30, the full range 0:10 to 3:00. Our 72–180 s sits inside that and above Jack's one-minute floor — so the "over too quickly to teach anything" worry is answered by the genre's own numbers rather than by intuition.
  • On mechanism, we are in the mode the genre deleted. King removed timed levels entirely in 2018 after negative player reception, judging them difficult and user-unfriendly; modern Candy Crush is move-limited. That is the evidence behind §11's CALL-OUT.
  • On retention, the precedent only half-supports us — and this is the important one. Candy Crush's actual engine is repeated failure on a single level: a pro player reports 7–10 days and 100–150 lives to pass a new hard level. That is loss aversion at maximum, monetised through lives and boosters. Jack has ruled the exact opposite for the Yard.
The honest conclusion — the Yard is not a Candy Crush loop, and it should stop being described as one

Calling the mini-games "our candy-crush" is right about their role — the daily habit, the endless supply, the thing you open the app for — and wrong about their engine. Candy Crush retains through the sting of near-miss failure against a wall you cannot pass. The Yard explicitly removes that, so its retention has to come from the two things left: the Rook climb and the board reset.

That is a thinner engine than v1.0.0 assumed when it cited the genre as precedent, and the closer analogue is not a match-3 at all — it is a practice mode, or a batting cage. People do return to those voluntarily, but for a reason Candy Crush does not rely on: the visible transfer of skill to the real thing. Which is fine, and arguably better, but it means the load-bearing claim moves. It is no longer "the climb is compelling"; it is "the player can feel the Yard making them better at the campaign."

That is measurable, and it is now the first thing to measure in prototype: does a player who drills F1 for twenty minutes notice their next battle reading differently? If yes, the Yard needs nothing else. If no, no amount of Rook polish will save it — and the honest fix would be to move some reward into the Yard, which reopens every inflation question §12 just closed by subtraction.

19CALL-OUTs — Open Judgment Calls

v1.0.0's six are all closed by Jack's rulings. Three new ones, all narrower than the last set.

1 · Attempt-limits or time-limits at rung 1

Full treatment in §11. The genre's biggest title deleted timed levels; our own GDD already has an untimed, budget-bounded pack. Recommendation: split by atom — time-limit the three drills whose skill is acting in time (B4, F3, F5), attempt-limit the pure reads (B2, D1, F1, F2, F4), keep the real battle clock unconditionally at rungs 2–4. A real fork: it decides whether rung 1 feels like a test or a batting cage.

2 · Does the grind door need an attempt gate?

§12. A player can reach the day's cap by grinding rung 1 and never touch the day's Muster drill, farming their comfort zone forever. Recommendation: the Long Muster opens once today's Muster drill has been attempted — attempted, not cleared, so still no downside. Alternative if that reads as coercive: halve the grind rate below the player's highest cleared rung. Or accept it, on the grounds that a player grinding rung 1 for currency is at least still drilling.

3 · Is D·f (territory / the forward gun) really a lever?

§8. Demoted into D2's later levels because it is one branch of the placement decision D2 already teaches. But it is a genuinely distinct trade — ground and reach versus growth and safety — with its own read (how fast is the DMZ coming, and can I afford to garrison that gun), and it is the atom I am least confident about folding in. If prototype shows it is its own read, it wants its own group, and the drill writes itself: hold the line, or hide the gun.

Streak — any completion counts including a single Yard level; full reset on a missed day (⚠ with the honest consequence flagged: streak gameplay buffs are now nearly free and should move to currency/cosmetics — economy doc's call). The Coach — attaches an entry point on a ladder rather than a drill, one tier below the failure; the persistent Roster (play or dismiss, never accumulating, dismissal permanent and free); the fog case wired into the mapping table; and a testable never-condescending rule — a line is condescending if it explains something the player has already demonstrated. Onboarding — the mini-games are the tutorial; a 10-level Induction that is a playlist rather than a mode, skippable with no nag ("a skip that nags is not a skip"); the escalating idle prompt ending in the pointing finger, scoped to the Induction and rung-1 first passes only. Wind promoted back to its own group (B5 The Drift) — Jack's reversal is right: drift-stop is a technique with a right answer, where board size and theater are conditions. The camera trade resolved as a board-size lever, closing v1.0.0's CALL-OUT 6. Territory/DMZ explained and demoted to a D2 lever, flagged as the least confident cut. Failure philosophy — "the Yard is where you succeed, the campaign is where you fail," which sizes the tier step, places the Coach's entry point, and rules that the difficulty lives in the Rooks, not in the clear. The Candy Crush question answered, cutting three ways: our 72–180 s is well inside the genre's level lengths; King deleted timed levels in 2018 after negative reception, now a live CALL-OUT about attempt-limits at rung 1; and Candy Crush's real engine is repeated failure on one level, which Jack has explicitly inverted — so the Yard is not a Candy Crush loop, and the load-bearing claim shifts from "the climb is compelling" to "the player can feel the Yard making them better."
