1What This Doc Owns

Three docs already describe pieces of the Fight — Cannon & Tower owns the verbs and the AoR, Fleet Memory owns the mind you are reading, Victory owns how a sortie resolves. Nobody owned the experience of playing it: what your eyes and hands actually do from 3-2-1-FIGHT to the wave's withdrawal. That is this doc.

The charter — connective tissue, not new mechanics

This doc does not re-specify the three command verbs (that is Cannon & Tower §6), the three-channel mind (Fleet Memory), the roster's in-Fight behaviour (Ships), or the sortie end-states (Victory §4–6). Where it needs one of those, it links. What it owns is the connective layer none of them could own alone: (1) the tempo of a sortie, (2) the assembled grid-read, (3) the attention economy, and (4) reading the ending before it is stamped.

Why it must exist as its own doc

The Fight is where the whole edifice is either a thinking experience or a plate-spinning one, and that verdict lives in the seams between the other docs, not inside any one of them. The AoR gradient (Cannon), the motion signature (Ships), and the fear haze (Fleet Memory) are each legible alone — the open question is whether they compose into one readable picture under real-time load. That question is this doc's to answer, and its answers are targets the prototype confirms — never a place we build a second copy of a mechanic.

Governance

Everything here serves The Why §2 (the Fight rewards reading, never reflexes) and §6 tension #5 (the Fight is the one arcade risk). The Fight is the “watch it run” step of Fleet Memory's debugging loop: Deploy wrote the plan, the Fight reveals it, the next Deploy fixes it. If any decision in this doc could be won by clicking faster, it is wrong.

2The Spine — In One Breath

The Fight is the reveal of a committed plan, read through a legible-but-blind mind, nudged by three costed verbs — where the scarce resource is attention, not actions, and the primary live skill is timing a single withheld signal.

You watch and hold; you command deliberately

Auto-fire runs your plan on its own (Cannon & Tower — Firepower). For most of a sortie you are reading, not acting — diagnosing whether the plan you committed in Deploy is meeting the mind you drew. But the verbs are not only an emergency valve: because the battle is deterministic, its threats arrive on a known, spaced schedule, and a skilled commander plans a sequence of verb-actions in advance and conducts them on cue. Reaching for a verb can mean “my plan is breaking, fix it” — or it can mean “this is the moment my planned maneuver fires.” Both are command; neither is a scramble.

The verbs are a planned command language — you conduct a known score

This is the point to be unmistakable about (Jack): re-tasking an AoR and switching a domain can be part of the plan, not just reactions to a broken one. Suppose you have learned the wave: ships hit the north sea first, then troops the east land, then bombers the north air. Because those events are spaced in time, a single group can answer all three — cover the north loaded for sea → re-task east and switch to land as the troops approach → re-task back north and switch to air before the bombers arrive. One group, walking a rehearsed route on schedule. That is direct command of your cannons, the debug-loop payoff (you learned the score, now you conduct it) — the exact opposite of plate-spinning.

Replay is strategy, not a mulligan — and the unit is the battle (Jack)

Win or lose, you can replay, and that is a legitimate strategic tool, not a consolation. The checkpoint is always the battle — a battle runs as waves of sorties, and a sortie as its phases (Build → Deploy → Fight), but those subdivisions exist for pacing and for how we talk about the game, not as save points. You re-run the whole battle, not a lone phase or sortie. It falls straight out of determinism: the same committed plans against the same seed reproduce the same battle, so a replay is a chance to execute the read better across it — time the held alpha you fumbled, start a crawl earlier, spend a domain switch on cue. This is the Edge-of-Tomorrow loop: the battle is the puzzle, and replaying it is how you solve it better. It is fair by the same logic as the leaderboard — everyone gets the identical battle and the identical chance to master it; skill is the ceiling you reach, not the luck you drew. Because the battle is also the scored unit (Battle Score), replay needs no special scoring rule: a re-run simply produces another battle result.

The one live skill is timing, and timing is a decision

The verb the commander leans on most is fire-control — mostly holding, then spending the held signal at the right instant (Fleet Memory — the Fight is mostly holding fire). That is a when, not a how-fast: a held alpha released a half-second early or late costs you the shot, but no amount of dexterity substitutes for having read the moment right. And the same is true of the planned maneuvers above — their skill is scheduling the crawl and the reload to land on time, not flicking them fast. This is the concrete line from The Why: success comes from what you decided, never from how fast you can act.

3The Shape of a Sortie

A sortie is not a flat stretch of chaos to survive — it has a beat structure over its duration. Naming the beats is what proves the Fight is deliberate rather than frantic: long passages of reading, punctuated by a few costed nudges.

The point of drawing the shape

The shape is mostly read beats with acting concentrated where decisions live — the held alpha and the planned/corrective maneuvers. But the anti-plate-spinning guarantee is not “few act-beats”: a sortie may be busy with commands and still be pure strategy, so long as those commands were plannable against known, spaced threats (see §6). The real red line is forced simultaneity — the shape has collapsed only if the board demands two costed moves at once or a move faster than the spacing allows. Conduct a busy score you rehearsed: fine. Scramble at surprises under a clock: failure.

4The Grid-Read — Everything On Screen, Composed

The heart of the connective tissue. Each source doc renders one layer; the player fuses them into a single read in real time. The design job is not the individual layers (they are owned elsewhere) — it is that they compose into one picture a person can hold without a spreadsheet.

Your side — the hard rail

  • AoR — three render states, escalating with how much you need to see — to keep the board from drowning in colour, an AoR shows only as much as the moment calls for (Cannon §4): at rest, a light outline of the grid squares it covers (extent only); while firing but unselected, the outline plus its bright core and outer boundary ring (enough to read “this zone is hot, here's its reliable centre and its reach” live — so a fringe miss is fair without a tap); when you tap a group, the full centre-to-edge gradient over the water/fog. So coverage extent is always readable, live reliability is readable on any firing group, and the complete gradient is summoned for the one you inspect — reserving the colour channel for the fogs until you ask.
  • The launch-zone dead-holes — each spawn's no-fire corridor punched into the coverage (Cannon §3), so you never expect a shot that can't connect.
  • Ammo, felt — the wave's fire visibly slackening as magazines empty (Victory §5). Never a numeric bar; you reason “they're nearly spent.”
  • Cannon health — a clean board until it isn't — in the Fight, a cannon shows no health bar until it takes a hit; the first damage brings the bar in, with the health colours (green → yellow → red, plus the veteran overlay) appearing right then (Cannon §8). By the time a bar is on screen it is because something is wounded and worth your eye — so a battered group announces itself, and an untouched board stays quiet. And a gun lost outright shrinks its group's AoR (fewer barrels = smaller zone and thinner core — Cannon §3), so your coverage visibly contracts as guns die: a second, spatial read of damage on top of the health colour. (Build and Deploy still show every cannon's bar and exact % — that is where you make protect decisions.)

Their side — soft, read through two fogs

The player reasons under two distinct uncertainties, and Fleet Memory §8 requires they render in visibly different visual languages so they are never conflated:

  • Board fog — what you can't see, spoken in opacity: fully opaque where you're blind, opened only by a transparent-centre / opaque-edge reveal lens on fog theaters (§7). Blindness on your side.
  • Mind fog — what the fleet knows of your fort, spoken in saturation (the distinct language from board fog — opacity vs colour never conflate): your ground sits in twilight where the fleet hasn't confirmed it and jumps to noon the instant it opens a sightline or lands a hit there. That twilight→noon gradient is the exposure read — the one hard rail on their side (an enclosed gun stays twilight, a ghost). Floating soft on top of it: the fear haze (tiered — where they're scared) and decay-as-behaviour (a bluff fading, read as ships edging back into a cooling flank).
  • The always-visible constants — regardless of either fog, every spawn point and its safe launch zone, and all DMZ / area-denial grids, stay fully visible and at full colour every sortie. You are never blind to where a wave emerges or to impassable ground.
The saturation doubles as a battle-health gauge — and the breach sun-ray (Jack)

Because mind fog brightens the fort as the fleet confirms it, the overall saturation of your board becomes an at-a-glance read on how the battle is going: a fort still mostly in twilight means the fleet is still blind to you; a fort creeping toward noon means you are being found. And the sharpest instance is dramatic on purpose — when a troop breaks through a wall, the sightline it opens to the next wall behind lights up instantly like a sun ray (the breach-reveals-the-next-layer rule of Fleet Memory §2, rendered as a sudden shaft of noon into a twilight interior). One glance tells you a layer just fell and which way the fleet's eyes now reach — loss made legible in light.

The hull tells — motion, not meters

  • Motion signature (Ships §4) — LATERAL = fear (skirting a HOT cell while still netting forward) and FORE-AFT = nerve (Committed → Steady → Wavering → Breaking → Gone). The two components never confuse: advancing-around-danger can't be mistaken for drifting-toward-the-exit.
  • The flag icon — marks the anchor hull. It says which hull holds the net; the motion says how close the net is to breaking. Separate jobs — the icon does not reopen the morale-bar door.
  • The crazy-Ivan jink — a hull jukes the instant a shell lands near (the Evasion axis; Cannon §6 explains why this makes the first volley your cleanest).
The composition rule — one hard rail, soft atmosphere on top

The read stays legible because it is layered by fidelity, not flattened: a firm floor of “here's what I know for certain” (your AoRs, your health, their exposure glint) with the uncertain layers (fear haze, decay, resolve) floating soft above it. Wrong toward all-soft → the picture reads as noise; wrong toward all-hard → the bluff dies and the signaling game with it. This is the same rail-plus-haze structure a stealth game gives you: a reliable light gem under an unreliable sense of suspicion.

The red line — bad load leaking in

The three channels live at three fidelities precisely so you reason about shapes, not cells (Fleet Memory — the load is the point). If the grid-read ever becomes “hold a hundred cells and a ledger in your head,” clerical bookkeeping has leaked in where reasoning should be — and the fix is always more summarizing and clearer diagnosis, never more to track. This doc is where that risk actually bites: the two-fog stack is Fleet Memory's flagged danger, and the Fight is where it either holds or fails. Guard it here above everything.

5The Attention Economy — The Real Bottleneck

The sharpest consequence of a watch-and-nudge Fight: because auto-fire makes actions cheap and the verbs make them rare, the scarce resource is not clicks — it is where the player is looking. The Fight is won or lost on whether the eye is drawn to what matters and free to ignore the rest.

This reframes choices already made

Several decisions from the other docs turn out to be attention decisions, and this is where that becomes explicit:

  • No morale bar (Ships §4) is not only anti-bookkeeping — a tell rendered as motion is caught in peripheral vision across the whole board, while a bar demands you look straight at it. Motion tells are cheap to attend; bars are expensive.
  • Idle guns sweep and coil (Cannon §5) — the board reads as alive and ready without asking for attention, so a quiet AoR can be safely ignored until something enters it.
  • The three-verb ceiling (Cannon §6) is an attention budget as much as a UI one. A fourth live verb doesn't just add a button — it adds a thing the eye must continuously track. This is a second, independent reason the verb budget is guarded.
The design job — make exactly two events seize the eye

Everything on the board should be glanceable except the two moments that demand a decision: (1) a valuable target committing into a bright core (the held-alpha trigger) and (2) a flank going wrong — a Committed hull driving a soft seam, or a group's core softening under fire (the correction trigger). The salience budget is spent on those two and nothing else. When the game draws the eye to a non-decision, it has wasted the player's scarcest resource.

Why planned command is cheap attention — and reaction is dear

The choreographed maneuvers of §6 cost almost nothing in attention precisely because they are self-scheduled: you know the north wave ends around now, so you are already watching for your cue to walk the group east — the event doesn't need to seize your eye, you brought your eye to it. A reactive move is the opposite: a surprise that grabs attention and forces a decision under time pressure. So the attention economy and the command-vs-plate-spinning line are the same line viewed twice — a plannable, spaced battle keeps the load on reading and conducting (cheap, deliberate); a battle of forced surprises spends attention on reacting (dear, and the arcade failure).

Honest risk — attention can still saturate

Cheap actions don't guarantee cheap attention. On a large board (64×48 — bigger than a phone screen, so you pan and zoom) with many groups facing a wide wave, the read itself can overflow even with nothing to click: you may not be looking at the flank that's breaking. The mitigations are real but unproven: the summarizing fidelities (shapes, not cells), the peripheral-motion tells that read even at a zoomed-out overview, and camera control (quick-drag pan; pinch zoom). Two genuinely open pieces, both flagged in §10: the number — how many simultaneous groups, and how wide a wave, before the read breaks — and the viewport — whether an off-screen event that needs the eye (a breaking flank, a committing target) gets a legible off-screen cue so the player knows to pan to it. The design target is stated (“the read stays a reasoning load at the widest wave the campaign fields, and nothing decision-critical happens unseen off-screen”); the confirming values are not yet known.

6The Three Verbs In Play — Cadence, Not Mechanics

The verb mechanics are Cannon & Tower §6's and are not repeated here. What this doc owns is the connective view: when each fires and why — and the key reframing, that every verb serves two roles, a planned maneuver conducted on a known schedule and a reactive correction when a plan breaks.

What you do — the input grammar, and what's locked

The gestures

Command is per individual group — you select and command one group at a time; there is no multi-select or "group-of-groups" meta-command, so the input never becomes a management layer of its own. The gesture set is small and non-conflicting (assembled from Cannon §6 + the camera rules): tap a group selects it and highlights its AoR (§4); hold-and-drag the selected group's zone re-tasks its AoR (a soft-lock holds until you lift — Cannon §6-A); the selected group's domain control (the two-ended slider) and fire-control toggle (hold / weapons-free) sit on it; and camera is quick-drag to pan, pinch to zoom. The one gesture that must stay unambiguous is drag: a quick drag on empty water pans the camera, a deliberate hold-then-drag on a selected group re-tasks it — the same distinction Build already draws between a pan and a piece-move. Note that every group already has an AoR walking in — the game auto-assigns a sensible default and you nudged it in Deploy (Cannon §3) — so live, you are moving an existing zone, never drawing one.

What you cannot do in the Fight — and why that matters

The Fight's command set is deliberately tiny because the structural decisions were spent in Deploy. You cannot place or move a cannon or wall, cannot form, split, or merge groups, and cannot re-stack a group's tower priority — all of that is Build/Deploy (Cannon §3, §8, §10). Live, you have exactly the three verbs on the selected group and the camera. That hard boundary is what keeps the Fight a reading phase: you live with the fort and the groups you committed, and adapt only their coverage, ammunition, and timing — never their construction.

Fire-control — the timing skill

The verb the commander leans on most, and the sortie's signature decision. Per Fleet Memory, holding fire is a memory move: a silent battery never enters the fear grid, so the fleet routes as if that ground is safe. So fire-control is information warfare conducted in real time — withhold the signal to keep the fleet mistaken, then spend it (reveal + coordinated alpha) the instant a valuable target has committed into your core, ahead of its crazy-Ivan juke. Held early it is chip damage and a triggered evasion; held right it is a big, high-percentage opening salvo.

AoR re-task — a planned maneuver or a correction

The zone crawls (baseline one grid/tick), firing degraded the whole way — a readable window you are choosing to open (Cannon §6-A). It serves two roles. As a planned maneuver: you scheduled this crawl in Deploy to walk a group from one known threat to the next (the north→east→north line above), and you trigger it on cue. As a correction: a flank is breaking now and can't wait a sortie, so you pay the crawl to save it. The crawl's cost is not a tax on acting — it is what makes the scheduling a real problem: you must start the move early enough that it lands before the next threat, which is a thing you plan, deterministically.

Domain switch — planned reload, or telegraphed panic

Same two roles. Planned: your one group is loaded sea for the north wave and you have already timed the switch to land for the east troops (the group goes silent to reload — Cannon §6-B — so you start it in the gap between threats). Panic: a bomber run you didn't plan for is crossing the map and you switch to air to meet it. With the third-domain rule tabled, a switch simply cancels or plays through — so a planned two-step (sea→land→air across the sortie) is three clean, separately-timed commitments, never a tangle.

The distinction that matters — command vs plate-spinning

This is the line to hold, and it is not “act rarely.” A busy sortie full of AoR-moves and domain-switches is skilled command when the moves are planned against known, spaced events — you are conducting a rehearsed line, and the determinism is what let you rehearse it. The same sortie is plate-spinning only when the game forces simultaneous, unplanned moves faster than you can think — reacting to surprise under speed pressure. Frequency is not the test; foreknowledge and spacing are. A player conducting ten scheduled commands against a score they learned is playing the game exactly as intended; a player flailing at three things at once that all landed unexpectedly is in the failure state.

The choreography is fragile — and that's good

A planned line depends on the tools that set its timing, so the enemy can break your schedule by breaking those tools. The sharpest case: your crawl speed is set by the Rally Flag tower (Cannon §6-A, §10) — snipe it and your next re-task crawls slower, so a maneuver you timed for the east arrives late and the troops are already ashore. Towers are destructible and the fleet prioritises them, so protecting the tower that underwrites your plan becomes part of the plan. This is the right kind of difficulty: not a faster reflex demanded, but a contingency to have anticipated — the fleet attacking your timing, which you answer in Deploy (screen the Rally Flag, or stack a backup) rather than in a scramble.

The obligation this creates — and the real red line

For the choreography to be command and not scramble, multi-threat sequences must be spaced enough that a planned line is physically possible — one group's crawl + reload has to fit the gap between the threats it is meant to walk between. That is a board- and wave-design law (sibling to the “≥2 solutions” rule), owned by Campaign / Build triage: if a board forces two costed moves at once, or a move faster than the spacing allows, the board — not the player — has failed. The arcade red line from The Why §6 survives, relocated precisely: the danger was never a busy commander, it is a board that demands reflex. Keep threats plannably spaced and a full sortie of commands stays a thinking act.

7Fighting Blind — The Fog Fight, Moment to Moment

In fog theaters the read inverts: you can't see what your guns are shooting at. Assembled from the player's seat (the mechanics live in Cannon §5 and the enemy side in Fleet Memory §4), the fog Fight has its own short arc.

Blind bombardment → contact → focus fire

A group whose zone holds an unseen contact fires blind at a heavy accuracy penalty — deliberately wasteful hunting, not sniping. A hit or near-miss lights that ship up; the penalty drops and the group concentrates. Every shot thins the fog a little, and a ship that fires reveals itself for a moment. So the fog burns off as the engagement heats — an opening-moments condition, not a permanent blindfold.

What a reveal looks like — a lens that holds, then collapses

Board fog is fully opaque where you're blind. A reveal (a Flare pulse, a Spotter lift, a sightline opening) opens a circle: transparent at the centre, fading to opaque at the edge — a lit lens, not a hard-edged hole. It runs two timers back to back: a visible-duration while the lens holds open, then a collapse as the circle shrinks inward until the fog closes over it. So sight in fog is a fading window you spend — read fast, act inside it. (Render owned by Cannon §5; the timer lengths tune in prototype.) The fairness constants never fog: spawn points, their safe zones, and DMZ grids stay lit every sortie.

What changes in the read

Information becomes the scarce resource on top of attention. Hold-fire in fog means “don't burn ammo blind — wait for a reveal, then strike.” The Reveal towers (Flare's pulse, Spotter's passive lift) skip the blind phase entirely, turning a fog zone back into a normal one — which is exactly why those towers are precious in fog theaters, and why a whole family of enemies (Recon, Wraith, Sower's smoke, Mimic) exists to make them matter.

Held open, owned elsewhere

The fog tuning is not locked — how harsh the blind penalty is, how long a reveal lasts, how much each shot thins the fog, and whether blind fire exists at all in the early game (probably not; introduce fog late). That open list is Cannon §5's to settle against the fog theaters. This doc only fixes the felt shape: blind → contact → focus, burning off as it heats.

8Reading the Ending — Felt Before It Is Stamped

Victory §4 owns the mechanics of the three sortie end-states. This doc owns reading which one is coming — because a thinking game lets you see the resolution developing and act on it, rather than having a result announced at you.

The ending is the diagnosis

How a sortie ends is the “read why” step of the debugging loop (Fleet Memory — Design Intent). You don't just receive an outcome; you watch it develop, understand what caused it, and fold that into the next Deploy — repair the seam that broke, relocate the gun they found, pre-load for the domain that surprised you. The Fight's real product is not the outcome but the lesson. The battle's respite and celebration come at the battle's end, not the sortie's — the tension is meant to ramp across the whole battle.

Reading the battle's end, not just the sortie's

Above the three sortie-endings sits the battle's own terminal read (mechanics in Victory §3–4 and Fleet Memory). Because the ship pool is capped and fixed-order, you can feel it draining — waves come thinner and you recognise you've seen most of what's coming. When the pool empties, the threat is spent and the remaining Fight time rolls into the Last Stand: a final window to enclose freely, with the pressure lifted. The other two battle-ends read off the same tells at scale — a fleet whose resolve has cratered across sorties stops committing (Retreat), or a committed one is ground to the last hull (Annihilation). So the moment-to-moment read has two horizons at once: is this wave about to break, and is the whole fleet running out of will or hulls — the second building out of the first.

9North-Star Check

An honest read of the phase against The Why §2: does the Fight keep strategy the fun?

What it gets right

  • Reading over reaction. The beat structure is mostly read-beats; the one live skill (fire-control timing) is a when, a decision, never a twitch.
  • Attention-as-bottleneck keeps the load a thinking load. Cheap actions + peripheral tells + a two-event salience budget mean the effort goes into reading a mind, not into executing under time pressure.
  • Costed verbs keep every nudge deliberate. The crawl's degraded-fire window and the domain switch's silent reload make repositioning a real bet, not a reflex.
  • The ending is legible. You see the resolution developing and can act on it — the fairness obligation of §3 (a loss you can see the cause of) delivered inside the phase.

The tensions this doc must hold

Three live risks — all instances of Why §6 tension #5 (the Fight sliding to arcade)
  • Stacked fog → noise. Two uncertainties at once can read as static rather than tension. Mitigation: the one-hard-rail composition (§4). This is the single highest-priority thing to get right in the Fight.
  • Attention saturation. Many groups against a wide wave may overflow the read even with cheap actions. Open number, not open design (§5, §10).
  • Re-task as default. If the main live verb becomes constant repositioning, the phase has drifted from watch-and-nudge to plate-spinning. The verb-cadence (§6) is the health check.
The honest limit — this is a doc of specified targets

Per The Why's design-first method, the felt claims here are designed as targets, not confirmed facts — because “does watching feel like commanding, not flailing?” and “is the two-fog read tense or noisy?” are precisely the questions only play can answer. The targets are stated precisely so the prototype has something to hit: a Fight that is read-dominant (two act-beats per sortie), peripherally legible (tells caught without staring), and reasoning-loaded, not clerical (shapes, never cells). Prototyping confirms we hit them; it does not get to discover the design.

10Open Seams — Not Settled

Domain-switch legibility — RESOLVED (Jack)

The fork is closed by removing the situation, not picking a side: the third-domain-mid-reload rule is tabled. A switch in flight can only be cancelled (meter reverses up to the old domain) or played through (meter runs down to the new one) — so the meter direction is unambiguous and the glance-legibility hazard is gone. To reach a third domain you wait for the current switch to finish, then switch again. Mechanic updated in Cannon §6-B.

AoR-distance reload — parked proposal, lands in this read

The proposed reload-scales-with-AoR-distance idea (arc-signalled: distant AoR = higher, slower arc; near = flatter, faster) would add a live element to the grid-read — the projectile arc becomes a thing the player reads moment-to-moment. Noted here because that is where it would live; it stays parked in Cannon & Tower and is not built into this doc.

Surfaced by the completeness pass — Jack's rulings
  • Pause / time-control — RESOLVED: there is none. The Fight runs continuously; no pause, no slow-motion. The only stop is leaving the app — hide it and the Fight freezes until you return, resuming on a 3-2-1 countdown (§3). Screenshot-then-hide to study the board is possible but high-effort / low-gain; the design neither relies on it nor guards against it. Determinism is untouched (a wall-clock freeze doesn't touch the seeded sim). This confirms the continuous-real-time reading the whole architecture was built for.
  • The Fight is replayable, win or lose — RESOLVED: the checkpoint is the battle. The earlier "Fight-only vs full-sortie" fork is dissolved, not decided: there is one checkpoint granularity — the battle. A battle runs as waves of sorties, and a sortie as its phases; those breakups are pacing and document-communication, not save points. You replay the whole battle (deterministic → you re-execute across it better), and because the battle is also the scored unit (Battle Score), no special per-phase or per-sortie scoring rule is needed. See §2.
  • Command throughput — CLARIFIED (resolved). The earlier "multi-group serial-command throughput" worry is bounded: command is per individual group (no group-of-groups selection), and every AoR is an auto-assigned default you merely nudge — so setup is light and live command is simple selection. Combined with the spacing law (threats spaced so you aren't forced to command many groups at once), the serial input stays comfortable. Any residual limit is a prototype feel-check, not a design gap.
  • Deploy phase — AGREED NEXT (queue item #8). The un-owned Deploy phase is the next design target — the other half of "Deploy is the plan, the Fight is the reveal," and the natural home for the auto-AoR-nudge experience and the replay mechanic above.
  • Audio & haptics — NEXT LAYER, but the constraint is set now (Jack). Sound and haptics come in a later layer, and when they do they must be beneficial, never a constant background drone: each cue earns its place by aiding a read or a decision. The hard rule: the game must be fully playable, and playable well, with no sound at all — audio and haptics reinforce the visual read, they are never a channel the game depends on. (Legibility stays visual-first.)
  • Attention-saturation ceiling — the number of simultaneous groups / wave-width at which the read breaks. Feel/number layer; prototype it.
  • The two-fog visual languages — SPECIFIED (Jack). Board fog = opacity (opaque when blind; a transparent-centre, opaque-edge reveal lens that holds then collapses). Mind fog = saturation (twilight unconfirmed → noon confirmed). Distinct channels of the eye, so they never conflate (Fleet Memory §8). Remaining UI-phase work is treatment polish, not the language.
  • Fog-Fight tuning — inherited open list from Cannon §5 (blind penalty, reveal duration, fog thinning, early-game fog gating).
  • All felt targets in §2–§9 await prototype confirmation — stated as targets, not yet measured.
