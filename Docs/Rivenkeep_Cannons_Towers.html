Design Pillars

You are a commander, not a gunner. You never aim or fire a shot. You decide where your guns look, what ammunition they load, and how they are grouped — then you watch your plan meet the enemy and adapt only when it breaks.

The four pillars
  • Commander, not soldier. Cannons auto-fire and auto-target. Your control is coverage (Areas of Responsibility) and ammunition (Land/Sea/Air domain) — allocation, not aim.
  • Tight toolkit, rich bestiary. The player masters a small, fixed set of cannons and towers. Variety and long-term growth live on the enemy side (ships, troops, bosses, theaters) — not in an ever-expanding cannon list. This is how the game stays learnable while growing for years.
  • Deploy is the plan; the Fight is the reveal. Deploy is a coverage puzzle you commit to. If the plan is good, the Fight needs little input — it is where you find out whether you planned correctly, and intervene only when you didn't.
  • Determinism is the teacher. Because battles are identical every run, failure is informative: the same crisis returns at the same moment, so you fold it into your next plan. Fail → learn → re-plan → win.

Scope note: this document defines the defender only (cannons, groups, AoR, towers). Ships and troops get their own documents; the GDD will reference all three.

1The Cannon Matrix — 8 × 3

There are 8 cannon archetypes (roles). Each exists in three domain versions — Land Sea Air — that share the archetype's role, damage shape, and quirks, and differ only in which domain they fire efficiently against. That is 24 cannons on the board but only 8 concepts to learn. "The Splasher" is one idea; you just field its Land, Sea, or Air cut for the threat in front of you.

Domain is ammunition, set per group. A group loads one domain at a time. Switching domain means the crews clear the old ammo and load new — a real cost (see Fight Verbs). Air is therefore not a separate anti-air cannon; it is a mode every archetype can load. That is the elegance: the roster grows in expressiveness (domain × archetype) without growing in things to learn.

Why 8×3 instead of 24 distinct cannons

A flat list of 24 cannons is unlearnable on mobile. Organized as a matrix the player already thinks in — my land guns, my sea guns, my flak — they hold a small mental model and reach into the right drawer. Same trick Kingdom Rush uses to feel deep with only four towers: keep the toolkit small, let the interactions be rich.

2Domain Efficiency — a Directional Cycle

Every cannon can hit anything in its AoR — land, sea, or air — but at reduced effect outside its loaded domain. The penalties form a ring: each domain is passable against one neighbour and poor against the other. No cannon is ever a dead pick; it is just efficient, mediocre, or bad depending on what it is loaded for.

The "passable" arrows chain Land → Sea → Air → Land; the "poor" arrows run the other way. Consequence for play: mixing domains across groups is itself a coverage puzzle. A Land group paired with a Sea group covers the sea gap cleanly; a Land group paired with an Air group leaves sea doubly soft. Coverage-vs-concentration recurs one layer down.

3Groups & Areas of Responsibility

Cannons that touch side by side (orthogonally) during Deploy become a group. A group is commanded as one unit and owns a single Area of Responsibility (AoR) — always a circle — and its crews auto-fire at anything inside it (holding fire when it is empty). Grouping is a Deploy-phase decision only — no merging or splitting during the Fight. You live with your plan.

  • Adjacency is orthogonal, not diagonal. Two cannons touching edge-to-edge group up. Two cannons touching only at a corner (pure diagonal) do not — until a third cannon touches both of them, at which point all three snap into one group. This lets fort shape, not just proximity, decide grouping.
  • One active tower per group (last-moved wins). If two towers end up touching the same group, only the most-recently-placed/moved tower connects; the other(s) gray out with an icon showing they're inactive. Move the winner away and a grayed tower can take over. No stacking tower effects.
  • More cannons → BIGGER AoR and more firepower. Each cannon contributes area (~1→base, 2→2×, 3→3×; exact scaling TBD) and every cannon can fire at anything in the zone — so a bigger group both sees wider and hits harder. Grouping is genuinely advantageous.
  • Lone Wolf cannons are the exception. They shrink a mixed group's AoR (antisocial), so they want to be alone. Two Lone Wolves can group together, but their AoR size does not grow — instead their accuracy tightens (see Falloff). Lone Wolves trade coverage for reliability.
  • A lone cannon is just a group of one — so "solo vs cluster" and "group vs AoR" are one system, not two.
The core tension (revised)

Grouping grows coverage and firepower, so the trade is not "wide vs hard." It runs on three axes at once: (1) Domain — one big group loads a single domain; splitting lets you run Sea here and Air there. (2) Direction — one big zone answers one approach; several zones answer several. (3) Shape (spatial tension) — because every AoR is a circle, a few big circles leave scalloped gaps along a straight coastline and waste coverage out to sea, whereas a line of smaller overlapping circles hugs the shore as a clean defensive wall. So big groups win on raw density; small groups win on fit. The deterministic fleet punishes whichever balance you chose wrong.

4AoR Accuracy Falloff

Hit chance is highest at the centre of an AoR and falls off linearly to the edge:

P(hit) = 100% − 75% × (distance ÷ radius)

Dead centre every cannon hits 100% of the time; at the very edge, 25%. So a 4-cannon group firing at a ship on its fringe lands, on average, one hit. Push the ship toward the centre and all four connect. You want the threat in your core — which is exactly why you place carefully in Deploy and rarely need to move in the Fight. Overlap the fringes of neighbouring circles and a seam ship is fired on by both groups, restoring good odds — the intended Deploy fix, not a Fight scramble.

Lone Wolves fall off differently — tight, not soft

A Lone Wolf's zone does not grow with company, so it compensates with accuracy: it falls off from 100% centre to only 60% at the edge (versus 25% for a normal cannon) — a small but reliable circle. Group two Lone Wolves and the edge climbs to 90% (the AoR stays the same size). So Lone Wolves are the precision answer: where a normal group gives a big, soft-edged zone, a Lone Wolf pair gives a small circle that hits nearly everything inside it. Different tool, different shape.

Overlap stacks the fringes

Where two AoRs overlap, a ship at the shared edge is inside both groups' fringes and is fired on by both at ~25% each — doubling the barrels and restoring good odds at the seam. Overlap is the deliberate cure for edge-weakness, and it is a Deploy decision, not a Fight scramble.

Requirement this creates

Because edge misses are real, the AoR must show its gradient (a visible centre-to-edge fade) so a player can see "that ship is in my weak fringe" and never feels cheated by an unattributable miss. The miss roll is seeded per sortie (like ship movement and every other roll), so the same board with the same layout always produces the same result — replays and leaderboards stay exact.

5Firepower Model — Probabilistic, Not Assigned

A group does not lock onto one target and does not have to concentrate fire. Each cannon independently fires at something in the AoR (per its target personality). So firepower is a matter of odds: more barrels in a zone means more shots in the water each tick, which means a higher chance any given ship goes down before it reaches the wall.

  • Overkill is wasted — piling cannons on a lone target is inefficient, which naturally discourages over-stacking without any artificial group cap.
  • A group need not always be hitting, or hitting the same ship — a big group simply makes it likely that things in its AoR die fast.
  • Immediate engagement: the instant a ship enters an AoR, the whole group opens fire. Idle guns read as coiled: their muzzles slowly, randomly sweep the horizon searching for targets — then the moment a ship enters the zone, every barrel snaps to the same bearing in unison. Searching, then a coordinated lock, sells "ready and hunting," never "asleep or broken."

Fog of War — firing at what you can't see

Some theaters hide the fleet in fog. If a group can't see the ships in its AoR, how does it fire? Proposed resolution (needs prototyping; the fog-bearing theaters live in the Ships doc):

  • Speculative fire. A group whose zone holds an unseen contact fires blind into the AoR — suppressing fire at a heavy accuracy penalty. It is deliberately wasteful: you are hunting, not sniping.
  • A blind hit reveals. The moment a speculative shot connects, that ship is lit up, the penalty drops, and the group concentrates on the revealed target (and any others now visible) at normal falloff. Blind bombardment → contact → focus fire.
  • Reveal tools skip the blind phase. The Spotter (passive fog-lift) and the new Flare tower (pulses illumination over the whole AoR) reveal ships before you waste shots — turning a fog zone back into a normal one. This is what makes those towers precious in fog theaters.
  • Interactions. Fog blunts the Marksman (it can't pick the highest-value threat it can't see — until a reveal); and hold-fire in fog means "don't burn ammo blind — wait for a reveal, then strike." Fog turns information into the scarce resource.
Open

This is a proposal, not a lock. Key tuning: how harsh the blind penalty is, whether revealed ships stay lit for a duration or only while in a reveal zone, and whether blind fire should be allowed at all in the very early game (probably not — introduce fog later). Settle against the fog theaters when we build the Ships doc.

6The Fight-Phase Verbs

Auto-fire runs your plan on its own. When it breaks you have three group command-verbs — re-task the AoR, switch domain, and fire-control — plus a separate Abilities spend. The two positional verbs cost silent time (the group can't fire while it happens), but both can be cancelled mid-cooldown, aborting the change and snapping the guns instantly back online in their previous state — so the cost is only paid if you complete the change. The two also stack (a group can re-task and switch domain at once: doubly silent, doubly committed).

A · Re-task the AoR

  • Hold-and-drag the group's zone to new ground. A soft-lock holds until you lift your finger, so you can carefully place a big lazy move.
  • Cooldown scales with move distance — small nudges are cheap; hauling coverage from the east flank to the west is a long silent gap and a real gamble (you uncover the east to cover the west).
  • The group and its AoR desaturate during the move.

B · Switch domain (load new ammo)

  • Per group, whole group switches together. Crews clear the old ammo and load the new — no cannon in the group fires during the reload.
  • Deliberately expensive, and restartable: change your mind mid-reload and the timer resets from full. Indecision is punished — commit to your read.
  • A countdown renders over the group and the group + AoR desaturate while offline. This is panic mode: you use it when a threat you can see coming (e.g. bombers launched from the back rank, visibly crossing the map) is about to enter a zone loaded for the wrong domain.
  • The Forge tower reduces both this and the fire-reload cooldown.
  • Cancel any time → immediate fire. Abort a domain switch (or an AoR move) mid-cooldown and the crews drop what they're doing and re-engage instantly in their old state. Only a completed change costs you the full silent window.

C · Fire control — hold / weapons-free

A per-group toggle between weapons-free (fire the instant anything enters the zone — the default) and hold fire (wait, and only open up when you say, or when a ship reaches the high-accuracy centre). This exists because of an enemy behaviour: a ship that gets hit performs a "crazy Ivan" — a sharp evasive juke that throws off the next incoming shots. (The manoeuvre is a ship trait, detailed in the Ships doc.)

Why this is the strongest of the three verbs

Fire-control ties three systems into one decision. Because of falloff, a shot at the centre of the zone lands far more reliably than one at the edge; because of the crazy Ivan, your first volley is your cleanest one before the ship starts juking. So holding fire until the ship reaches your centre, then unloading a coordinated alpha strike, can out-damage firing early and chasing an evading target. Fire early = chip damage but you trigger evasion and shoot at edge odds; hold = a big, high-percentage opening salvo, at the risk the ship damages your wall or slips through while you wait. Pure commander timing — no aiming — and it finally makes the falloff gradient something you actively play, not just place around.

Abilities — cannon-focused Stonwryt boosts

Separate from the command verbs: spend Stonwryts mid-battle to boost your cannons at the right instant — overcharge a group's damage, force a rapid-reload, a one-off barrage. Cannon-focused only (they buff the guns, not walls or economy), and naturally occasional because currency is finite — so they punctuate a crisis rather than becoming constant micro.

Honest flag: watch the verb load

That is now three command-verbs (AoR / domain / fire-control) plus Abilities. Each is simple, but together they approach the ceiling of a "watch and nudge" Fight — the plate-spinning we deliberately designed out. Mitigation: fire-control and domain are usually set in Deploy and only touched in a crisis; Abilities are rare by cost. If prototyping shows the Fight feels busy, the first cut is to demote a verb to a Deploy-only standing order. Do not add a fourth live verb.

Set it in Deploy, adapt in the Fight

Each group's starting domain, AoR, and fire-control stance are set during Deploy. A well-scouted commander walks in already loaded and stanced for the right threat on the right approach; changing mid-fight is the exception, not the default. Lost to bombers last run? Pre-load a group to Air next run — the plan absorbs the lesson.

7The Cannon Levers

Each archetype is one point in this space, following the Kingdom-Rush clarity rule: one primary role, one signature quirk, one honest weakness. No stat-soup all-rounders — the "dominant generalist" is the failure mode we are explicitly avoiding.

Damage shapeSingle-target · splash (area) · chain (jumps) · persistent zone (burn).
TrackingAbility to hit fast movers (vs whiff).

9Utility Tower Candidates 11 → pair down to ~6

Towers are 2×2, deal no damage, and enhance only the one group they physically touch. One active tower per group (a lone cannon may take one too; a second touching tower grays out — last-moved wins). They are the roster's second growth axis — coverage, information, sustain, tempo, and output multipliers the damage cannons deliberately lack. Here are 11 candidates; we pair down against the ships (target ~6). A tower slot is a real cost: one fewer barrel, plus 2×2 of fort space.

Fixed rules

Towers are not upgradeable and give no in-battle-improvable effects — this keeps the cannons the special, invested pieces. Towers are destructible: kill the tower and the group loses its enhancement, so enemies prioritise towers — a forward tower is a real risk (the Decoy Mast weaponises that fact). Overlap to watch when pairing down: Powder vs Magazine (damage vs rate), Forge vs Rally (which cooldowns), Spotter vs Flare (passive vs active fog reveal). (Parked: Relay — see Future.)

10Mini-Games — The Casual Layer

The mini-games are the "Candy Crush" of Rivenkeep: a big, standalone, genuinely fun casual layer that also happens to teach the game. Each core skill (walling, enclosure, deploying, setting an AoR, switching domains, grouping, tower placement, fire-control) has several mini-games on a climbing difficulty ladder — deep enough that a player could happily live here for a long time without touching a campaign.

  • Generatively harder. Because a player may stick with them indefinitely, mini-games are procedurally generated to keep escalating past any hand-authored ceiling — endless difficulty, not a fixed tutorial.
  • Randomness is allowed here. Mini-games are not on the leaderboards, so unlike the deterministic ranked battles they can use RNG freely — fresh layouts every attempt, no fairness/seeding constraint.
  • The economy on-ramp. Mini-games are the main early Stonwryt earner, and the natural home for more frequent ads (rewarded/optional). Earning up front here means battles can carry far fewer ads — the casual layer subsidises the campaign's clean feel.
  • Also the practice area. The same system doubles as a no-stakes sandbox to rehearse a skill or a specific battle situation on demand.
  • They deepen by combining phases until the top of the ladder is Campaign 1, Battle 1 — so the first real battle lands as "wait, this is easy," and there is never a cliff.
  • User-generated boards. Let players build mini-games too, with rewards for creators — and the best community boards get promoted into the game for everyone. A near-endless content firehose that costs us little to sustain.
Honest flag: UGC is a big, separate build

User-generated boards are a strong long-term play, but they are their own project: a builder tool, sharing/discovery, a rewards/curation pipeline, and moderation (reuse the on-device name/content moderation from the GDD, extended to boards). Ship the generative + practice + ads layer first; treat UGC as a later milestone, not launch scope.

11Determinism & the Mastery Loop

The Fight's skill is not reflexes; it is reading and adapting. Because the simulation is deterministic, the crises are deterministic — the same flank hits at the same tick every run — so mastery is a loop:

plan → watch it tested → get hit → learn why → fold into next Deploy → win

  • Failure is intended, early, and humane. The first loss is deliberately tuned to land around the 5th–8th sortie of play — after a few easy wins, early enough to teach while the stakes are trivial. The lesson is the point: failure is a normal, fun part of the loop. Some of the best games are the ones where you fail several times but inch closer each run until it clicks — that feeling is the target, not an accident.
  • Failure is cheap. A loss never drains permanent Stonwryts or upgrades — only the consumables spent that run. Retry is instant, and the identical crisis rewards the player who learned.
  • The post-battle AI debrief is the coach: "your east group sat idle all game while the west wall fell at tick 40 — pre-task it west." This loop is only possible because of determinism; it turns the core technical thesis into the central skill of the game.
  • A perfectly-planned battle needs zero Fight input. If we've done our jobs, the Fight is a spectacle of your plan working — you intervene only when it doesn't. The 3-lever star system rewards the quality of the plan (turtle / perfect-defence / annihilation are just different plan qualities).

12Parked / Future ("War 2" & later)

Deliberately deferred — do not build yet
  • Friendly fire (symmetric). Cannons don't target friendlies. Likely first version: heavily-reduced splash can chip your own wall — just enough to discourage letting troops camp on it, not enough to feel punishing. Ships wouldn't be immune to their own splash either (reward letting them cluster). Ship behind a late-game flag; playtest OFF by default.
  • Overheat / cooldown rhythms per cannon — a firing cadence to manage. Risks re-introducing soldier-skill; good War-2 candidate.
  • Commander-triggered cannon actives — per-cannon special abilities. Overlaps the timed-consumable ability verb; park to avoid muddying "commander not soldier."
  • Relay tower — lets two non-touching groups share an AoR. Powerful but a complexity spike; for now, groups that want to combine simply touch.

13Open Questions — To Prototype Before Locking

Honest list of what is not settled, so we don't mistake a sketch for a decision:

  • Falloff needs work. The linear 100%→25% model is a starting point, not a final answer — the curve shape, the edge floor, and how it reads on screen all need tuning and prototyping. This is the mechanic the "watch, don't fiddle" promise rests on, so it gets first prototyping attention.
  • The multi-target family. Candidates 4–7 (Splasher / Chain / Sweeper / Saturation — blob vs jump vs line vs wide) all "one shot hits many." They're the preferred flavour over multi-shot, but four is too many — resolve which feel distinct against the ships and expect to keep only 1–2.
  • Control & personality clusters. Frost / Suppressor / Disabler (and the Jammer tower) overlap on "control"; Marksman / Interdictor / Finisher overlap on "who do I shoot." Pair down against real enemies.
  • The third Fight verb. Abilities (timed consumables) is the proposal; the "firing-orders toggle" is the alternative. Decide during the roster pass.
  • The whole model is unvalidated until the bestiary exists. A defender's toolkit is only as good as the crises it answers. The first thing to prototype (a stripped Fight with a couple of dummy ships) is the single question: does managing AoRs feel like commanding, or like flailing? If falloff-coverage feels good there, the model is validated; if not, we rethink before investing in ships.
