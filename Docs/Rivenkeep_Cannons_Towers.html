Design Pillars

You are a commander, not a gunner. You never aim or fire a shot. You decide where your guns look, what ammunition they load, and how they are grouped — then you watch your plan meet the enemy and adapt only when it breaks.

The four pillars
  • Commander, not soldier. Cannons auto-fire and auto-target. Your control is coverage (Areas of Responsibility) and ammunition (Land/Sea/Air domain) — allocation, not aim.
  • Tight toolkit, rich bestiary. The player masters a small, fixed set of cannons and towers. Variety and long-term growth live on the enemy side (ships, troops, bosses, theaters) — not in an ever-expanding cannon list. This is how the game stays learnable while growing for years.
  • Deploy is the plan; the Fight is the reveal. Deploy is a coverage puzzle you commit to. If the plan is good, the Fight needs little input — it is where you find out whether you planned correctly, and intervene only when you didn't.
  • Determinism is the teacher. Because battles are identical every run, failure is informative: the same crisis returns at the same moment, so you fold it into your next plan. Fail → learn → re-plan → win.

Scope note: this document defines the defender only (cannons, groups, AoR, towers). Ships and troops get their own documents; the GDD will reference all three.

1The Cannon Matrix — 8 × 3

There are 8 cannon archetypes (roles). Each exists in three domain versions — Land Sea Air — that share the archetype's role, damage shape, and quirks, and differ only in which domain they fire efficiently against. That is 24 cannons on the board but only 8 concepts to learn. "The Splasher" is one idea; you just field its Land, Sea, or Air cut for the threat in front of you.

Domain is ammunition, set per group. A group loads one domain at a time. Switching domain means the crews clear the old ammo and load new — a real cost (see Fight Verbs). Air is therefore not a separate anti-air cannon; it is a mode every archetype can load. That is the elegance: the roster grows in expressiveness (domain × archetype) without growing in things to learn.

Why 8×3 instead of 24 distinct cannons

A flat list of 24 cannons is unlearnable on mobile. Organized as a matrix the player already thinks in — my land guns, my sea guns, my flak — they hold a small mental model and reach into the right drawer. Same trick Kingdom Rush uses to feel deep with only four towers: keep the toolkit small, let the interactions be rich.

2Domain Efficiency — a Directional Cycle

Every cannon can hit anything in its AoR — land, sea, or air — but at reduced effect outside its loaded domain. The penalties form a ring: each domain is passable against one neighbour and poor against the other. No cannon is ever a dead pick; it is just efficient, mediocre, or bad depending on what it is loaded for.

The "passable" arrows chain Land → Sea → Air → Land; the "poor" arrows run the other way. Consequence for play: mixing domains across groups is itself a coverage puzzle. A Land group paired with a Sea group covers the sea gap cleanly; a Land group paired with an Air group leaves sea doubly soft. Coverage-vs-concentration recurs one layer down.

3Groups & Areas of Responsibility

Cannons and towers are 2×2 footprints. Two pieces that touch side by side — sharing at least one cell-edge (not just a corner) — form a group. A group is commanded as one unit and owns a single Area of Responsibility (AoR) — always a circle — and its crews auto-fire at anything inside it (holding fire when it is empty). Grouping is a Deploy-phase decision only — no merging or splitting during the Fight. You live with your plan. Grouping is never declared: it is read off fort geometry, so a placement can silently fuse two groups — the JOIN / FUSE / NEW pre-commit preview that prevents that is specified in the Deploy doc §4, along with the Build↔Deploy seam it creates (courtyard geometry decides command granularity).

  • Adjacency is orthogonal, not diagonal. Because cannons are 2×2, only one cell-edge needs to touch — the footprints can sit offset. Cannons touching only at a corner (pure diagonal) do not group, until a third cannon bridges both, at which point all three snap into one group. Fort shape, not just proximity, decides grouping.
  • A tower touches exactly one group. If a tower ends up touching two groups, those groups fuse into one big group — the tower is a bridge, exactly like a bridging cannon. This holds even if that tower is grayed out (physical touch merges groups; active/inactive only decides whose buff applies).
  • Multiple towers, one active — a priority stack for resilience. A group may hold several towers; only the #1 is active, the rest sit grayed as backups. Order is set in Deploy by last-tap (the fourth field of the Deploy order card §5, and the one field that is locked for the whole Fight): placing or tapping a tower makes it #1 and pushes the old #1 down to #2, and so on. Because towers are destructible and enemies target them, this is insurance — snipe the active tower and the next auto-promotes, so a key group's buff isn't a single point of failure. The cost is honest: extra towers eat fort space and barrels. (Only one buff applies at a time — no stacking.)
  • More cannons → BIGGER AoR and more firepower. Each cannon contributes area (~1→base, 2→2×, 3→3×; exact scaling TBD) and every cannon can fire at anything in the zone — so a bigger group both sees wider and hits harder. Grouping is genuinely advantageous.
  • Big groups get more from one tower. A tower buffs the whole group, so the single 2×2 tower slot pays off across every cannon in a large group — another reason to consolidate. (The counterweight is the spatial/shape tension below.)
  • Lone Wolf cannons are the exception. They shrink a mixed group's AoR (antisocial), so they want to be alone. Two Lone Wolves can group, but their AoR size does not grow — their accuracy tightens instead (see Falloff). Lone Wolves trade coverage for reliability.
  • A lone cannon is just a group of one — so "solo vs cluster" and "group vs AoR" are one system.
  • THE GROUP OUTLINE — how a group tells you it exists (Jack). Every cannon carries a coloured outline around its own 2×2 footprint. The instant two cannons share a cell-edge, their separate outlines resolve into a single unbroken outline tracing the perimeter of the whole group — the union of every footprint in it. A group is not a label or a menu state; it is a shape. It composes exactly as the adjacency rule does: a third gun bridging two diagonals collapses three outlines into one, a tower bridging two groups does the same (grayed or not), and a gun lost in the Fight redraws the perimeter around what remains. The AoR changes at the same instant, and that is the second half of the signal — on joining, the outline merges and the zone re-forms as the group's larger AoR, or, for a Lone Wolf pairing, visibly tightens instead of growing (falloff). Two channels moving together — a perimeter and a coverage circle — make the grouping event impossible to miss without either channel having to shout, and they teach the adjacency rule by demonstration rather than by tooltip. Fusion is a legitimate deliberate play (two medium zones become one large one, more barrels, one tower buffing all of it), so the outline's job is to make it unmistakable rather than to prevent it. The pre-commit preview (a dragging ghost showing the perimeter it would create) and the one-level fusion undo — remove the bridging cannon and the previous groups' AoRs are restored; pick up any other cannon and that memory is discarded — are the Deploy doc §4's.
  • AoRs are auto-assigned; you nudge them, you don't draw them (Jack). The game gives every cannon and every group a sensible default AoR the moment it is placed/formed — pointed at the nearest sensible threat approach — so the player is never staring at a blank coverage map. In Deploy that default is easy to move (drag it to the ground you actually want); adjusting a good default is the task, not building coverage from scratch. Command is always of an individual group — you select and re-task one group at a time; there is no "group-of-groups" / multi-select meta-command. (The default-assignment rule — radius from cannon count, centre offset toward the nearest uncovered approach, seam-overlap bias, deterministic tie-break — and the free instant nudge are owned by the Deploy doc §6; the live, costed re-task is the Fight doc's.)
  • Placement — the one terrain rule. A cannon's 2×2 footprint needs solid open ground; it cannot sit on a fragile grid (ice, crust, crag, bog, fissure, fract) — cannons are too heavy, the ground won't bear the weight and recoil. This is a property of the terrain, not the cannon: no cannon type is ever locked out of a board, some ground just can't hold a gun. (This is the only placement constraint — otherwise every cannon plays every board.)
The core tension (revised)

Grouping grows coverage and firepower, so the trade is not "wide vs hard." It runs on three axes at once: (1) Domain — one big group loads a single domain; splitting lets you run Sea here and Air there. (2) Direction — one big zone answers one approach; several zones answer several. (3) Shape (spatial tension) — because every AoR is a circle, a few big circles leave scalloped gaps along a straight coastline and waste coverage out to sea, whereas a line of smaller overlapping circles hugs the shore as a clean defensive wall. So big groups win on raw density; small groups win on fit. The deterministic fleet punishes whichever balance you chose wrong.

The one place coverage can't reach — enemy spawn launch zones

Every enemy spawn point owns a launch zone: a corridor where emerging hulls accelerate fast and cannot be hit. It exists for fairness — the ship pool emerges in a fixed, deterministic order (the flagship first, or among the first — Ships §4), so without it a gun parked on the emergence point would simply farm ships as they appear and snipe the flag on spawn. Spawn-camping is ruled out at the source. In the AoR this renders as a displayed dead zone in the coverage — the same centre-to-edge gradient the AoR already shows (§4), with the launch zone punched out as an explicit no-fire hole — so a player never wastes coverage there and never feels cheated by a shot that "should" have connected. This is a targeting constraint, not a placement one: you may build anywhere solid, but you engage a wave (and its flag) only once it clears its launch zone into live coverage. The flagship's brief, catchable assembly window (Ships §4) opens after it accelerates out — never on the spawn itself.

4AoR Accuracy Falloff

Hit chance is highest at the centre of an AoR and falls off linearly to an edge floor F:

P(hit) = 100% − (100% − F) × (distance ÷ radius)

For a plain, uncoordinated cannon the floor is F = 25%: dead centre it hits 100%, at the very edge 25%, so a 4-cannon group firing at a ship on its fringe lands, on average, one hit. Push the ship toward the centre and all four connect. You want the threat in your core — which is why you place carefully in Deploy and rarely need to move in the Fight.

Coordination — one stat, expressed as a bigger bright core

The edge floor is not a fixed number; it rises with a single quantity, Coordination. Everything that used to be a separate "bonus" is now the same stat at a different value:

F = 25% + 15% × (coordination), soft-capped below 100%

SituationCoordinationEdge floor F
Mixed group (no same-type partner)0~25%
Pure same-type group (each cannon has a partner)~1~40%
Lone Wolf (precision baked in)~2.3~60%
Two Lone Wolves grouped~4.3~90%

So the old Battery bonus, the pure-group bonus, and the Lone Wolf's tight falloff are no longer three rules — they are one line with different inputs. A bigger pure battery just has more coordinating partners, so its floor rises for free (no new number to tune), still counterweighted by the domain-lock and circle-fit tensions. Numbers to balance, but the shape is the point: one generating stat, not a ladder of exceptions.

Express it as a growing core, not a higher edge number

Same math, better feel: instead of showing the player "your edge is 40% not 25%," render Coordination as a larger high-accuracy centre — a disciplined group's bright core visibly bulges outward, and only the thin rim is chancy. This is the more elegant framing and the more legible one: the player sees coordination paying off in the size of the reliable zone, rather than experiencing invisible better-odds. It directly answers the "will anyone perceive the probability?" risk.

Overlap stacks the fringes

Where two AoRs overlap, a ship at the shared edge sits in both groups' rims and is fired on by both — stacking the barrels and restoring good odds at the seam. Overlap is the deliberate cure for edge-weakness, and it is a Deploy decision, not a Fight scramble.

Requirement this creates — the three render states (Jack)

Because edge misses are real, the player must be able to see “that ship is in my weak fringe” and never feel cheated by an unattributable miss — but the gradient can't be painted over the whole board at rest, or it floods the colour channel the fogs need (Fleet Memory §8). So an AoR has three render states, escalating with how much the player needs to see it:

  • Resting (not firing, not selected) — a light outline of the grid squares it covers. Coverage extent only, low visual weight; the colour channel stays free for the fogs.
  • Firing (engaging a target, not selected) — the outline plus its bright centre core and its outer boundary circle, and nothing between. Enough to read live “this zone is hot, here's its reliable core and its reach” — so a fringe miss is fair without a tap — but not the full gradient, so several firing groups don't repaint the board.
  • Selected (tapped) — the full centre-to-edge colour gradient over the water/fog (a coordinated group's core bulging outward). The complete reliability read, summoned for the one group you're inspecting.

So the fringe-miss fairness is served live by the firing state (core + rim visible) and in full on demand by the selected state — resolving the earlier “does a firing group show its gradient?” question without re-flooding the channel. The miss roll is seeded per sortie (like ship movement and every other roll), so the same board with the same layout always produces the same result — replays and leaderboards stay exact.

5Firepower Model — Probabilistic, Not Assigned

A group does not lock onto one target and does not have to concentrate fire. Each cannon independently fires at something in the AoR (per its target personality). So firepower is a matter of odds: more barrels in a zone means more shots in the water each tick, which means a higher chance any given ship goes down before it reaches the wall.

  • Cannons prefer the shot most likely to land. When several targets sit in the AoR, a crew's default is to fire at the one with the highest hit probability — i.e. whatever is nearest the centre — over a low-odds target out at the fringe. (Special target-personality archetypes override this: the Marksman chases the highest-value threat, the Interdictor the enemy nearest your wall, the Finisher the lowest-HP one.) This default also rewards hold-fire: let a ship reach your centre and it becomes both the high-% shot and the preferred one.
  • Overkill is wasted — piling cannons on a lone target is inefficient, which naturally discourages over-stacking without any artificial group cap.
  • A group need not always be hitting, or hitting the same ship — a big group simply makes it likely that things in its AoR die fast.
  • Immediate engagement: the instant a ship enters an AoR, the whole group opens fire. Idle guns read as coiled: their muzzles slowly, randomly sweep the horizon searching for targets — then the moment a ship enters the zone, every barrel snaps to the same bearing in unison. Searching, then a coordinated lock, sells "ready and hunting," never "asleep or broken."

Fog of War — firing at what you can't see

Some theaters hide the fleet in fog. If a group can't see the ships in its AoR, how does it fire? Proposed resolution (needs prototyping; the fog-bearing theaters live in the Ships doc):

  • Speculative fire. A group whose zone holds an unseen contact fires blind into the AoR — suppressing fire at a heavy accuracy penalty. It is deliberately wasteful: you are hunting, not sniping.
  • A hit or a near miss reveals. When a speculative shot connects or lands close, that ship is lit up, the penalty drops, and the group concentrates on it (and anything else now visible) at normal falloff. Blind bombardment → contact → focus fire.
  • Every shot thins the fog a little, and a ship that fires reveals itself for a short time (its muzzle flash gives it away). So the fog naturally burns off as the engagement heats up — it's an opening-moments condition, not a permanent blindfold.
  • Reveal tools skip the blind phase. The Spotter (passive fog-lift) and the new Flare tower (pulses illumination over the whole AoR) reveal ships before you waste shots — turning a fog zone back into a normal one. This is what makes those towers precious in fog theaters.
  • How a reveal renders (Jack-specified). Board fog is fully opaque where the player can't see. A reveal opens a circle: transparent at the centre, fading to opaque at its edge (a lit lens, not a hard-edged hole). The lens runs two timers back to back — a visible-duration countdown (it holds open), then a collapse timer (the circle shrinks inward on itself until the fog closes). So a reveal is a fading window, not a permanent lift. This render is fog-theater only; non-fog boards have no board fog to open.
  • Always visible, every sortie — the fairness constants. Regardless of fog: every spawn point and its safe launch zone (see §3) is always visible, and DMZ / area-denial grids are always fully uncovered. The player can never be blind to where a wave will emerge or to impassable ground.
  • Interactions. Fog blunts the Marksman (it can't pick the highest-value threat it can't see — until a reveal); and hold-fire in fog means "don't burn ammo blind — wait for a reveal, then strike." Fog turns information into the scarce resource.
Open

The render is now specified (the transparent-circle lens, the visible-then-collapse timers, the always-visible spawns/safe-zones/DMZ above). What still tunes in prototype: how harsh the blind penalty is, the visible-duration and collapse-timer lengths, how much each shot thins the fog, and whether blind fire is allowed at all in the very early game (probably not — introduce fog later).

6The Fight-Phase Verbs

Where the lived use of these verbs lives

This section specifies the verb mechanics. How they are used moment-to-moment — the cadence of a sortie (mostly fire-control, an occasional re-task, a rare domain switch), the attention economy that makes three the ceiling, and the read that tells you when to reach for one — is assembled in the Fight doc. Mechanics here; the experience there.

Auto-fire runs your plan on its own. When it breaks you have three group command-verbs — re-task the AoR, switch domain, and fire-control. The two positional verbs both take time and desaturate the group, but they cost differently. A domain switch goes fully silent (the group can't fire while it reloads). An AoR move keeps firing — at reduced rate and accuracy — while its zone crawls to the new ground (see below). Both can be cancelled, and they cancel differently too: an AoR move freezes where it is, while a domain switch has to be un-done — the ammo has to come back out. The two also stack — a group can re-task and switch domain at once, in which case the domain reload keeps it silent while its coverage crawls, so when the ammo finishes the guns come back online already re-laid on the new ground.

A · Re-task the AoR

  • Hold-and-drag the group's zone to new ground. A soft-lock holds until you lift your finger, so you can carefully place a big lazy move; on release, the zone crawls to its new home.
  • The AoR travels — it does not teleport. The zone's centre slides toward the new position at a baseline one grid per tick (the Rally Flag tower and certain cannon identities modify this). A small nudge finishes almost at once; hauling coverage from the east flank to the west is a long crawl and a real gamble — you uncover the east progressively as the west comes online.
  • The guns keep firing, but degraded, for the whole crawl. Unlike a domain switch, the group does not go silent — it fires throughout at reduced rate of fire and reduced accuracy. Think of it as the crews working out a new gun-laying on the move: shots are slower and looser until the lay settles.
  • Firing solutions follow the AoR. The guns engage whatever the moving zone currently covers, tracking the crawl — they do not keep shooting the old ground. Old targets fall out of the zone as its centre slides off them; new ones enter as it arrives.
  • On completion, full stats snap back instantly. The moment the zone reaches its destination, rate of fire and accuracy are restored to normal — the re-lay is done. There is no lingering penalty once the move finishes.
  • Cancel freezes the crawl in place. Let go / tap away mid-move and the zone stops wherever it currently sits — it does not snap back to the start and does not jump ahead. The guns then finish laying onto that partial position and recover to full. (Contrast the domain switch, whose cancel must reverse the reload.)
Why this makes AoR a different cost than a domain switch

The two positional verbs feel distinct on purpose. A domain switch takes the group fully offline (no fire) and its cancel must be un-done — reload reversed. An AoR move never goes offline; it pays in degraded fire during a crawl, restores the instant it lands, and its cancel simply freezes where it is. So re-tasking is the softer, always-firing adaptation — a moving, weakened coverage gradient — while a domain switch is the hard, silent commitment. That keeps AoR the main live verb without making it free: a long haul is a real, readable vulnerability window.

B · Switch domain (load new ammo)

  • Per group, whole group switches together. Crews clear the old ammo and load the new — no cannon in the group fires during the reload. A countdown renders over the group and the group + AoR desaturate while offline. This is panic mode: used when a threat you can see coming (bombers launched from the back rank, visibly crossing the map) is about to enter a zone loaded for the wrong domain.
  • Cancel reverses the counter — it does not snap back. The ammo already pulled has to be re-seated, so cancelling counts the meter back up before the guns fire again. On an 8-count, cancel at 6-remaining and it climbs 6→8 then fires (in the original domain); cancel at 2-remaining and it must climb 2→3→4…→8 first. Bailing late is expensive — so a switch is a real commitment, not a free feint. (This is a stronger version of the old "restartable" rule: adaptation always has a price.)
  • Only two things can happen to a switch in flight: cancel it, or let it play through. A mid-reload change to a third domain is not offered (tabled deliberately) — it was the one rule whose meter could run counter-intuitively, and the Fight lives or dies on glance-legibility. So the meter direction is unambiguous: down = loading the new domain (play through), up = cancelling back to the old one. To reach a third domain you wait for the current switch to finish, then switch again — one clean commitment at a time.
  • The Forge tower reduces both this and the fire-reload cooldown.
UI concept (not final)

Show the switch as a two-ended slider between domain icons: Land / Sea / Air each own a colour + icon, one at each end of the track, with a marker that travels between them and a tray that fills with the colour of the domain being loaded. A normal switch slides the marker from the current icon to the target icon; cancel slides it back the other way. Because a third domain is never picked mid-reload, the marker only ever travels between two ends — the destination is always the icon it is heading toward, so the travel reads as purposeful, never arbitrary.

Honest flag

Reverse-on-cancel makes a switch a real commitment — bail late and the ammo still has to be re-seated before the guns fire again, so flip-flopping is costly. That's intended (it enforces "commit to your read"), and it's why domain should normally be set in Deploy and switched only for a telegraphed threat. With the third-domain rule tabled, the meter now only ever counts one way per action (down to load, up to cancel), so the earlier "does it travel up or down for the same tap?" legibility hazard is gone — the countdown reads cleanly. Numbers (the count length, the Forge reduction) still tune in prototype.

C · Fire control — hold / weapons-free

A per-group toggle between weapons-free (fire the instant anything enters the zone — the default) and hold fire (wait, and only open up when you say, or when a ship reaches the high-accuracy centre). This exists because of an enemy behaviour: a ship that gets hit performs a "crazy Ivan" — a sharp evasive juke that throws off the next incoming shots. (The manoeuvre is a ship trait, detailed in the Ships doc.)

Why this is the strongest of the three verbs

Fire-control ties three systems into one decision. Because of falloff, a shot at the centre of the zone lands far more reliably than one at the edge; because of the crazy Ivan, your first volley is your cleanest one before the ship starts juking. So holding fire until the ship reaches your centre, then unloading a coordinated alpha strike, can out-damage firing early and chasing an evading target. Fire early = chip damage but you trigger evasion and shoot at edge odds; hold = a big, high-percentage opening salvo, at the risk the ship damages your wall or slips through while you wait. Pure commander timing — no aiming — and it finally makes the falloff gradient something you actively play, not just place around.

Abilities — moved to Future

A cannon-focused Stonwryt-boost verb was considered as a third live action but parked to protect the verb budget (see Future). The Fight keeps three command-verbs only.

Honest flag: guard the verb budget

Three command-verbs (AoR / domain / fire-control) is the ceiling for a "watch and nudge" Fight — the plate-spinning we deliberately designed out. It stays comfortable because two of the three (domain and fire-control) are normally set in Deploy and only touched in a crisis, leaving AoR re-tasking as the main live action — and even that now carries a transitional cost (degraded fire while the zone crawls), so it rewards deliberate moves over twitchy ones. This is exactly why Abilities was pushed to Future: do not add a fourth live verb.

Set it in Deploy, adapt in the Fight

Each group's starting domain, AoR, and fire-control stance are set during Deploy — three of the four fields on the Deploy order card, using the same widgets these verbs use, so the player learns this whole control surface unhurried with no enemy on the board. A well-scouted commander walks in already loaded and stanced for the right threat on the right approach; changing mid-fight is the exception, not the default. Lost to bombers last run? Pre-load a group to Air next run — the plan absorbs the lesson.

8Cannon Health, Nurture & Veterans

A cannon is not a disposable turret — it is a persistent asset you raise. This system (carried over from the GDD's combat design and reconciled to the new model) is what makes protecting your guns matter beyond the current sortie, and it reinforces the enclosure win from a second direction.

Damage degrades effectiveness on a curve

Every cannon has HP; enemy fire chips it during the Fight. Effectiveness follows an inverted-logistic curve — near-full HP barely matters, then it falls off a cliff: ~100–75% HP ≈ full performance, 50–25% a sharp drop, below 25% nearly useless, 0% destroyed for the rest of the Fight. In new-model terms, a damaged cannon contributes less Coordination (its share of the group's bright core shrinks) and less firepower — so a battered group's zone visibly softens.

Nurture between sorties — raise a veteran

Between sorties, surviving cannons are rebuilt and rewarded, pushing HP above 100% (a veteran) up to a 200% cap. Bonuses come from exactly the behaviours the rest of the design already wants:

  • Survived the Fight → a big rebuild (e.g. +50%, capped), so a battered gun comes back.
  • Enclosed (walls around it, best with a castle) → a standing bonus each cycle. This is the key link: lob-over-walls already made you want to enclose your guns; nurture pays you again for the same act, so enclosure earns its keep three times over (protection + territory/victory + veteran growth).
  • Held position → an "unmoved" bonus. Read the polarity correctly: this is a reward for having sited the gun well in the previous sortie, not a penalty for moving — physically relocating a cannon in Deploy simply forgoes it, decaying with distance. (This is the physical Deploy move — distinct from free AoR re-tasking in the Fight, which doesn't touch nurture.)

A veteran above 100% contributes extra Coordination and firepower; at 200% it's a maxed asset — near-perfect bright core and roughly double output. Veterans carry between battles within a campaign (only cannons at ≥100% persist; at a campaign boundary everyone resets to 100% except the three best veterans the player carries forward — see Victory §7). This is a soft, within-campaign progression — distinct from the permanent per-archetype Stonwryt mastery ranks — and it's the source of real attachment ("that's my 200% Standard from three battles back").

Why it fits the new model

It rhymes with everything: HP feeds the Coordination stat (so nurture and falloff are one currency, not two), it rewards enclosure (the spine of the whole game), and the "unmoved" bonus gives the physical-placement decision weight without touching the Fight verbs. It also answers the "cannons are disposable" flatness the new doc had — you now grow guns, not just place them.

Effectiveness at the breakpoints (the inverted-logistic curve above): 100% = normal accuracy + damage; 150% = noticeably tighter core and +50% firepower; 200% = perfect accuracy at every range and double firepower (the maxed veteran). Health-bar display. The rule is phase-split (Jack). In Build and Deploy — the planning phases — every cannon always shows its bar and its exact percentage below it (e.g. "147%"), so the veteran/damage modifier is fully trackable for protect-and-deploy decisions. In the Fight, the board stays clean: a cannon shows no bar until it takes a hit, and the first damage brings the bar in with its health colour right then — so a bar on screen always means “this one is wounded and worth your eye,” and an untouched fort reads quiet (an attention-economy win — see the Fight doc §5). Non-cannon units follow the ordinary rule everywhere (no bar at 100%, a bar only when damaged). Colour: green at 100%, yellow 51–99%, red ≤50%; a light-green section overlays from the left for the >100% veteran bonus.

Carry. Within a campaign, only cannons at ≥100% carry to the next battle (below 100% is not saved); if two of a type carry, the higher-percentage one deploys first. At a campaign boundary, everyone resets to 100% except the three best veterans the player carries forward (Victory §7).

How the pair-down works

Clusters to resolve against the bestiary and cut hard: the multi-target family (Splasher / Chain / Sweeper / Saturation — blob vs jump vs line vs wide) probably yields 1–2 keepers; the control family (Frost / Suppressor / Disabler, plus the Jammer tower's overlap); the target-personality family (Marksman / Interdictor / Finisher). 10Utility Towers — One Amplifier per Subsystem 6 axes → ~6 towers

Towers are 2×2, deal no damage, and enhance only the one group they touch. A group may hold several towers, but only the #1 is active — the rest are grayed backups that auto-promote if the active one is destroyed (order set in Deploy by last-tap; see Groups and Deploy §5). Stacking towers is resilience, not extra buffs — one effect applies at a time. The cannons have a generating structure (archetype × domain); the towers now have one too — each tower amplifies exactly one game subsystem it plugs into. That single rule both organises the list and guarantees distinctness: six subsystems, six towers, no overlap. Where an axis holds more than one candidate expression, we pick the best one when the ships tell us which subsystems actually get stressed — and drop any axis no enemy pressures. A tower slot is a real cost: one fewer barrel, plus 2×2 of fort space.

Fixed rules & the pair-down

Towers are not upgradeable and give no in-battle-improvable effect — the cannons stay the special, invested pieces. Towers are destructible, so enemies prioritise them (the Decoy Mast weaponises that fact). The pair-down keeps the axes fixed and chooses the best expression per axis against the bestiary — and cuts any axis the ships never stress. So a tower earns its slot only when some enemy makes its subsystem matter. (Parked: Relay — see Future.)

11The Recommended Shortlist a proposal · nothing above is cut

§9 and §10 stay the full superset — every one of the 16 archetypes and every tower expression is preserved. This section layers a recommendation on top: the shippable set the bestiary actually makes necessary, read off the Ships doc's Counter-Web Map (§12 there). It is a proposal for the joint pair-down, not a decision — treat §9/§10 as the menu and this as the suggested order.

The method, and the asymmetry

Keep a cannon only where some enemy makes its job necessary and it can't collapse into another lever or a tower; every proposed cut leaves its target answerable another way (the no-forced-playstyle rule, applied in both directions). The cut is deliberately asymmetric: the defender toolkit stays small — legibility beats variety — while the enemy roster stays wide and is staged across the campaign rather than culled, because breadth is the anti-boredom budget.

Coverage & no-one-off check

The eight answer every threat angle — most by cannon, the rest by tower or verb: general hull → Standard/Suppressor; armour → Piercer; priority / back-line → Marksman; breakthrough at the wall → Interdictor (+Breaker); surviving a hot zone → Suppressor; buying time / shoving → Breaker; dense clump → Splasher; wide swarm or bomber cloud → Saturation. The three not on the list — fast jukers, healers, enemy specials — are covered without a cannon (a fire-control alpha in your falloff centre + the Jammer tower; Marksman on the healer; Marksman on the caster), and stealth / hazards are tower jobs (Flare, Camouflage, Stonewright). Nothing is uncovered.

On one-offs: two cannons are always-on generalists (Standard, Suppressor); the other six are load-for-matchup specialists — not one-offs, because each answers a recurring category across many lineages, not a single enemy. The one to watch is Saturation: its breadth depends on wide / dispersed formations recurring. If bomber clouds stay rare (air-density is still an open knob) and troop waves never spread wide, Saturation drifts toward one-off. Keep it honest by making wide lines and clouds a staple — the roster already carries the axes (Swarm's wide-line, the bomber cloud/stream) so it always has work. Splasher is safe by construction: troop stacking (×4) structurally manufactures the clumps it answers.

The eight not on the shortlist (still on the menu in §9)

Chain, Sweeper — multi-target redundancy (a spaced wave → Saturation + focus; a file → Interdictor-at-the-tip + Saturation). Rapid — fast movers die to a fire-control alpha + Jammer; feeble vs armour. Frost — collapses into the Jammer tower (both slow) + Suppressor. Burner — ground-denial answered by Flare + Stonewright. Finisher — a healer is answered by Marksman on it + focus-fire. Disabler — collapses into Marksman (kill the special-user). Overwatch — coverage is the Spotter tower's job, not a barrel.

Roster — kept whole, staged not cut

Nothing in the Ships doc is culled. Every lineage stresses a kept lever, so the roster is sequenced, not trimmed: the canonical eight lead the campaign (each teaches one layer), the expansion eight arrive as mid/late richness (Mimic latest). Two ripples worth noting now — with Rapid/Frost off the shortlist, Corsair/Wraith lean on Jammer + fire-control; with Finisher off it, Chirurgeon-Fleet becomes a "Marksman the healer" fight.

Decisions — all three settled
  • Cannon slot 8 — SETTLED. Eight cannons; slot 8 = Saturation. Coverage checked below — every enemy angle is answered and no cannon is a one-off.
  • Tower count — SETTLED: seven. Sustain ships two expressions — Stonewright (repair) and Camouflage (conceal). Tempo = Rally (Forge the swap); Output = Powder (Sights the swap).
  • Herald — SETTLED: boss-only. The resolve layer lives as an intensity knob on the flagship (Ships doc §4), not a common lineage.

12Parked / Future ("War 2" & later)

Deliberately deferred — do not build yet
  • Manual live tower-switch. Multiple towers already auto-promote when the active one dies (shipping behaviour). A future option: let the commander tap to swap the active tower mid-Fight — but on a warm-up cost (a short window where neither buff applies), mirroring the domain reload so adaptation still costs something. Parked because a free instant swap would be a fourth live verb and break the "adaptation has a price" theme; revisit only if M4 shows the Fight has headroom.
  • Abilities (cannon-focused Stonwryt boosts). Spend Stonwryts mid-battle to boost the guns (overcharge, rapid-reload, one-off barrage) — buffs cannons only, never walls or economy. A good future lever, but held back now to protect the three-verb Fight budget. Revisit only if the Fight proves it has room for a fourth action.
  • Friendly fire (symmetric). Cannons don't target friendlies. Likely first version: heavily-reduced splash can chip your own wall — just enough to discourage letting troops camp on it, not enough to feel punishing. Ships wouldn't be immune to their own splash either (reward letting them cluster). Ship behind a late-game flag; playtest OFF by default.
  • Overheat / cooldown rhythms per cannon — a firing cadence to manage. Risks re-introducing soldier-skill; good War-2 candidate.
  • Commander-triggered cannon actives — per-cannon special abilities. Overlaps the parked Abilities idea; park to avoid muddying "commander not soldier."
  • Relay tower — lets two non-touching groups share an AoR. Powerful but a complexity spike; for now, groups that want to combine simply touch.

13Open Questions — To Prototype Before Locking

Honest list of what is not settled, so we don't mistake a sketch for a decision:

  • Falloff needs work. The linear 100%→25% model is a starting point, not a final answer — the curve shape, the edge floor, and how it reads on screen all need tuning and prototyping. This is the mechanic the "watch, don't fiddle" promise rests on, so it gets first prototyping attention.
  • The multi-target family — RESOLVED (§11). Paired down against the bestiary: Splasher (blob — vs troop stacks) and Saturation (wide — vs swarms / bomber clouds) kept; Chain and Sweeper cut. Only Saturation's reliance on wide formations staying common is still a watch-item (tied to the open air-density knob).
  • Control & personality clusters — RESOLVED (§11). Suppressor holds control (Frost collapses into the Jammer tower; Disabler into Marksman). Target-personality: Marksman + Interdictor kept; Finisher is an add-back only if the regen lineage ships early.
  • Does the domain switch earn its rules-weight? — VALIDATED. The Ships doc's Switch-Moment Catalogue (§10 there) supplies enough telegraphed switch moments (the Skyfall / bomber run, sea→air / sea→land transitions) to justify the live verb. What remains is feel-tuning of the costs, not the rule set.
  • Coverage — VALIDATED; feel is the open part. The bestiary now exists (Ships doc) and the Counter-Web Map (§12 there) confirms every threat-angle has an answer and every kept lever is stressed. What genuinely remains is the prototype feel-check — does managing AoRs feel like commanding, not flailing? — a feel/number-layer question, no longer a design gap.
