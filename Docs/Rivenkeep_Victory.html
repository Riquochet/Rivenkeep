1What This Doc Is

The victory model: how a battle is won, how each sortie resolves, and the ammo logic that ties them together. The battle win-condition and star system are the GDD's — kept intact because they are already right. What this adds is the sortie-resolution layer (the three end-states) and the ammo model that gives them a real-world cause.

The shape in one breath

You win a battle by enclosing the required castles — nothing else. Stars and Battle Score grade how well you did. Underneath, a battle is a series of sorties — waves drawn from a capped, fixed-order ship pool — and each wave attacks until it has spent its ammo and withdraws to reload, unless you rout it first or it breaches you along the way. How the sorties go decides how much of the pool you ever face.

2The Battle Win — Beat Back the Attack, Hold the Castles

CORRECTED — enclosure is not the win condition

Earlier versions of this doc (and the GDD text it inherited) stated that the battle is won the moment the required castles are enclosed at the end of any Build phase. That was wrong, and it was wrong in a way that propagated. Ruling (Jack): "Victory is not enclosing castles. Victory is beating back the attack and maintaining the number of castles enclosed. That is why there is a last stand."

The error mattered because a great deal was derived on top of it — sortie count was modelled as a construction schedule, and the scoring ruling in §3 was shaped around an exploit that the correct win condition makes impossible. Both are rebuilt below. The Last Stand is the tell that should have caught this earlier: if enclosing ended the battle, a last stand would have nothing to be last of.

Victory: the attack is beaten back, and the castles are still held

The player wins when both are true:

  • The attack is beaten back — the fleet stops attacking, for one of the two reasons in §4a: its ships are gone, or its ships have no purpose left and its resolve collapses queue-wide.
  • The required number of castles is enclosed at the end of the battle, which is the end of the Last Stand.

So enclosure is not the goal — it is the thing you must maintain while pursuing the goal. That is a different game and a better one: the player is not racing to close a ring and stop, they are holding ground under attack for as long as the attack lasts. It also puts the two halves of the design on the same footing at last: the fort is what you hold, the fleet is what you must break, and neither alone is a win.

Why the Last Stand exists — and it only makes sense under this reading

When the attack is beaten back, the battle is not over: the fleet is spent, and every second of Fight time you banked by ending sorties early rolls in at 100% (§5). The Last Stand is the final, unopposed window to reach or restore the required enclosure count — and to push past it for the Area lever, since nothing is shooting at you any more.

This is why it is called a last stand and why it is the only phase that inherits all of the banked time rather than half. It is also why the time economy has always paid for repels: every sortie you cut short is time you spend here, and here is where the battle is actually decided. A player who beat the fleet back quickly arrives with a long Last Stand and closes rings at leisure. A player who ground through every magazine arrives with almost no time and has to already be enclosed.

Defeat: the required count becomes unreachable

Defeat is still only a genuinely unwinnable state, and the game still never forces a lost cause. It arrives when the required castle count can no longer be reached: castles degraded to 3/3 and destroyed below the requirement, or required castles rendered mathematically un-enclosable. If that becomes true mid-Build, the battle ends immediately — a clean loss the player understands rather than a drawn-out death.

What is not defeat: being temporarily below the requirement. A breakthrough breaks a ring and drops a castle out of enclosure, and that is a wound to repair next Build, not a loss (§6). "Maintaining" the count means holding it by the end, with the Last Stand as the final chance — not holding it unbroken every second.

The consequence that reshapes the whole battle: it is now a RACE, and boards must be authored for it

Under the old reading, the wall schedule was the battle length: you fought until you could close the ring, then you won. Under the correct reading the two are independent and must both be satisfied, which makes the battle a genuine race between two clocks:

  • The fleet's clock sets how long the battle lasts — the queue of formations divided by how fast you eliminate them (§4b). This is the number of sorties you get.
  • The wall's clock sets how long you need — the Build phases required to lay and hold the required rings (Build §5), plus every re-plug the fleet forces on you.

Board-authoring law, and it is checkable: the sorties a board's fleet provides must exceed the Build phases its requirement demands, by a margin wide enough that a player who loses a ring to a breakthrough can still recover. Too tight and the board is unwinnable; too loose and the back half is an unopposed construction exercise. This is a sibling to the ≥2-solutions law and it belongs to the same owners (Campaign / Build triage).

And it is a much better problem for the player than the old one. You are not choosing when to stop; you are deciding how much of each sortie to spend building versus how much to spend breaking the fleet — knowing that breaking the fleet faster shortens the battle and buys Last Stand time, while building faster secures the requirement but leaves the fleet intact and the battle long.

Stars & Battle Score — the skill metric (this is the fairness engine)

Three independent levers — Ships Accounted For, Cannon Health, Area Enclosed — each 1–3 stars, averaged and rounded up, with a hidden ★★★! for near-perfect play. Beneath the stars, Battle Score = ships% + area% + cannon% (max 400) is the real prize: full-resolution performance, so two players with identical ★★★ have different scores and the leaderboard knows who played better. This one mechanic is the fairness vision of The Why §3 — skill, not luck, ranked honestly — already built.

One note the corrected win condition changes: it is no longer true that "ship-killing is rewarded without ever being required." Beating back the attack requires, at minimum, destroying the fleet's purpose-bearing hulls (§4a). Ship-killing is now a condition of victory, and the levers grade how well you did it — which is a cleaner relationship between the win and the score than the old one.

The 0★ pacifist easter egg

Zero stars is reachable: kill no ships and lose every cannon, yet still meet the minimum enclosure (castles enclosed = win) → 0★. The first 0★ in each campaign pays a Stonwryt bonus of 10 × campaign level (Cam 1 = 10, trivial; Cam 32 = 320, brutally hard to survive with no kills and no surviving gun). Undocumented — discovered organically; pays once per campaign.

Worked examples — Ships ★★★(3) + Cannons ★(1) + Enclosure ★(1) = 1.67 → ★★; Ships ★(1) + Cannons ★★★(3) + Enclosure ★★★(3) = 2.33 → ★★★. Any two levers at ★★★ guarantee ★★★ overall. Beneath the stars, Battle Score = ships% + area% + cannon% (max 400): 80% + 45% + 110% = 235; two identical ★★★ can post 287 vs 312.

3The Ship Pool — Capped, Ordered, Deterministic

A battle does not throw its whole fleet at the player at once. It has a fixed pool of ships with a set emergence order and set spawn-rate. What varies is how much of that pool the player ever meets — and that is driven entirely by how the sorties end.

Bounded, so it is not a treadmill

The pool is capped. "More ships might still be coming" is never runaway escalation — it is the fleet committing more of a finite, pre-set reserve. This is the resolution to the anti-treadmill rule (The Why §4): the thing that makes a returning wave dangerous is that it learned (persisted Contact + a cognition tick), not that it is endless. Order and rate being fixed keeps the whole thing deterministic — identical play meets an identical battle.

Sortie count is emergent — and that is a feature

Because the pool is drained by performance, the number of sorties is not a fixed number. A player who repels efficiently drains the pool fast and reaches the Last Stand sooner; a player who merely holds faces more of it. When the pool empties, the threat is spent — the remaining Fight time rolls over to the Last Stand, and the player encloses freely. Emergent battle length is not a fairness problem: two players who play identically get the identical battle; a better player simply earns a shorter one.

Fleet sizing — the derivation this whole doc set hangs from rebuilt ·

Pool size is not the design input, and treating it as one is how the pre-AoR numbers went wrong. The input is how much threat is on the board at once; the pool is what that requires the fleet to hold in reserve in order to deliver it for the number of sorties a battle should last. This section owns that input, because the pool is owned here. The number-sets downstream of it — cannon slots (Deploy §2), the wall budget (Build §5), the contact ramp (Campaign §4) and the three phase timers — each stay with their own owner and derive from what is established here.

Greenfield — every legacy number in this area is void, not adjusted

The redesign of how ships fight changed the defender's problem from targeting to allocation, and that invalidates the whole numeric layer around ships, troops, bombers and cannons — not just the slot formulas. The following are void and are being rebuilt from this section outward, not tuned:

  • Counts: ship pool sizes, spawn rates, sortie counts per battle, troop counts per transport, bombers per carrier, cannon slot formulas, cannon type caps.
  • Interactions: hull HP and hits-to-kill, cannon reload and rate of fire, ship and troop movement speeds, AoR radius and area scaling, accuracy falloff floors and the Coordination increment, proximity fire bonuses, cannon recoil damage, troop stack damage multipliers, command-confusion batching tiers, troop pathfinding cost weights, ammo loads.
  • Times: all three phase timers, sortie length, transit times, crawl speed, reload and switch durations.

Where a shape was designed independently of its magnitude — the falloff being linear to an edge floor, stacking being super-additive, batching degrading with population — the shape survives and only the number is void. Where the number was the design, it is gone. Nothing below inherits a legacy value; two legacy figures happen to reappear in the results and that coincidence is treated as coincidence, not as corroboration.

The unit: a contact, and why it is not a hull

The design is bounded by what a person can read, so the sizing unit has to be a unit of reading rather than a unit of content. A contact is one coherent object of attention — one thing that moves, routes and breaks as a single body, and that one coverage decision answers.

This is not a convenience. It is what the tracking literature actually measures. Attentive tracking runs to roughly three to five items, and the same capacity applies to groups: group tracking limits are about four groups and are independent of how many items compose each group. Two further findings pin the edges of the rule — group tracking degrades as inter-object spacing increases, and it degrades when common fate is disrupted. In other words, a body of ships holding a shape is genuinely one tracked object, and it stops being one the moment it spreads out or stops moving as a unit. The formation grammar in Ships §9 and the perceptual rule are the same rule.

A wave may arrive wide and simple, or narrow and clever — never both.

The two counts, kept strictly apart — this is the load-bearing distinction

Everything downstream depends on not confusing these, and they are easy to confuse because both are "how many enemies."

  • CONTACTS are the attention budget. They are capped hard and low, because they are capped by human tracking. This is the number the legibility ceiling constrains.
  • HULLS are the statistical substrate. Resolve, fear propagation, contagion, command-confusion batching and the rout cascade are all population mechanics — they need bodies to run through. This number can be several times the contact count.

A wave of 2 contacts made of 4 hulls each is two objects to read and eight bodies for the network to propagate through. That is a trivially legible wave and a fully functioning resolve layer, at the same time. The compression the formation buys is precisely what lets the game be simple to read and rich underneath.

This corrects an error in the first pass of this section, which computed the resolve layer's population floor in hulls and then compared it against a contact count — two different quantities — and concluded that resolve could not come online until the mid-War. That conclusion was wrong. Resolve is online from Campaign 1, provided early waves arrive in coherent formations, which is also what makes them easy to read. See Fleet Memory §7.

The ceiling: a trained skill, not a low cap revised ·

The Why §6 names legibility-under-load as the single point of failure, so fleet size is still sized against legibility rather than difficulty. What changed is the reading of what "legible" means at the top of the campaign.

The measured limits are these: attentive tracking runs to roughly three to five items ordinarily, rising toward eight when the objects move slowly and collapsing toward one when they move fast; and group tracking is about four groups, independent of how many items compose each group. Rivenkeep sits at the top of that range by construction — its contacts move at about a grid per second, they are visually differentiated by lineage rather than identical, and they are pre-learned, because the battle is deterministic and the wave has been met before. A player facing a known wave is predicting, not tracking.

Ruling (Jack): eight is the destination, not the danger line

"I understand that tracking 8 targets may be difficult, but that is war. I am hoping that when more targets are on the battlefield, the player has learned the mental skill to handle this."

So the late campaign targets 8 simultaneous contacts rather than treating 8 as a wall to stay behind. The ramp runs 2 → 8 across the War (Campaign §4), which means the player's tracking capacity is a trained quantity that rises with them, and the training is not left to chance: the mini-games are the instrument, and this is the single sharpest reason they are a pillar rather than an onboarding nicety. A player who cannot hold eight slow, familiar, differentiated contacts cannot play the late War, and the ladder that gets them there is a designed part of the game.

The law that makes 8 safe — attention triage, and why it is the same skill as coverage triage

Eight contacts is at the edge of what anyone can hold continuously. Above that edge a player stops tracking everything and starts sampling — checking a quiet approach every few seconds rather than watching it. That is not a failure mode; it is what a commander does. But it only works if the game never punishes a reasonable sampling pattern, so it becomes a hard authoring law:

At most four contacts may be decision-bearing at once.

Total contacts may reach eight; the number that are simultaneously asking something of the player must stay at or below four, which is the measured group-tracking figure and the same number the Fight §5 salience budget already implies. The surplus must be contacts that are legitimately ignorable for a spell: a formation still crossing open water, a wave already committed into a core and dying, a bearing the player has correctly read as quiet. Everything that is not asking for a decision must be glanceable.

Why this is elegant rather than a patch: the player already cannot cover every approach with guns, and now cannot watch every approach with attention. Those are the same problem at two scales, and they are solved by the same act — reading the ground and deciding where the loss goes. Coverage allocation and attention allocation are one skill, which is why raising the contact count deepens the game instead of merely loading it. Red line: if a board ever punishes a player for not watching a contact that gave no reason to be watched, the board has failed, not the player. This is the sibling of the ≥2-solutions law and the threat-spacing law, and it is owned the same way — by board and wave authoring (Campaign / Build triage).

≤ 3 simultaneously live approaches holds unchanged, and for a different reason than tracking: it is pan cost. Three bearings means at most two off-viewport, which bounds the off-screen-cue problem Fight §10 flags to two edge indicators. Four bearings cannot be attended even if the contact budget allows it.

The battlefield's size — derived, and it wants to be bigger new ·

Board size was inherited (64×48) and never derived. It is on the table, and the derivation says it is too small — not by a little. Three independent requirements each set a minimum, and they agree.

1 · Approach depth — how much water a contact must cross

A contact has to be readable and answerable before it arrives, so the water between spawn and shore is not empty space, it is the phase's thinking room. It breaks into three bands:

On a 64-wide board with a fort in the middle there are barely 32 grids to each side, so no approach on the current board is deep enough — which is a strong hint that the old 64×48 was sized for the pre-AoR game where the player tapped individual hulls and reading time was not a mechanic.

2 · The fear grid gives the same number, independently

The fleet's fear map is about 10×10 cells over the play area and that resolution is deliberate (Fleet Memory §3) — coarse enough that a cell cannot be inverted to a gun position, fine enough to read as a heat shape. For fear navigation to be visible — a hull skirting a hot cell, probing a cooling one, committing through a cold one — a contact must cross two to three fear cells on its approach. On a 128-wide board a cell is ~12.8 grids, so two to three cells is 26–38 grids. That is the same answer as band arithmetic above, reached from the enemy's side rather than the player's, and it is the load-bearing one: the fear grid is the fleet's entire visible personality, and it cannot be read on an approach too short to cross it.

Worth noting the direction of this: the fear grid stays 10×10 whatever the board size, so a bigger board makes each fear cell bigger and fear behaviour more legible, not less. This is an argument for growth, and one of very few places where scale helps legibility instead of hurting it.

3 · Fort core — what triage variety physically requires

The ≥2-near-equal-solutions law (Build §5) demands that a board offer genuinely different castle sets to seal — a cheap keystone cluster versus a cliff-backed pair plus an outlier. That requires castles at genuinely different spacings: some pairs 8–12 grids apart so one ring takes several, others 25+ apart so taking both is expensive. Ten castles at that spread, plus the courtyards to seat 12 guns and the rings to enclose them, wants a fort region of about 50×35 grids. Below that every castle set is a keystone set and triage collapses into one answer.

The recommendation: 128 × 80

Width = 40 (deep approach) + 50 (fort core) + 38 (second approach) ≈ 128. Height = 40 (third approach) + 35 (fort) + slack ≈ 80. That is 3.3× the area of 64×48, and it is the smallest board that supports the design's own stated maximum — three live approaches at full reading depth around a fort with real triage variety.

It still fits a phone, and here is why. Fitting 80 grids into ~360 pt of usable landscape height gives 4.5 pt per grid, so the whole 128-wide board renders in ~576 pt and sits inside a landscape viewport at overview. What matters is that the tracking read happens at contact scale, not gun scale: a four-hull formation spans ~6 grids and therefore ~27 pt, which is comfortably trackable; a 4-gun AoR is 16 grids and therefore ~72 pt, which is unmistakable. A 2×2 cannon renders at ~9 pt — visible as a mark, too small to tap. That is exactly the tradeoff ruled below, and it is correct: the overview is for reading the battle, not for commanding it.

The derived upper bound. A contact needs roughly 20 pt to be trackable, so at 6 grids per contact the floor is 3.33 pt per grid, which caps board height at about 108 grids on a phone. 80 sits well inside; 128 tall would not. Width is bounded by pan tolerance rather than by legibility.

128 × 80 CONFIRMED (Jack) — and the ratio is doing a second job

Accepted. Worth recording why the aspect is a fortunate result rather than an arbitrary one: 128 × 80 is exactly 16:10, a standard video ratio, and Jack's note is that "this is a video playback ratio with banner information."

That matters because of the replay export, which is 9:16 vertical (1080 × 1920) with panels carrying HUD and branding. A 16:10 board fitted to 1080 px wide renders at 1080 × 675, leaving 1245 px of vertical frame for banner panels above and below — generous, and enough for a real title treatment rather than a cramped strip. So the board's shape is simultaneously the shape the design needs and the shape the share format wants.

Why not 16:9 (128 × 72), which would need no letterboxing at all? Because 72 is below the derived minimum: the height requirement is 40 grids of approach plus a 35-grid fort core, so 75 is the floor and 80 is the floor plus slack. 72 would force either a compressed fort core (costing triage variety) or a shallow approach (costing reading time). The board's proportions are set by the game; 16:10 letterboxing into a 16:9 frame is thin bars and costs nothing.

What growth costs, honestly
  • It does not inflate the Build job. Wall demand is driven by perimeter, not board area (Build §5), and perimeter is set by the fort core, which is sized above on its own grounds. A 3.3× board does not mean 3.3× the pieces.
  • It does inflate authoring. 3.3× the terrain per board, across 190 battles and 10 theaters. This is the real cost and it lands on the battle generator, not on the design.
  • It inflates panning, which is answered by the zoom ruling below rather than by shrinking the board.
  • It raises the entity count in view, which makes the performance requirement below a hard one rather than a nicety.

A middle option exists if 3.3× is too much authoring: 96×64 (2× area) supports ~32-grid approaches and a ~32-grid fort core — under the derived 36–40 on depth and under 50×35 on the core, so it buys most of the reading room while accepting slightly compressed triage variety. It is a defensible compromise; 64×48 is not, because it fails every one of the three requirements above.

Zoom and pan — a designed information tradeoff, and a hard performance requirement (Jack)

"Also, understand that the player will be able to zoom in and out. This zoom and pan SHOULD ALWAYS BE PERFORMANT. The phone size is part of the game. A commander does not always have a full picture when targeting an AoR. On the flip side, an overview does make it harder to target AoRs."

This is now canon, and it is a genuine mechanic rather than a UI concession. The screen is a scarce resource and the two zoom levels trade against each other:

Out (board scale)The whole read — every contact, every AoR extent, both fogs, the fear shape, the fort's saturation as a health gaugeCommand precision. Guns are marks, not shapes; a nudge at overview is coarse.

So the commander is genuinely choosing between seeing and acting, moment to moment — which is the attention economy (Fight §5) expressed in the camera instead of in the eye. It also means the zoom level is part of the plan: a player who scheduled a crawl in Deploy knows they must zoom in to execute it, and therefore knows they will be briefly blind elsewhere. That blindness is the cost of the command, and it is the most diegetic cost in the game.

The performance requirement is load-bearing, not polish. Because zoom and pan carry information the player needs continuously, any stutter, lag or frame drop during them is not a rough edge — it is a read the player has lost, in a real-time phase, on a decision timer. The requirement is therefore stated as a design constraint rather than an engineering aspiration: pan and zoom must remain smooth at the widest wave the campaign fields, on the oldest supported device. If a board's entity count cannot hold that, the board is too big or the wave is too wide — the fix is a smaller board or a narrower wave, never a coarser camera. Implementation belongs in the SDD (Rivenkeep_SDD.html); the constraint belongs here, because it caps board size and wave width as surely as legibility does.

The hull population — and what Rampart actually licenses

Rampart is the acknowledged benchmark, and it supplies a hard number: a maximum of 16 ships in play at any time, on a single screen, with the player aiming a crosshair by hand. That is worth reading carefully, because it is evidence about the wrong thing if taken at face value. Rampart never asks the player to read sixteen ships' intent — its ships often sit still, and the task is picking one target at a time out of a crowd. So 16 is a population figure, not a tracking figure. It licenses hull count; it does not license contact count.

Applied: Rivenkeep's board is roughly four phone viewports, and the player never targets an individual hull. So a simultaneous population of 16–24 hulls is well within what a comparable game has shipped legibly — provided those hulls compose into no more than 6–8 contacts. That population is also comfortably above what the resolve network needs to behave as a network rather than as a handful of individual morale checks.

The floor: allocation needs two shortfalls, not one

Allocation exists only when you cannot cover everything, and it has to bite in both ways or it is thin:

  • Spatial shortfall — more live bearings than commandable groups, so a bearing must be left thin. On its own this is a good decision but a binary one: which flank do you abandon.
  • Throughput shortfall — a covered bearing still leaks, because the group cannot account for everything crossing it. On its own this is a damage check, and damage checks drift toward the stat treadmill the design refuses.

With both present the decision becomes the real one: which approach do I cover, and how much do I concentrate there knowing the covered one still bleeds.

TKP and k₁ — derived twice, from sources that share no quantity

TKP(n) — transit kills per passage — is the hulls a group of n guns accounts for while one contact crosses its zone. Two effects compound. Barrels scale with n: four guns fire four times as often as one. Time in the zone scales with √n: because AoR area is linear in gun count (Jack's ruling above), the radius — and therefore the chord a contact crosses — grows as the square root. Multiplying the two:

TKP(n) = k₁ · n1.5

The exponent is now settled, and it was the biggest open assumption in the chain. The previous pass flagged AoR area-scaling as the single most load-bearing unknown, because the exponent rides on it: linear area gives 1.5, whereas linear radius would give 2.0 (barrels ∝ n × time ∝ n). Jack's ruling settles it as area. Worth recording what the alternative would have meant, since the difference is not cosmetic: under n² the curve is pinned at the same place for a 4-gun group but is much steeper below it — TKP(1) would be 0.23 rather than 0.45, and TKP(2) 0.92 rather than 1.27 — so small groups would be relatively far weaker and the pressure to consolidate into one big group would be considerably stronger. Linear area is both the simpler mental model and the gentler consolidation curve, which is the right combination.

Route 1 — top-down, from the locked star thresholds. ★★★ Ships Destroyed is 90%+ and ★★ is 60% (§2, inherited from the GDD and not up for revision). Survivors retreat and re-emerge, so they are shot at again each sortie, which makes the cumulative kill fraction over N sorties ≈ 1 − (1 − S)N, where S is the fraction of arrivals the fort accounts for per passage. At the reference N = 3: 90% requires S ≈ 0.55, and 60% requires S ≈ 0.25. A late-War wave is ~20 hulls, so excellent play must account for ~11 hulls per passage. A late-War fort is 12 guns in 3 groups of 4, so Σ TKP = 3 · k₁ · 41.5 = 24 k₁ = 11 → k₁ ≈ 0.46.

Route 2 — bottom-up, from geometry and the pinned constants. Corrected v2.6.0. A 4-gun group has r = 8, so a diametral transit is 16 grids — which is what the engagement band was sized to guarantee; at 1 grid/s that is 16 s in the zone; at a 4 s reload that is 4 shots per gun; 4 guns × 4 shots = 16 shots; × 0.69 mean hit probability = 11.1 hits; ÷ 3 hits per hull = 3.7 hulls. So TKP(4) = 3.7 and k₁ = 3.7 ÷ 8 ≈ 0.46.

⚠ What was wrong, and why the fix strengthens the result

This route previously read “the mean chord across it is ~20 grids” — and a chord of a circle of radius 8 cannot exceed 16. The 20 was load-bearing: it produced 20 shots, which at an assumed 0.55 mean gave 11 hits and reproduced k₁. So the “two independent routes agree” claim rested on an impossible geometry, and it is right that the falloff rebuild was the session that caught it.

Fixing the chord drops the shot count to 16 and therefore raises the mean hit probability the route requires, to 0.69. That is exactly what a linear falloff to a 38–40% floor produces — so the mean is no longer an input to route 2, it is route 2’s output, and k₁ = 0.46 did not have to move. The most load-bearing constant in the game survived its own audit, and the cross-check is now stronger than the version that was wrong: route 1 pins k₁ from the star thresholds, route 2 pins the edge floor from k₁, and the falloff numbers stop being free parameters.

Two independent routes agree to two significant figures: k₁ = 0.45. The agreement carries weight because the routes share no quantity — route 1 uses the star thresholds, the sortie count and the fort's gun count; route 2 uses AoR geometry, ship speed, reload and hits-to-kill. They share Rampart as a source (hull HP in one, the reload rule in the other) but no single figure appears in both. Where they would disagree is where the model is wrong, so this is the first number in the game with a real cross-check under it.

S is not a difficulty lever — it is a consequence, and this matters

The supply ratio S is derived from the star thresholds, not chosen. The thresholds are fixed, therefore S is fixed: perfect allocation accounts for roughly 55% of arrivals per passage, and a player who simply accepts the default AoRs accounts for roughly 25%. That gap is the skill gradient the leaderboard measures.

Two consequences worth stating plainly, because both invert an intuition:

  • The fort is never meant to clear the sea. Even under perfect play roughly 45% of arrivals get through a passage. That is not a failure state — a single passage is not the battle. Victory is beating back the whole attack across many sorties while holding the castles (§2), so leakage on any one passage is normal and the wall is what absorbs it. A fort sized to sink everything in one pass would have no allocation decision in it at all, which is the same as having no defensive decision in it.
  • There is no third difficulty family. An earlier draft of this section treated the under-supply fraction as a dial and flagged the worry that a defender-side squeeze would be a third escalation track beside the two Campaign §3 allows — board-combinatorial and enemy-cognitive — and an invisible one, which would break the law that the player always understands why they lost. Now that S is derived rather than chosen, the worry dissolves: nothing escalates by squeezing the defender. What the five named difficulty levels move is the slot grant, and a gun count is fully visible to the player, so it is a legible handicap setting of the ordinary kind rather than a hidden difficulty track.

Sortie count — superseded, see §4b corrected ·

This section's derivation was built on the wrong win condition

An earlier pass derived sortie count as a construction schedule — how many Build phases it takes to lay the required wall — because the win condition was then believed to be enclosure. With the win condition corrected (§2), that derivation is wrong in kind: laying the wall no longer ends the battle, so it cannot set the battle's length.

Sortie count is now derived from the fleet, in §4b: the queue of formations divided by how fast the player eliminates them. Jack's 3–10 range and −1/+2 deviation are unchanged and are reproduced by the new derivation without being fitted to it.

What survives from the old work, and is genuinely useful, is the wall clock — the number of Build phases a requirement demands (Build §5). It is no longer the battle's length; it is the deadline the player must beat inside the battle's length. That is the race stated in §2, and it is a better problem than the one it replaces.

Also carried forward: the fading fleet. Pool ≈ 2.5× the peak wave, deliberately not enough to sustain full strength across ten sorties, so the back half of a long battle arrives thinner and more battered — which is why ten sorties is not harder than three. Under formation persistence (Ships §9) this now reads as composition rather than as count: late formations are patchwork, some at strength, some limping on damaged orphans, some coreless and unwilling.

Ships Accounted For — the scoring lever, re-derived revised ·

Jack's original question was whether ships that never emerged can count as kills. The answer changed when the win condition was corrected, so both the reasoning and the rule are restated here.

What the corrected win condition did to the old objection

The previous pass refused a flat "unmet ships count" rule because it was exploitable: a player could win by enclosing quickly at the end of an early Build phase, leaving most of the pool unmet, and bank ★★★ on the ships lever for having sunk almost nothing. That exploit no longer exists, because you cannot win by enclosing at all — the attack has to be beaten back first (§2). Unemerged reserves can therefore only exist in one situation: the fleet stopped coming. Which is precisely the achievement Jack's question was pointing at.

So the objection dissolves. What survives from it is the underlying principle, and it is still the right one: score by cause, never by absence. Because there remain two very different reasons a fleet stops coming, and only one of them is something the player did.

The rule

The unemerged credit is proportional to cores destroyed, and that single choice does all the work. Rather than testing which kind of retreat happened — which would need the engine to distinguish a resolve break from a stalemate drain and invite argument at the edges — the credit simply scales with the thing that actually causes purpose collapse:

  • You sank all four carriers and their escorts went home. Cores destroyed = 100%, so the reserves count in full. Correct: you ended the fleet's ability to fight, and the hulls that never sailed never sailed because of that.
  • You turtled and the fleet lost heart with its carriers intact. Cores destroyed ≈ 0%, so the reserves count for nothing. Correct: they stayed home because you declined the battle, not because you won it.
  • You sank two of four carriers and the rest withdrew. Half credit. Correct, and pleasingly so — partial achievement, partial reward, with no threshold to cliff-edge on.

Why this is better than the retreat-type test it replaces: it is one continuous quantity that the game already tracks, it cannot be gamed by turtling, it needs no special case for the stalemate path, and — best of all — it points the scoring at the same target the win condition points at. Hunt the cores: it is how you win, and it is how you score. A design where the optimal line and the winning line are the same line is a design that does not need to argue with its players.

Unchanged, so the leaderboard is untouched

Thresholds stay any / 60% / 90% / 100%, and the denominator stays the capped, deterministic pool. Two players on the same board still face the identical roster in the identical order, so the metric remains exactly comparable. The lever is renamed from Ships Destroyed in §2, and only the numerator's definition has changed.

4Sortie Resolution — Three End-States

A sortie is one committed wave. It attacks, spends its ammo, and pulls back to reload — that withdrawal is the natural end of the sortie. Its outcome is one of three, gated by the wave's visible resolve (the per-ship network — Ships §4) and its ammo. This doc owns the three end-states as mechanics; the Fight doc owns reading which one is coming before it is stamped (fire slackening → held; signatures cascading aft → repel tipping; a Committed hull driving a seam → breakthrough imminent). One coupling clarified (Jack): the checkpoint is always the battle — the whole battle is the replayable, scored unit. A battle runs as waves of sorties and a sortie as its phases, but those are pacing / document subdivisions, not save points, so an individual sortie's outcome is a step within a battle run rather than a separately-replayable result. Replaying (win or lose) re-runs the battle deterministically; the end-states below are the resolution of each sortie within a run, and Battle Score (§2) totals the run.

4a · Beating back the attack — the two ways a fleet stops new ·

Because victory now requires the attack to be beaten back, "the fleet stops" needs a precise definition rather than a feeling. Ruling (Jack): "The ships' defeat comes when there are no more ships, or, maybe, when the ships no longer have a purpose to come out — like when we had 4 aircraft carriers with 6 screen ships and all 4 carriers were sunk. The screen ships have no purpose. That is why they retreat. This is all about the resolve."

A · ANNIHILATION — there are no more ships

The queue is empty and nothing is left on the field. The literal end, and the rarest: it requires sinking every hull including the ones that fled. Full credit on every lever, and the cleanest ★★★! path there is.

B · PURPOSE COLLAPSE — the ships have nothing left to come out for

The elegant one, and it grounds resolve in something structural rather than only in fear. The fleet's hulls divide into two kinds:

KindWhich lineagesWhat it is for
Purpose-bearing — the coresTransports (deliver troops), carriers (launch bombers), bombards and breachers (break wall), the flagship (command)These are the ships that can actually accomplish something against the fort. They are the fleet's reason to sail.
Purpose-serving — the screensBulwarks, Quartermasters, escorting raidersThese exist to get a core to where it can do its job. They have no objective of their own.

Sink every core and the screens are hulls with nothing to escort and no way to win. Their resolve does not merely drop — it has nothing to hold it up. The fleet stops committing, and the attack is beaten back with ships still afloat.

Two constraints on this, ruled by Jack — it is a pressure, not a kill switch
  • Sinking the last core does not end the battle. "You shouldn't be able to kill the payload ship and the battle ends. Those screen ships can do damage too and they might be out for revenge." Orphaned screens stay on the board, stay armed, and stay dangerous — and losing its purpose may make a hull briefly more committed rather than less, a fanatical spike before the collapse. So purpose collapse is a resolve pressure that develops, and the battle ends when the fleet stops committing rather than the instant a hull class runs out.
  • The symmetric route: strip the screens and the cores may refuse to sail. "Kill enough screen ships and the payload ships may not come out to battle." A core will not risk an unescorted run, so there are two opposite legitimate lines to beating the attack back — kill the cores so the screens lose their reason, or kill the screens so the cores lose their nerve. They suit different boards and different fleets, and they are answered by different defender tools (deep-centred zones and back-line personalities for one; forward coverage and area weapons for the other). That symmetry is what stops core-hunting becoming the single dominant strategy, which was the real risk this section carried after it was written.

Both routes are now designed (v2.5.0). The formation-composition session settled the four items this paragraph used to defer: the elimination rule (§4b), the revenge reading and the orphan fork (Ships §9), and the sortie-refusal threshold (immediately below). What was settled before and still holds is the shape: two routes, both valid, neither instant.

SORTIE REFUSAL — the nerve route, with a threshold new ·

Derived from the same unit the screen slots are: one shield slot ≈ one TKP-hull of absorbed throughput (Ships §9). A core sails when its escort can pay for the crossing it has to make. So:

A core refuses to sail when its shields fall below half its own screen requirement.

Re-evaluated every sortie, so refusal is a stall, not a state — the core sails again as soon as the fleet can screen it. That is the same shape as purpose collapse: a pressure, not a kill switch. Riders do not count — only shields, because only a shield can occupy the position that draws your fire.

Every board offers a route — reading which one is the skill, which is the no-forced-playstyle law holding at fleet-structure level.

The tell must be a presence, not an absence

“No payload sailed this sortie” is an absence, and absences are the hardest thing in the game to read — a player who cannot see why nothing came out has not learned anything. The fix uses geometry that is already always visible: spawns and safe launch zones are never fogged (Fight, cont.11). So put the refusing cores there — loitering in the launch zone, visibly declining to come out, while their wolfpacks sail without them. The player sees the payloads sitting at home. That is the nerve route’s equivalent of the coreless residual, and it is readable at a glance.

The fleet’s own behaviour supplies the second half of the tell: because at least one convoy sails every sortie while a convoy is willing (§4b), an all-wolfpack sortie is itself the signal that the cores have lost their nerve. The refusal state and the preparation sortie are the same event seen from two sides.

Not exploitable into a farm: refusing cores still bleed to Retreat via stalemate drain (Ships §4), and the credit rule in §3 pays nothing for reserves whose cores are intact. Stripping screens is also the expensive route — it costs many sorties, banks no time, and scores badly on Cannon Health. That is the intended symmetry with decapitation’s difficulty.

Why this is the best thing in the victory model: it makes decapitation at fleet scale the primary strategic line, and it gives every back-line-targeting tool in the defender kit a reason that runs all the way to the win condition. The Marksman, the back-line target personality, the fight-through-the-screen problem, the flagship's screening behaviour — all of it now points at the same thing: find what the fleet is for, and kill that. A player who sinks four carriers has won more decisively than one who sank sixteen escorts, and the game now agrees with them.

C · The third path, which must stay valid-but-costly — stalemate retreat

Resolve also drains from lack of progress, which guarantees termination and rules out an infinite stalemate (Ships §4). So a player who simply turtles and never fights will eventually see the fleet lose heart and retreat. That path has to remain possible — the design forbids a forced playstyle — but it must never be the good one, and three things hold that line:

  • It is slow. Stalemate drain is a trickle compared to a rout cascade, so it costs many sorties.
  • The castles pay for the time. Every sortie under fire is another chance at a degradation tick, and the requirement is what you must still be holding at the end.
  • It scores badly, by construction. See the credit rule in §3 — unemerged reserves are credited in proportion to the purpose-bearing hulls destroyed, so a fleet that left with its carriers intact hands the player nothing.

This is the same shape as the already-designed "outlast the magazine" option: a real choice, honestly available, and reliably worse than fighting well.

4b · The queue — what comes out, and how many sorties there are new ·

Ruling (Jack): "Which ships come out per sortie is based on how many target groups are allowed for that battle and how many targets are in the queue." That replaces the previous derivation of sortie count wholesale, and it is a much better model because it makes battle length a property of the fleet rather than of the player's construction job.

The two numbers that produce a sortie
  • C — target groups allowed this battle. The contact budget: how many formations may be on the board at once, ramping 2 → 8 across the campaign (§ the ceiling, and Campaign §4). This is the legibility number and it is the hard cap on a sortie's width.
  • Q — formations in the queue. The battle's total roster of formations, capped and fixed in order. This is the depth behind the width.

A sortie fields min(C, formations remaining), and survivors have priority: a formation that pulled back re-forms (below) and goes out first next sortie, with fresh formations drawn from the queue to fill the remaining slots. So the queue drains only by however much the player finished — which is the point, and it is why repelling produces more sorties than grinding does.

sorties ≈ Q ÷ (formations eliminated per sortie)

THE ELIMINATION RULE — what “finished” means new ·

The formula above is only as good as its denominator, and “eliminated” was undefined. It is now:

A convoy is eliminated the moment its last core slot empties.
A wolfpack is eliminated when its last hull dies — or when its errand becomes void.

That is one rule stated twice: a formation is eliminated when its purpose can no longer be carried. In a convoy the purpose lives in a hull; in a wolfpack it is distributed across every hull. The three formation kinds are owned by Ships §9.

Why not the two alternatives

  • Not “all hulls dead.” That makes the elimination rate a hull-clearing rate — a throughput metric — and it inverts the incentive: killing screens would shorten the battle faster than killing cores, which is the opposite of the design’s thesis. Damage is temporary; purpose is permanent.
  • Not “core dead + orphans absorbed elsewhere.” That makes the rate depend on the fleet’s bookkeeping instead of the player’s act. The count becomes retroactive and unreadable, and a fleet with no under-screened formation to absorb into would never register an elimination the player had plainly earned.

The real objection behind the third option, answered. Kill a core and Q drops by one while the hull count does not move at all — the surviving escorts are still afloat. That is not a leak, it is the tradeoff working: killing cores fast makes the remaining battle shorter but denser — fewer sorties, more banked Last Stand time, and thicker formations to face on the way there, because released escorts reinforce what is left. It is the same rhythm as repel-versus-grind, one level up.

Errand-void — eliminating a wolfpack without killing it

A wolfpack’s purpose is its errand, so removing the reason removes the formation:

  • A Reaver pack whose fixed gun is destroyed — or re-enclosed, so the fix goes cold.
  • A Recon picket whose sector is already mapped.
  • A Sower that has laid its field.
  • A Provocateur you never fired at.

So enclosure discipline literally shortens the battle, and bait-and-relocate pays off at the queue level rather than only at the hull level. It also supplies the attrition that stops skittish wolfpacks clogging the front of the queue, and it closes the endgame with no special case: once every convoy is eliminated, no wolfpack has a convoy to prepare for, every errand voids, the packs stand down, and the fleet retreats. Purpose collapse resolves through the wolfpack rule rather than beside it.

HULL CAP BEATS CONTACT CAP LATE — so a sortie is wide OR heavy, physically new ·

Realistic escort ratios expose a collision between three separately-derived limits: 16–24 simultaneous hulls (from Rampart), 6–8 simultaneous contacts (from tracking research), and convoys of 5–12 hulls (from convoy history). Eight contacts made of real convoys would be 60+ hulls — three times the cap. Eight contacts requires ≈3-hull formations, and the fleet no longer has those in quantity.

This is not a contradiction to patch — it is an existing law becoming physical. "A wave may arrive wide and simple, or narrow and clever — never both" was written as an authoring constraint on the contact budget. The hull cap now enforces it without anyone authoring anything:

CORE COUNT RAISED — 4 / 9 / 14, and it changes what winning by decapitation costs Jack, v2.6.0

Ruling (Jack): “‘Core count at ~10 late is the softest number in the pass.’ Then make it harder. When I sight a number, it is only a suggestion to the current state. If we need to modify that number to fit the game better, then do it.”

The old figure (10 late) was fitted to a feel target — about one core per sortie of good hunting — and that turned out to be the problem: it made the decapitation route land exactly at the end of a short battle, so hunting cores alone both won the battle and won the banking. Late cores are now 14, derived instead from throughput:

  • What a strong decapitator manages — bounded by the coverage law, not assumed. The previous pass wrote "≈1.4 core-kills per sortie" by assuming a doctrine of one hunting group and two covering. The assumption is removable, because the number of hunting groups is forced: at most 3 approaches may be live and a late fort is 3 groups of 4, so holding every bearing consumes every group and any group diverted to deep-centred core-hunting uncovers a bearing outright. An uncovered bearing delivers its payloads intact, which is a breakthrough and a castle-degradation tick — survivable for a sortie, not for eight. So:
    Hunting groups hCore-kills / sortieSustainable?
    0≈0.6 (leak only, 3 groups × 0.21)Yes — but decapitation never happens
    1≈1.2 (0.8 hunting + 2 × 0.2 leak)Yes — one bearing thin, absorbed by the wall

    So the honest figure is a range with a hard ceiling: 1.2 sustained, up to ≈1.5 with occasional bursts — derived from the approach limit and the leak fraction, both of which are themselves derived. Nothing here is a taste.
  • 14 cores ÷ 1.2–1.5 = 9.3 to 11.7 sorties. The sortie cap is 10. So pure decapitation cannot reliably finish inside the battle at all — it lands at the cap in the best case and past it in the sustained case. That is a stronger statement than the last pass's "it buys the long battle," and it is the intended consequence of Jack's ruling. The boundary is worth recording because it is the actual design choice: 12 cores would make pure decapitation finish exactly at the cap; 14 makes it require help. 14 is chosen.
  • Purpose collapse stays a real route — it just stops being a solo one. You reach it by killing cores and by stripping screens until survivors refuse (§4a) and their wolfpack escorts void their errands. Neither half suffices; together they close it comfortably inside 7–8 sorties.
  • So the 7-sortie battle requires mixing routes. To finish faster you must also strip screens hard enough that the surviving cores start refusing (§4a), which stalls the queue into all-wolfpack preparation sorties whose errands then void (the elimination rule above). The two routes stop being alternatives and become complements, which is a stronger result than the cont.17 version and it holds at the level of arithmetic rather than only at the level of tools.

How 14 cores are paid for, and the second-order effect that falls out. Not by growing the pool — it stays ≈2.5× the peak wave. Cores rise from ≈19% to ≈24% of hulls and shields fall to pay for it, which is affordable only because shared screens scale sub-linearly (Ships §9): the late fleet runs 14 cores across 7 convoys, so most convoys carry two or three. The consequence is a good one and was not designed in: a late fleet commits more purpose behind thinner escorts. That is the fading fleet expressed as composition, it makes each individual core easier to reach, and — because refusal is measured in shields — it means the nerve route opens naturally as the battle goes long. The fleet gets more desperate and more brittle at the same time.

The elimination rate moves, and the sortie range does not. Because a convoy is only eliminated when its last core dies, packing cores into convoys lowers the rate: 15 formations over 7–10 sorties is ≈1.5–2.1 per sortie, superseding the earlier ≈2–2.5. Jack’s 3–10 sortie range — the number that actually matters — is unchanged, and 15 ÷ 1.5 = 10 reconciles at the slow end while 15 ÷ 2.1 = 7.1 reconciles at the fast end.

Falls out: decapitation is not available early, and should not be

Early cores are 4, but an early fort is 2–4 guns — nowhere near the throughput to deep-centre a zone and still hold an approach. So Campaign 1–6 cannot be won by core-hunting, and must be won by annihilation (the pool is only 17 hulls) or by holding the wall clock. The decapitation route debuts when gun count reaches 5–8, which is a teaching-order fact the campaign should use deliberately rather than an accident: Introduce → Isolate → Combine applied to the win condition itself. THE FELT TARGET — "real, and always just barely overwhelming" (Jack) new ·

The provenance table below says where every number came from. This says what they are all for, and it is Jack's phrase: the game has to feel real and always just barely overwhelming. Writing it down revealed it was already the hidden through-line under several rulings made separately:

  • Contacts: "eight is the destination, not the danger line" — the late campaign targets the measured tracking ceiling rather than a safe distance below it.
  • Decision-bearing load: ≤4 is a cap the late game should sit against, not a budget to underspend.
  • Supply: S ≈ 0.55 means 45% of arrivals leak through even under perfect play. The fort is permanently and deliberately insufficient — and that is now a stated intention rather than a consequence.
  • The wall: difficulty rises by the requirement squeezing freedom, not by the enemy growing.

The law: sit at the top of every legible band, never the middle.

The test: at the widest wave the campaign fields, a player should be able to say what every approach is doing — and have no attention left over while doing it. Slack means the band is underspent. That is a sharper version of the test already in §3, which asked only the first half.

"Barely" is the red line, and it is not negotiable. Overwhelming is the target; unfair is a failure. The existing protections are what hold them apart: no board may punish a player for not watching a contact that gave no reason to be watched, every loss must be attributable, and no board may be winnable only by placing fast. Overwhelmed-and-reading is the goal. Overwhelmed-and-guessing is a bug.

Consequence for this section's numbers: derivation from real-world figures is step one of two. Step two asks what was scarce in the real case, what is scarce here instead, and which way the asymmetry pushes — and it pushes one way consistently, because bits die cheaply where humans do not. A historically-calibrated pressure feels slacker in-game than it did in life, so where a real range exists and the design must choose, choose against the player. Authoritative home for both laws: Rivenkeep_Why.html §4; recorded in the journal panel meanwhile.

What is different about this version. Every term now traces to something outside the design: core-kills per sortie to the approach limit and the derived leak fraction; shields per core to the 1943 convoy operations-research finding; the hit probabilities to the locked star thresholds; the Lone Wolf floor to sniper rounds-per-kill; hull population and hits-to-kill to Rampart. The only figure still supplied by preference is Jack's 3–10 sortie range — which is the right place for the one judgement call to live, because it is a statement about how long a battle should feel, and nothing outside the design can answer that.

The sortie composition law — at least one convoy, while one is willing

A sortie fields min(C, remaining) with survivors first — but the fleet sails to accomplish something, so at least one convoy sails every sortie while a convoy is willing to, and the fleet spends at most one consecutive all-wolfpack preparation sortie.

Without this the model degenerates: wolfpack lineages are mostly skittish (Corsair, Recon, Sower, Wraith), so they repel early, survive, re-emerge first, and clog the front of the queue — and the player can never reach a core. With it, there is always a payload out there to find, which is also the sortie’s readable structure. And the exception carries the refusal tell: an all-wolfpack sortie means no core was willing (§4a).

The flag needs no exception. Because the flag is a marker on an ordinary hull with a job of its own (Ships §4, revised), field promotion moves the standard elsewhere in the fleet rather than resurrecting the formation it left — so the command convoy is eliminated by the normal rule and counts inside Q like any other.

Skill moves the elimination rate, and therefore the sortie count, but only within a tight band — Jack's −1 to +2. Retreat is not safe passage

A withdrawal happens inside the Fight — so retreating ships and troops take incoming fire as they flee. This folds "annihilation" into the model with no extra concept: annihilation is simply a repel where you sank the whole wave on its way out, and a total sink drains the pool toward the Last Stand.

Honest watch-out — same ships, same order can calcify

"You see the same wave again" is a strength only while the wave is still worth re-reading. If the cognition creep is too faint and the new-ship injections too light, a strong player turns re-engagement into a memorised re-run — solved, not strategic. The creep and the injections must carry real weight here; this is the determinism-vs-renewable-strategy tension (The Why §6) living inside one mechanic.

5Ammo — Why the Fleet Pulls Back

The engine under the sortie rhythm. Ships can only shoot what they carry. A wave spends its magazines and must fall back to reload — that, not an arbitrary clock, is why a sortie ends and why even a winning wave withdraws.

The asymmetry — attacker on a clock, defender not

Ships are mobile, so their ammo is finite. Cannons are static, so they sit on effectively unlimited stores — a fortress hoards powder a fleet can't carry. This is literally true of ships versus forts, and it hands the defender a structural edge: every enemy shot is a spent resource; yours is not. Ammo becomes the sortie's diegetic clock — the wave attacks until its punch is spent, then reloads during the Build/Deploy pause. The Fight timer does not disappear, though: it stays the phase's time budget, and the time you don't spend rolls forward (see below). Ammo decides when the wave withdraws; the timer measures how much time you banked doing it.

Time is the commodity — ending early pays

The GDD's time economy runs underneath all of this and is kept whole: unused Build time rolls to Deploy, unused Deploy to Fight, 50% of unused Fight time rolls to the next sortie's Build, and on the final sortie 100% of remaining Fight time rolls to the Last Stand. Ammo and rollover interlock cleanly: a repel (or a full annihilation) ends the Fight before the wave's clock runs out, so more time rolls forward — you are paid twice for routing them, once in the cut-short wave and again in the banked time. A held sortie spends the whole magazine and burns most of the timer, so little rolls over. The Last Stand is the culmination of everything banked — every sortie you ended early feeds the final chance to enclose. Time saved is time to win with.

Relative, per-lineage — a fiction on the Firepower axis

Amounts are relative, never exact counts, and they read straight off lineage × axis: heavy shooters (Bombard, Leviathan) carry big, slow-firing loads; raiders (Corsair) carry little and run dry fast — hit-and-run made literal; and the delivery lineages barely shoot at all — a Runner or a Breacher culminates by dropping its payload, not by emptying a magazine, so ammo is nearly moot for them. Firepower-axis ships carry more; evasion-axis ships less.

Keep it felt, not managed

Ammo must stay a felt property — the player sees the fire slacken and the wave pull back to reload — never a numeric magazine bar on every hull. A managed ammo stat would be exactly the cognitive load that erodes the thinking game (The Why §4). The player reasons about "they're nearly spent," not about counters.

The strategy it unlocks

Two new decisions fall out, both serving strategy-as-fun. Bait them into wasting ammo — a feint that draws fire on a decoy now costs them rounds, not just position; deception gains an attrition payoff. And outlast the magazine — weather their fire until they are dry — becomes a genuine option. It stays valid-but-costly, never dominant: pure turtling invites breakthrough and kills nothing, tanking your Battle Score, so the breakthrough threats and the star gradient hold it in check.

6Breakthrough — Hurt, Not Devastate

Breakthrough is the sharp end of the sortie loop, and it must cost enough to sting without ever dropping the player into a hopeless spiral. The design goal: one breakthrough hurts and is recoverable; repeated breakthroughs on the same front are what lose the battle.

The event, defined objectively

A breakthrough is not a fuzzy damage threshold — it is a single legible event: the wave breaks a required castle's enclosure. That is the exact mirror of the win condition, so it is unambiguous and it threatens the one thing that matters. When a required ring falls, the sortie's outcome is stamped breakthrough.

The locked-in cost — three layers that scale
  • The ring is broken → that castle drops out of enclosure and must be re-enclosed next Build. The immediate pressure.
  • Cannons in the breach zone are lost → the emotional cost: the veterans you nurtured, gone. This is where breakthrough hurts.
  • A degradation tick on the newly-exposed castle → the compounding cost, nudging it toward 3/3 destruction.

So one breakthrough is a real wound — rebuild the ring, mourn the guns, watch the degradation — but survivable. Defeat only arrives when breakthroughs compound: a castle taken to 3/3 that drops you below the required count. Never fatal on its own; fatal only as the last straw.

The anti-death-spiral guarantee

Why the breaching wave withdraws instead of pressing its advantage: a wave that punches through has culminated — it concentrated force and spent ammo to breach, and real doctrine is breach → consolidate → exploit with a fresh wave, never overextend a spent one into a defender about to counterattack and rebuild. So it falls back to reload and lets the next, heavier, wiser wave exploit — which, not by accident, hands the player the rebuild window. Ships do not vanish; they retreat having succeeded, the same motion as any withdrawal. Combined with the mid-Build impossibility check (if you genuinely cannot re-enclose, the battle ends at once in a clean loss), this delivers the guarantee: the player always has either a real path to recover or a clean, understood defeat — never a drawn-out lost cause. That resolves the two-minds worry: reset the board so recovery is always possible; let the locked-in damage carry the stakes.

7Cannon Carry — Three Veterans Across Campaigns

Within a campaign, then a chosen few beyond it

Inside a campaign, cannon veterancy carries battle-to-battle as the GDD has it — guns grow toward 200% across a campaign's fights, and losing one to a breach is a real loss. At a campaign boundary, only the three best cannons carry forward — the player chooses them, with our recommendation — as a long emotional through-line: three veterans that survive the whole War. Every other cannon resets fresh at the new campaign.

Why capped carry — a principle check

Carrying only three (capped at 200%) is deliberate, not stingy. A full veteran fleet carried across campaigns would make later battles easier and let the player win by out-gunning instead of out-thinking — the exact stat-snowball The Why forbids, wearing a friendlier coat. Three keeps it an emotional attachment, never a power ramp. It also protects the fairness goal: less carried strength keeps campaign starts comparable on the leaderboard. Two conditions make it honest — difficulty is tuned to assume you hold a small veteran core (so it never trivialises), and the clean-slate-per-campaign reset is preserved for everything else.

8Why It Works — the North-Star Check

An honest read of the whole model against the one test: does it keep strategy the fun?

What it gets right
  • The win stays a crisp goal (enclose the required castles) while Battle Score turns how well into an honest, skill-ranked number — the fairness engine, already built.
  • The capped, ordered pool makes escalation bounded and deterministic — the anti-treadmill rule satisfied, and emergent battle length rewarding the strong player.
  • Ammo gives the sortie a diegetic clock (with the GDD time economy intact beneath it — ending early banks more time toward the Last Stand), explains the pullback with real logic, and opens two genuine strategies (bait-to-waste, outlast-the-magazine) — depth from an intuitive asymmetry, not a new managed system.
  • Same-ships-same-order makes the information war symmetric and teaches motivation-not-script — stable roles, rising skill: the ideal knowledge structure for a game that must never fully solve.
  • Breakthrough hurts without devastating, and the withdraw-to-reload reset guarantees recover-or-lose-cleanly — no death spirals, the player always understands the state.
The two things to keep honest

First, the re-run risk: "same wave again" stays strategic only if the cognition creep and new-ship injections carry real weight — otherwise re-engagement calcifies into a memorised sequence. Second, the ammo-turtle: outlast-the-magazine must stay valid-but-costly, held in check by breakthrough threats and the star gradient, or it drifts toward a dominant auto-win. Both are live instances of the foundational tensions — watch them as the numbers get tuned.
