1What This Doc Is

The victory model: how a battle is won, how each sortie resolves, and the ammo logic that ties them together. The battle win-condition and star system are the GDD's — kept intact because they are already right. What this adds is the sortie-resolution layer (the three end-states) and the ammo model that gives them a real-world cause.

The shape in one breath

You win a battle by enclosing the required castles — nothing else. Stars and Battle Score grade how well you did. Underneath, a battle is a series of sorties — waves drawn from a capped, fixed-order ship pool — and each wave attacks until it has spent its ammo and withdraws to reload, unless you rout it first or it breaches you along the way. How the sorties go decides how much of the pool you ever face.

2The Battle Win — Kept From the GDD

Victory: enclosure, full stop

The player wins the moment all required castles are enclosed at the end of any Build phase (including the Last Stand). That is the sole win condition. Ships never have to be destroyed — surviving ships stay a threat, but the battle is won the instant the enclosure requirement is met. Clean, legible, and the crisp goal a strategy game needs.

Defeat: the walls are the health bar

Defeat is reached only at a genuinely unwinnable state, and the game never forces a lost cause: required castles un-enclosable at the end of a Build phase, or a castle degraded to 3/3 dropping you below the required count. If mid-Build it becomes mathematically impossible to enclose, the battle ends immediately — a clean loss the player understands, never a drawn-out death.

Stars & Battle Score — the skill metric (this is the fairness engine)

Three independent levers — Ships Destroyed, Cannon Health, Area Enclosed — each 1–3 stars, averaged and rounded up, with a hidden ★★★! for near-perfect play. Beneath the stars, Battle Score = ships% + area% + cannon% (max 400) is the real prize: full-resolution performance, so two players with identical ★★★ have different scores and the leaderboard knows who played better. This one mechanic is the fairness vision of The Why §3 — skill, not luck, ranked honestly — already built. It is why ship-killing is rewarded without ever being required.

The star thresholds — migrated from the GDD

The per-lever thresholds below were the GDD's; they live here now (they were the deferred "numbers layer" for scoring). Each lever scores 1–3 independently; the battle rating is their average, rounded up.

★★★! demands excellence, and is hidden. It is earned only when ≥2 levers hit ★★★! and the third is at least ★★★ — plain averaging can never reach it. It appears in no UI as a goal; when earned it flies in as a surprise. Server-tuned thresholds target the frequency The Why sets (≈ once every 15–20 battles for a skilled player).

Adaptive thresholds. Thresholds are not permanently fixed — server-side analytics track each lever's rate independently and nudge gradually: a lever whose 3-star rate runs too high (>40% of players) tightens; a too-rare 1-star loosens. Offline play uses the last-fetched values. Last Stand time does not feed stars — spend it enclosing more territory (the Area lever), not as a passive bonus.

The 0★ pacifist easter egg

Zero stars is reachable: kill no ships and lose every cannon, yet still meet the minimum enclosure (castles enclosed = win) → 0★. The first 0★ in each campaign pays a Stonwryt bonus of 10 × campaign level (Cam 1 = 10, trivial; Cam 32 = 320, brutally hard to survive with no kills and no surviving gun). Undocumented — discovered organically; pays once per campaign.

Worked examples — Ships ★★★(3) + Cannons ★(1) + Enclosure ★(1) = 1.67 → ★★; Ships ★(1) + Cannons ★★★(3) + Enclosure ★★★(3) = 2.33 → ★★★. Any two levers at ★★★ guarantee ★★★ overall. Beneath the stars, Battle Score = ships% + area% + cannon% (max 400): 80% + 45% + 110% = 235; two identical ★★★ can post 287 vs 312.

3The Ship Pool — Capped, Ordered, Deterministic

A battle does not throw its whole fleet at the player at once. It has a fixed pool of ships with a set emergence order and set spawn-rate. What varies is how much of that pool the player ever meets — and that is driven entirely by how the sorties end.

Bounded, so it is not a treadmill

The pool is capped. "More ships might still be coming" is never runaway escalation — it is the fleet committing more of a finite, pre-set reserve. This is the resolution to the anti-treadmill rule (The Why §4): the thing that makes a returning wave dangerous is that it learned (persisted Contact + a cognition tick), not that it is endless. Order and rate being fixed keeps the whole thing deterministic — identical play meets an identical battle.

Sortie count is emergent — and that is a feature

Because the pool is drained by performance, the number of sorties is not a fixed number. A player who repels efficiently drains the pool fast and reaches the Last Stand sooner; a player who merely holds faces more of it. When the pool empties, the threat is spent — the remaining Fight time rolls over to the Last Stand, and the player encloses freely. Emergent battle length is not a fairness problem: two players who play identically get the identical battle; a better player simply earns a shorter one.

4Sortie Resolution — Three End-States

A sortie is one committed wave. It attacks, spends its ammo, and pulls back to reload — that withdrawal is the natural end of the sortie. Its outcome is one of three, gated by the wave's visible resolve (the per-ship network — Ships §4) and its ammo. This doc owns the three end-states as mechanics; the Fight doc owns reading which one is coming before it is stamped (fire slackening → held; signatures cascading aft → repel tipping; a Committed hull driving a seam → breakthrough imminent). One coupling clarified (Jack): the checkpoint is always the battle — the whole battle is the replayable, scored unit. A battle runs as waves of sorties and a sortie as its phases, but those are pacing / document subdivisions, not save points, so an individual sortie's outcome is a step within a battle run rather than a separately-replayable result. Replaying (win or lose) re-runs the battle deterministically; the end-states below are the resolution of each sortie within a run, and Battle Score (§2) totals the run.

All survivors retreat to spawn — and re-emerge first, in order, smarter

Whatever the outcome, the surviving ships fall back to their spawn points and reload during the pause. Next sortie they are the first out, in the same order they came before — minus any that were sunk (dead stays dead), each carrying the damage it took last time. This is the player's hard-won intelligence made mechanical: you have seen this wave. You know what came from where and what it wanted. The cognition tick means it may execute better than before — but you know its motivation, not its script, which is exactly the knowledge a thinking game runs on: the lineage's goal is stable and knowable, its skill is what rises.

Retreat is not safe passage

A withdrawal happens inside the Fight — so retreating ships and troops take incoming fire as they flee. This folds "annihilation" into the model with no extra concept: annihilation is simply a repel where you sank the whole wave on its way out, and a total sink drains the pool toward the Last Stand.

Honest watch-out — same ships, same order can calcify

"You see the same wave again" is a strength only while the wave is still worth re-reading. If the cognition creep is too faint and the new-ship injections too light, a strong player turns re-engagement into a memorised re-run — solved, not strategic. The creep and the injections must carry real weight here; this is the determinism-vs-renewable-strategy tension (The Why §6) living inside one mechanic.

5Ammo — Why the Fleet Pulls Back

The engine under the sortie rhythm. Ships can only shoot what they carry. A wave spends its magazines and must fall back to reload — that, not an arbitrary clock, is why a sortie ends and why even a winning wave withdraws.

The asymmetry — attacker on a clock, defender not

Ships are mobile, so their ammo is finite. Cannons are static, so they sit on effectively unlimited stores — a fortress hoards powder a fleet can't carry. This is literally true of ships versus forts, and it hands the defender a structural edge: every enemy shot is a spent resource; yours is not. Ammo becomes the sortie's diegetic clock — the wave attacks until its punch is spent, then reloads during the Build/Deploy pause. The Fight timer does not disappear, though: it stays the phase's time budget, and the time you don't spend rolls forward (see below). Ammo decides when the wave withdraws; the timer measures how much time you banked doing it.

Time is the commodity — ending early pays

The GDD's time economy runs underneath all of this and is kept whole: unused Build time rolls to Deploy, unused Deploy to Fight, 50% of unused Fight time rolls to the next sortie's Build, and on the final sortie 100% of remaining Fight time rolls to the Last Stand. Ammo and rollover interlock cleanly: a repel (or a full annihilation) ends the Fight before the wave's clock runs out, so more time rolls forward — you are paid twice for routing them, once in the cut-short wave and again in the banked time. A held sortie spends the whole magazine and burns most of the timer, so little rolls over. The Last Stand is the culmination of everything banked — every sortie you ended early feeds the final chance to enclose. Time saved is time to win with.

Relative, per-lineage — a fiction on the Firepower axis

Amounts are relative, never exact counts, and they read straight off lineage × axis: heavy shooters (Bombard, Leviathan) carry big, slow-firing loads; raiders (Corsair) carry little and run dry fast — hit-and-run made literal; and the delivery lineages barely shoot at all — a Runner or a Breacher culminates by dropping its payload, not by emptying a magazine, so ammo is nearly moot for them. Firepower-axis ships carry more; evasion-axis ships less.

Keep it felt, not managed

Ammo must stay a felt property — the player sees the fire slacken and the wave pull back to reload — never a numeric magazine bar on every hull. A managed ammo stat would be exactly the cognitive load that erodes the thinking game (The Why §4). The player reasons about "they're nearly spent," not about counters.

The strategy it unlocks

Two new decisions fall out, both serving strategy-as-fun. Bait them into wasting ammo — a feint that draws fire on a decoy now costs them rounds, not just position; deception gains an attrition payoff. And outlast the magazine — weather their fire until they are dry — becomes a genuine option. It stays valid-but-costly, never dominant: pure turtling invites breakthrough and kills nothing, tanking your Battle Score, so the breakthrough threats and the star gradient hold it in check.

6Breakthrough — Hurt, Not Devastate

Breakthrough is the sharp end of the sortie loop, and it must cost enough to sting without ever dropping the player into a hopeless spiral. The design goal: one breakthrough hurts and is recoverable; repeated breakthroughs on the same front are what lose the battle.

The event, defined objectively

A breakthrough is not a fuzzy damage threshold — it is a single legible event: the wave breaks a required castle's enclosure. That is the exact mirror of the win condition, so it is unambiguous and it threatens the one thing that matters. When a required ring falls, the sortie's outcome is stamped breakthrough.

The locked-in cost — three layers that scale
  • The ring is broken → that castle drops out of enclosure and must be re-enclosed next Build. The immediate pressure.
  • Cannons in the breach zone are lost → the emotional cost: the veterans you nurtured, gone. This is where breakthrough hurts.
  • A degradation tick on the newly-exposed castle → the compounding cost, nudging it toward 3/3 destruction.

So one breakthrough is a real wound — rebuild the ring, mourn the guns, watch the degradation — but survivable. Defeat only arrives when breakthroughs compound: a castle taken to 3/3 that drops you below the required count. Never fatal on its own; fatal only as the last straw.

The anti-death-spiral guarantee

Why the breaching wave withdraws instead of pressing its advantage: a wave that punches through has culminated — it concentrated force and spent ammo to breach, and real doctrine is breach → consolidate → exploit with a fresh wave, never overextend a spent one into a defender about to counterattack and rebuild. So it falls back to reload and lets the next, heavier, wiser wave exploit — which, not by accident, hands the player the rebuild window. Ships do not vanish; they retreat having succeeded, the same motion as any withdrawal. Combined with the mid-Build impossibility check (if you genuinely cannot re-enclose, the battle ends at once in a clean loss), this delivers the guarantee: the player always has either a real path to recover or a clean, understood defeat — never a drawn-out lost cause. That resolves the two-minds worry: reset the board so recovery is always possible; let the locked-in damage carry the stakes.

7Cannon Carry — Three Veterans Across Campaigns

Within a campaign, then a chosen few beyond it

Inside a campaign, cannon veterancy carries battle-to-battle as the GDD has it — guns grow toward 200% across a campaign's fights, and losing one to a breach is a real loss. At a campaign boundary, only the three best cannons carry forward — the player chooses them, with our recommendation — as a long emotional through-line: three veterans that survive the whole War. Every other cannon resets fresh at the new campaign.

Why capped carry — a principle check

Carrying only three (capped at 200%) is deliberate, not stingy. A full veteran fleet carried across campaigns would make later battles easier and let the player win by out-gunning instead of out-thinking — the exact stat-snowball The Why forbids, wearing a friendlier coat. Three keeps it an emotional attachment, never a power ramp. It also protects the fairness goal: less carried strength keeps campaign starts comparable on the leaderboard. Two conditions make it honest — difficulty is tuned to assume you hold a small veteran core (so it never trivialises), and the clean-slate-per-campaign reset is preserved for everything else.

8Why It Works — the North-Star Check

An honest read of the whole model against the one test: does it keep strategy the fun?

What it gets right
  • The win stays a crisp goal (enclose the required castles) while Battle Score turns how well into an honest, skill-ranked number — the fairness engine, already built.
  • The capped, ordered pool makes escalation bounded and deterministic — the anti-treadmill rule satisfied, and emergent battle length rewarding the strong player.
  • Ammo gives the sortie a diegetic clock (with the GDD time economy intact beneath it — ending early banks more time toward the Last Stand), explains the pullback with real logic, and opens two genuine strategies (bait-to-waste, outlast-the-magazine) — depth from an intuitive asymmetry, not a new managed system.
  • Same-ships-same-order makes the information war symmetric and teaches motivation-not-script — stable roles, rising skill: the ideal knowledge structure for a game that must never fully solve.
  • Breakthrough hurts without devastating, and the withdraw-to-reload reset guarantees recover-or-lose-cleanly — no death spirals, the player always understands the state.
The two things to keep honest

First, the re-run risk: "same wave again" stays strategic only if the cognition creep and new-ship injections carry real weight — otherwise re-engagement calcifies into a memorised sequence. Second, the ammo-turtle: outlast-the-magazine must stay valid-but-costly, held in check by breakthrough threats and the star gradient, or it drifts toward a dominant auto-win. Both are live instances of the foundational tensions — watch them as the numbers get tuned.
