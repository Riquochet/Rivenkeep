1How to Use This Doc

Every other doc says how Rivenkeep works — the GDD and the module docs (Fleet Memory, Cannon & Tower, Ships, Campaign) hold mechanics, numbers, structure. This doc says why. It is the reasoning the "how" has to serve, and it is the standard every future decision is measured against.

The rule

When a design choice comes up — a new mechanic, a tuning value, a cut, a feature — it is not judged by "is it cool" or "is it hard." It is judged by the principles below, and above all by the one test in §2. If a choice can't be defended here, it doesn't belong in the "how," however clever it is. When this doc and a "how" doc disagree about intent, this doc wins; the "how" gets fixed.

The standing commitment

This build proceeds on brutal honesty at every step (§8). The foundational tensions in §6 are not solved — they are permanent load-bearing risks that every step must be checked against. This doc is updated as the thinking sharpens; it is never allowed to drift into comfortable self-congratulation.

2The North Star — Strategy Is the Fun

Rivenkeep is a thinking game. The fun is strategy: reading a situation, choosing among real options, and outwitting an opponent. It is not a reflex game, not a spectacle game, not a collection game. Everything else is in service of that or it is cut.

The one test — apply it to every decision

Does this keep the player making an interesting decision against a mind — rather than executing a memorised optimisation against a number? If it deepens the decision, it belongs. If it lets the player stop thinking (a dominant build, a stat check, a rote line), it is a failure however fun it looks in isolation. "Interesting decision" has a precise meaning here: several options, none strictly dominant, each with legible consequences the player can reason about before committing.

Thinking, not arcade — the concrete line

A thinking game and an arcade game can look similar for a second and are opposites underneath. The dividing line: success comes from what you decided, never from how fast you can act. Reaction speed, execution precision under time pressure, and twitch are arcade currencies and are deliberately kept out of the win condition. The Build puzzle is low-pressure timed thinking — a soft, generous clock that creates urgency without ever becoming a reflex test (a skilled player finishes with time to bank); the Fight is "watch and nudge," not plate-spinning (this is why the AoR re-task was made a slow, costed crawl rather than an instant snap). The Build timer is deliberately kept in that band: tight enough that a long ring and awkward pieces bite, never so tight that speed wins — if a board is only beatable by placing fast, the timer is mistuned for that board (see Build §2). If a battle is ever won by clicking faster, the identity has broken.

3Skill, Not Luck — the Fairness That Makes It Epic

Because the game is deterministic, two players facing the same battle face exactly the same problem. There is no RNG to bless one and curse another. The only variable is how well each player played — and that is what makes Rivenkeep both fair and, potentially, big.

Why determinism is a "why," not just a "how"

Determinism is usually treated as a technical choice. Here it is a values choice. Same spawns, same terrain, same destinations, for everyone, every time. That means: a loss is always the player's to own ("I didn't solve it," never "the dice hated me"), which converts frustration into motivation to retry. And it means leaderboards measure skill, not fortune — a ranking is an honest comparison of decision quality, so competition is real and a strategy guide is even possible. A game where the difference between players is how they think, not who got lucky, is a game people can respect, master, and argue about. That is the ground an epic, widely-played game stands on.

The mechanism: one fixed seed per battle

Determinism resolves to a single concrete rule. Every battle carries one fixed seed, and that seed drives all of the battle's randomness — which pieces the dual draw offers, the order and timing ships emerge, what makes a hull waver and turn back, every stochastic beat. The one seed fans out into separate, independent streams — one for the piece draw, one for the fleet, and a general stream for the rest — so a run of bad pieces can never perturb how the ships behave, yet every stream is reproducible from that seed. Restart the battle and it re-seeds with that same number: played the same way, it unfolds identically, down to the last piece. Nothing is ever rerolled — what you draw still answers to your own choices (trashing a piece pulls the next from the fixed stream), so the draw is a deterministic lever you work, never a slot you pray to. A retry only lets you solve it better, never fish for a luckier draw. That is what disarms the retry-as-brute-force tension (§6) at the root: the problem never changes, so the only thing a replay can improve is your understanding of it. Across the campaign the 190 seeds are chosen far apart, so every battle is a genuinely different problem with its own unrelated draw — no two neighbours share a family resemblance in their randomness. One seed per battle is therefore the single backbone under both halves of this doc: the fairness of §3 (the same problem for everyone, every time) and the renewable variety of §5 (a fresh, distinct mind on every board).

The obligation determinism creates

Fairness is only delivered if the loss is legible — the player must be able to see why they lost and what they could have done. Determinism without legibility is just an unfair puzzle with a hidden answer. So the fairness promise of §3 is only kept if the legibility principle of §4 is kept. They stand or fall together.

4The Guiding Principles

The load-bearing design laws. Each exists to protect the north star; each has teeth (it rules choices in and out).

Difficulty is cognition, not stats

Enemies get harder by thinking better — sharper estimators, not bigger health bars or faster guns. Inflating numbers converts strategy into optimisation (find the damage build, repeat); escalating intelligence keeps the decision space alive forever. Any hull/gun growth is a minor component inside the one cognition-led improvement curve, never a parallel stat-treadmill. This is the single most important principle and the reason the game can stay strategic to Battle 190.

Legibility is the beam that holds the roof

Depth is only strategic if the player can reason about it and predict outcomes. Every enemy has a tell; the fear grid is visible; outcomes are deterministic; and large spaces are made legible by generators (read any ship as lineage×axis, any cannon as concept×domain — never memorise a list). An interaction the player can't reason about is not a hard decision, it is a coin flip — and a coin flip is not strategy. When depth and legibility conflict, legibility wins.

Determinism with a wide solution space

A deterministic board with one answer is a lock with one key — solved once, then dead. Determinism is only compatible with strategy if every board admits many viable solutions, no single dominant line. Board design must actively protect solution-space width; a board that funnels to one optimal sequence has failed the north star even if it is "hard."

No forced playstyle

The fleet gets harder to exploit, never harder to beat only one way. Every threat has more than one answer; the clever techniques (feint, bait, deception) are the elegant path, never the mandatory one. A player who refuses to feint can still win every board. Any enemy beatable by exactly one trick is a bug — it has replaced a decision with a requirement.

Variety of minds is the strategic engine

Because a solved board stops being strategic, renewable strategy comes from the breadth of different minds the campaign marches past the player — lineage × axis × cognition-creep × geometry. Every board is a fresh mind to read. This makes variety load-bearing, not decorative: the campaign layout is where "strategy is the fun" is actually won or lost.

The felt target — real, and always just barely overwhelming new ·

Ruling (Jack): “The game has to feel real and always just barely overwhelming.” This is the law that says what all the other numbers are for, and it has teeth in a specific way: the design sits at the top of every legible band, never in the middle of it.

It was not a new preference when it was named. It was the through-line already running under a set of rulings made separately, which is the strongest evidence a law is real:

  • Contacts — eight is the destination, not the danger line. The late campaign targets the measured tracking ceiling rather than a comfortable distance below it.
  • Decision-bearing load — the cap of four is a limit the late game should sit against, not a budget to underspend.
  • Supply — the fort accounts for roughly 55% of arrivals under perfect allocation, so 45% leaks through even when the player plays flawlessly. The fort is permanently and deliberately insufficient. That is now an intention, not a consequence.
  • The wall — difficulty rises by the requirement squeezing the player’s freedom, never by the enemy growing.
  • Timers — low-pressure, but timed. Never absent.

The test. At the widest wave the campaign fields, a player should be able to say what every approach is doing — and have no attention left over while doing it. Slack means the band is underspent. That is the sharpened form of the legibility test in §6, which asked only the first half of the question.

“Barely” is doing the load-bearing work, and it is the red line. Overwhelming is the target; unfair is a failure, and the two are separated by protections that are not negotiable: no board may punish a player for not watching a contact that gave no reason to be watched; every loss must be attributable; no board may be winnable only by placing fast. Overwhelmed-and-reading is the goal. Overwhelmed-and-guessing is a bug.

Read against the tension in §6: legibility under load is the design’s single point of failure, and this law deliberately runs the load to the edge of it. That is not a contradiction — it is the reason §6 is a permanent risk rather than a solved one. The design chooses to live at the boundary, so the boundary has to be measured rather than guessed, and every number in the fort/fleet chain exists to locate it.

Real numbers are inputs, not authorities — the translation step new ·

Ruling (Jack): “We have to couch it in terms of the game. In the real world, humans die. In the game, bits die.”

The design derives its numbers from real evidence wherever evidence exists — military history, operations research, perceptual psychology, shipped games. That is right, and it is not sufficient. A real figure carries a reason as well as a value, and the reason usually does not survive the trip. Deriving from the world is step one of two; the second step is a translation check, and it has three questions:

  • What was actually scarce in the real case? The worked example: a sniper averages 1.5 rounds per kill not because of marksmanship but because a second shot reveals his position and gets him killed. The scarce thing is the sniper’s life. Import the number without the scarcity and you have taken the arithmetic and thrown away the reason it held.
  • What is scarce here instead? Cannons are static, ammunition is effectively unlimited, and a hull that dies returns as queue depth. Our scarcities are attention, coverage, slots, reload cycles, and banked time — so the real figure has to be re-expressed in those, and it often lands on a different mechanic than the one it came from. Sniper doctrine belongs on the Marksman’s reload, not its accuracy.
  • Which way does the asymmetry push? Consistently one way. Because bits die cheaply where humans do not, a historically-calibrated pressure feels slacker in-game than it did in life. So restoring the felt weight means erring toward the harsher end of the evidence: where a real range exists and the design must choose, choose against the player.

Note the consequence for method: a midpoint is the correct estimator for a quantity and the wrong one for a pressure. Any number that governs how hard the game feels should be read off the demanding end of its range, with the range recorded so the choice is visible rather than buried.

This law is what keeps the previous one honest. “Just barely overwhelming” is a claim about feeling, and feeling is exactly where a designer’s intuition is least reliable and most confident. Real-world anchors are the discipline that stops the felt target becoming a mood — and the translation step is the discipline that stops the anchors becoming a costume.

Supporting laws
  • Enclosure is a tradeoff, never a dominant turtle. Enclosed guns are safe (ghosts to the fleet) but spatially costly and coverage-limited; breakthrough threats punish the pure turtle. Protect the tension — never let hiding be strictly best or strictly punished.
  • The Fight rewards reading, not reaction. Anticipation and belief-manipulation win the Fight; speed does not. Keep live actions rare and costed.
  • Introduce → Isolate → Combine. Never more than one new thing at a time — for enemies, terrain, and the toolkit. Challenge comes from combining the understood, never from confusion.
  • Every number is derived, never arbitrarily set. A number’s provenance is part of its definition. Acceptable sources, best first: (1) inverted from something already locked in the design; (2) a measured real-world figure; (3) the midpoint of a measured range, with the range recorded; (4) derived to a stated design requirement. “Set” is not on the list. Where a number cannot yet get past (4) it ships with its derivation route named, so a future reader knows what would replace it. The teeth: a number flagged as soft is not a number to note, it is a number to re-derive — and re-deriving has twice found an error in supposedly-settled work.
  • Dream big, then edit down. Generate wide first (no scope-scolding in ideation); cut later, deliberately, against these principles — and preserve the superset when we cut, so nothing good is lost early.
  • Skill is taught, not assumed — the mini-games. The edifice assumes a player who can read the fleet, but that reading is learned — so the game must build it. The mini-games are that on-ramp: short, downside-free, endlessly replayable drills that ramp from one piece of a phase → a whole phase → two phases → a full run. They are load-bearing for the north star (can\'t read the mind → can\'t enjoy the strategy), they answer tensions #2 and #3 directly (they build the reasoning that stops retry becoming brute-force, and train the legibility-reading the system leans on as it combines), and they are the retention hook — the game\'s "candy-crush." Full design pending (after the Build & Fight phases — they are built from the phases); requirements captured in the journal.

5What "Thinking" Actually Means Here

Be precise about the kind of thinking this game rewards, because it shapes everything. The fleet is deterministic — a fixed policy over its seed and observation-history. "Cognition" is a better fixed policy. The player is not dueling a live, adapting opponent; they are learning and exploiting a legible fixed mind.

This is a good choice — and its consequences must be owned

A fixed, legible mind is exactly what makes the game fair, solvable, replayable, and rankable (§3). But it means the thinking is the player's act of reading a system, not an ongoing arms race — and therefore within a single board, once the mind is read, the board is solved. The "signaling game" — baiting, feinting, denying the fleet contacts — is real depth, but it is puzzle depth (find the exploit in a fixed policy), not adversarial depth (outwit a reacting rival). Design honestly for what it is.

Where the renewable strategy actually lives

Follows directly: since a solved board is no longer strategy, the game stays strategic only through the stream of genuinely different minds across the campaign. The renewability is between boards, not within one. So the strategic lifeblood of the game is variety-of-minds (§4), delivered by the campaign layout. If boards repeat their strategic shape — same read, same answer, new paint — the game solves itself no matter how elegant the engine. The campaign doc is not "content planning"; it is where the core promise is kept or broken.

The subtle-mind constraint this forces

The cognition creep must be felt but observable: subtle in magnitude (the player rarely catches the exact moment it sharpened) yet legible in kind (the sharper behaviour shows, so "why isn't my old plan working?" always has a discoverable answer — "they stopped falling for the bait; they verify now"). Felt-but-invisible reads as cheating and poisons the fairness promise; felt-but-observable turns frustration into the curiosity that is the fun.

6The Foundational Tensions — Permanent Load-Bearing Risks

These are not solved and will not be "finished." They are the pressure points where the thinking game quietly decays into an arcade grind or a dead puzzle. Every step is checked against them. Honest status, not reassurance.

1 · Determinism vs renewable strategy — the deepest tension

Determinism gives the fair, solvable puzzle; strategy needs an open decision space. They coexist only if every board has many viable solutions (§4) and if the campaign supplies fresh minds (§5). A board that solves to one line, or a campaign that repeats strategic shapes, breaks the north star while still feeling "hard." Number-one thing to get right.

2 · Retry can bypass reasoning

Determinism + retry has a dark side: brute-forcing a board by memorising spawn timings instead of reasoning about the fleet — pattern-matching dressed as strategy. The line must fall on understanding. The cognition creep helps (memorised lines age out), but it argues against boards whose only solution is one precise memorised sequence.

3 · Legibility under load — the single point of failure

The system is deeply dimensional (cannons concept×domain, enemies lineage×axis, three memory channels, five layers, seven towers, triage, cognition creep, the Fight verbs). Depth is strategic only while legible; past the ceiling it degenerates into guessing or guide-following and the whole edifice fails at once. The generators and the legibility law hold the line. Guard legibility above every other consideration as systems combine — this is the one thing that, if lost, loses everything.

The ceiling now has a number — and sizing is run against it new ·

Tension #3 was, until now, a warning without a bound. The fleet-sizing pass gave it one, by treating the enemy count as a legibility question rather than a difficulty question: the right fleet size is the smallest one that makes coverage a genuine allocation problem, never the largest the fleet's mind could support. The bound, owned by Victory §3:

  • 8 simultaneous contacts as the late-campaign destination — where a contact is one coherent object of attention, so a formation holding shape counts once whatever its hull count. This is a single global budget, not an allowance per bearing.
  • At most 4 contacts decision-bearing at once — the law that makes 8 safe. Total contacts may reach eight; the number simultaneously asking something of the player must stay at four or below, and the surplus must be legitimately ignorable for a spell. Above the tracking edge a player stops tracking and starts sampling, which is what a commander does — and it works only if the board never punishes a reasonable sampling pattern.
  • ≤ 3 simultaneously live approaches — a fourth is not hard, it is unattendable: you cannot pan a board several times the screen's size to three places at once.
  • 16–24 hulls is fine, and is a different number. Resolve, fear and cascades are population mechanics and need bodies; attention needs objects. Keeping the two apart is what lets the game be simple to read and rich underneath, and conflating them is a mistake this doc has already made once.
  • One budget shared between count and sophistication — a wave arrives wide and simple, or narrow and clever, never both.
The reframe that matters: this ceiling is trained, and the training is a designed part of the game

The earlier reading of this tension was defensive — keep the load low so legibility survives. That was wrong in spirit. The ceiling is a function of player skill, not a constant, and the campaign is 190 battles long precisely so it can raise that skill.

Two mechanisms do the raising. Determinism converts tracking into prediction: a wave already met is not four objects being followed, it is a remembered shape being checked. And the mini-games are the explicit instrument — a ladder that takes a player from reading one approach to holding a whole board. That is the strongest available argument that they are a pillar rather than an onboarding nicety: a player who cannot hold eight slow, familiar, differentiated contacts cannot play the late War, so the ladder that gets them there is load-bearing against this very tension.

And the coupling that makes the load worth carrying: the player already cannot cover every approach with guns, and now cannot watch every approach with attention. Those are the same problem at two scales, solved by the same act — reading the ground and deciding where the loss goes. So raising the contact count deepens the game rather than merely loading it, which is the difference between depth and noise and is the whole subject of this tension.

Red line, unchanged in force and sharpened in form: if a board punishes a player for not watching a contact that gave no reason to be watched, the board failed, not the player. Sibling to the ≥2-solutions law and the threat-spacing law, and owned the same way — by board and wave authoring.

These are not intuitions. They come from the measured limits of attentive tracking — roughly three to five items ordinarily, rising toward eight when the objects move slowly, and about four groups regardless of how many items compose each group, with performance degrading as the items in a group spread out. Rivenkeep's contacts are slow, visually differentiated and pre-learned, so they sit at the top of that range; the Fight's other loads (two fogs, health, ammo, live verbs) argue for holding below it. Hence 6 as the target rather than 8. The full provenance is in Victory §3.

And the corollary that matters most here: that ceiling is a function of player skill, not a constant. A deterministic, pre-learned wave converts tracking into prediction, which raises the real limit above the laboratory four — and the instrument for raising it is the mini-games. That makes them load-bearing against this tension rather than an onboarding nicety: they are how a player earns the capacity the late campaign spends. The test to hold: at the widest wave the campaign fields, a player should be able to say what each approach is doing. If they cannot, the ceiling was set too high — and the fix is a smaller fleet, never a better HUD.

Tension #4 is now answered structurally, not by balance new ·

The win condition has been corrected: victory is beating back the attack while maintaining the required enclosure count, not closing a ring (Victory §2). That does more for this tension than any amount of tuning could.

Under the old reading, "turtle everything" was held in check only by the difficulty of the Build puzzle and by breakthrough threats — that is, by balance, which is always one bad number away from failing. Under the correct reading it is held in check by the shape of the win itself: enclosing cannot win, because the attack still has to be beaten back, and beating it back means at minimum destroying the fleet's purpose-bearing hulls. A player who never fights cannot win by building.

The turtle line does not vanish, and should not — the no-forced-playstyle law forbids closing it off. A player can still outlast the fleet until its resolve drains from lack of progress. But that path is now structurally worse rather than merely tuned worse: it costs many sorties of castle degradation, and the scoring credits unemerged reserves only in proportion to the cores destroyed, so a fleet that goes home with its carriers intact hands the player nothing. Valid, honestly available, reliably inferior — exactly the shape this doc asks of every non-preferred playstyle.

Worth recording the general lesson, because it applies beyond this tension: the strongest fix for a dominance risk is a win condition that does not admit it, not a cost that discourages it. This one was hiding in plain sight for several passes — the Last Stand only makes sense if the battle continues past enclosure, and nobody asked what it was last of.

4 · Enclosure dominance (the turtle) & 5 · the Fight sliding to arcade

If enclosure were pure upside, "turtle everything" would solve the game — it is saved only by the hard Build puzzle and breakthrough threats, so keep it a genuine tradeoff. And the real-time Fight is the one arcade risk — the costed AoR-crawl and Deploy-first design keep it deliberate, but the line to hold forever is: the Fight rewards reading, never reflexes.

The elegance verdict

The core — a blind, deterministic mind whose difficulty is its intelligence; enclosure discipline; castle triage; no forced playstyle; cognition over stats — is genuinely elegant and rare, a real answer to the treadmill problem, and it points straight at strategy-as-fun. The surface is ambitious and sits near the ceiling of manageable dimensionality; the difficulty-layer set has mild internal overlap (terrain/weather both environmental; pieces/territory both Build-difficulty; territory and triage both enclosure pressure) — tolerable, tightenable later, not worth breaking the clean combinatorics for now. The whole is elegant if and only if legibility survives the fold-together. Build to keep the player reasoning about a fresh mind each board, and strategy will be the fun.

7Applied Psychology — Why the Mechanics Work on the Player

The north star says strategy is the fun; this is the applied layer beneath it — the engagement principles that keep a player in the chair long enough to learn to read a mind, and coming back after a loss rather than quitting. It is the psychological "why" under the mechanics: not what they do, but why they hold a person. (Folded in from the GDD's original design. Every principle is still measured against §2 — it earns its place only by deepening the decision, or by protecting the player who is still learning to make it, never by manufacturing compulsion for its own sake.)

Flow state (Csikszentmihalyi)

Keep the player in the channel between boredom (too easy) and anxiety (too hard). The 16 piece tiers, the difficulty layers, and their combinatorial progression give a smooth curve — always slightly challenged, never overwhelmed, because each component was mastered alone before it was combined (the Introduce → Isolate → Combine law, felt from the inside). Time-pressure is calibrated to "just enough": Build timers create urgency but a skilled player finishes with time to bank. And every action has instant feedback — pieces snap, ghost previews show the landing, HP bars update live — so the player never wonders "did that work?"

Loss aversion (Kahneman & Tversky)

A loss is felt roughly twice as hard as an equivalent gain, and the design uses that deliberately — and bounds it. The one-battle drop-back stings enough to motivate a retry; the campaign floor stops the sting cascading into rage-quit (the Dark Souls bonfire principle). Cannon investment makes protection personal — a veteran at 140% is real accrued value, so losing it hurts and the player chooses to guard it. Castle degradation (the 3-strike counter) makes loss approach visibly, ticking 1/3 → 2/3, converting dread into urgent action rather than despair.

Variable-ratio reinforcement (Skinner) — kept honest

Rewards at unpredictable intervals are the most compelling schedule; here the vector is the dual piece draw — two pieces from the tier pool, sometimes exactly what you need, sometimes not — with trash, storage, and spackle as pressure valves that let the player manage bad luck without erasing it. The hidden ★★★! rides the same principle onto skill: it can land on any battle and cannot be predicted, so earning it is a genuine spike. The guardrail that keeps it honest: the randomness is over pieces, never over the outcome — the board stays deterministic (§3), so this is a slot-machine feel bolted to a fair, skill-decided game, not a real gamble. Target frequency: a skilled player earns ★★★! about once every 15–20 battles (server-tuned) — rare enough to feel earned, common enough that everyone tastes it, ideally once by Campaign 15.

Near-miss psychology

A near-miss motivates more than a clean failure. The design seeds them everywhere: a pentomino that almost fits ("if I'd rotated it…"), one block short of enclosure at the buzzer ("so close…"), a ship surviving on a sliver into the next sortie ("one more tick of fire…"). Each is a learning loop that fuels "just one more try" — and points at what to do differently, so the pull is toward understanding, not brute repetition (the retry-vs-reasoning tension, §6).

The Zeigarnik effect

Incomplete tasks create tension that pulls for completion. The earned time bank is visible and grows with good play — the player doesn't want to "waste" it by losing. A ★★☆ on the home screen is an open loop begging for the third star; "4/6 battles" in the campaign bar calls the player back. Every unfinished element is a small hook.

Endowment & self-determination

People value what they invested in. Walls placed across several sorties feel mine; a 130% cannon is a companion, not a number — which is what makes the Build phase feel like it matters rather than chores between fights. Underneath sit Self-Determination Theory's three needs, each deliberately fed: autonomy (a real choice every sortie), competence (visible skill growth through stars and mastery), and relatedness (identical-for-all boards create a shared, comparable experience — the same ground §3's fairness stands on).

The four nested "just one more" loops
  • Micro (10–30s): place a piece → see the result → choose the next. Tiny decisions, instant feedback.
  • Phase (30–90s): Build → Deploy → Fight — three distinct activities that keep any one kind of thinking from going stale (the three-phase cognitive rotation).
  • Battle (2–5 min): multiple sorties, time banking, the pool draining — "one more sortie" becomes "let me finish this battle."
  • Campaign (20–60 min, across sessions): star collection and unlock milestones — a pull that spans days ("three stars from the next unlock").

8The Commitment

Brutal honesty at every step

As the game is built, each step gets an honest read against this doc — what it strengthens, what it risks, where it strains a principle or a tension. No step is waved through because it is exciting or because momentum is nice. Praise and risk are reported together, calibrated. When a step violates a principle, that is said plainly, and the step is changed or the principle is consciously and explicitly revised here — never quietly ignored.
