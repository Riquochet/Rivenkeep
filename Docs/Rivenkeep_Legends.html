  • The pairs. Where one event is told from both sides, the Rivenmen’s stone leaf is followed at once by the wood’s leaf that faces it. The one pair that cannot sit side by side is I.1 and the Epilogue, because the first tale is answered only at the very end. The two are cross-referenced.

  • The canon held throughout. The enemy people are the Mystaeri. The Shorelanders are reborn as the Rivenmen. Elder Halvard and Elderess Rhyna are Aetherbonded, husband and wife, and every act of theirs is done together. The Captain is never named.
  • Your lines. Every line of yours the plan keeps is kept word for word. Where a line had to bend to fit a ruling of yours (Rhyna never acts alone), the change is the smallest one that works, and the closing section names it.
  • The critic’s pass. This version restores several of your legend’s phrases the draft had paraphrased away, fixes a dozen small contradictions between tales, and tightens the delivery map. OF THIS BOOK

Written by Seren Reader-of-Wood, apprentice to the Bonded of the Keep, at the front of this Book, when the Book was already half full.

This is the Book of the Riven Stone. It holds the tales that are laid at the Long Hearth of Rivenkeep, in the hall behind the inner gate, under the wall where the torn cloak flies. They are laid in the hours after an assault, when the guns are cooling and the mortar has not yet set, and there is nothing left to do with the hands but hold them to the fire.

We did not make the custom. The first builders of the Keep made it, and we forgot it, and found it again at the bottom of a chest in the deepest vault, as you will read. When their stone was set and their tools were cleaned, the first builders sat at this hearth, and each night one of them laid a tale. We do as they did. Our wall is rising again, and so is this Book.

Beside the hearth, in a niche of the hearth-wall, lies the old capstone of the inner gate. It fell in the forgotten years and lay in the gateway until the Bonded turned it over. Halvard’s fathers cut it and Rhyna’s forebears laid it, and the Stonwryt of the first builders is still sharp on its underside. Whoever holds it tells. When the tale is done the teller sets it back in its niche, and we say that the tale is laid; for a tale that is only told is gone by morning, and a tale that is laid is part of the wall.

When a tale of ours is laid, the hearth answers it, all together: We remember. The answer comes from the first words of the Captain’s Title, and it is the only thing at this hearth that is always said the same way.

Not every tale in this Book is ours. Some are the wood’s. The Bonded read them from the wreck-wood that the tide brings in under our wall, and from the dark stone that lies in the chest I carried up the mountain, and from other wood besides. The wood’s tales are not told with the stone. The Bonded hold the wood itself, her palms flat on the grain and his hands over hers, and speak what it gives them. When a wood telling ends, the hearth does not answer, for it does not know how. I have written that silence down each time, as it was.

The Bonded would have the wood’s tales in this Book. “A tale told from one side of a wall,” one of them said, and the other finished, “is half a tale.” So where one thing was seen from both sides, the two tellings are set facing each other, ours on the left leaf and the wood’s on the right. Where the wood’s leaf could not yet be read, I have left it blank, with only the grain showing. Whoever opens this Book may see the shape of what we did not yet understand, and wait with us.

When the Bonded tell, one of them begins and the other ends, and they would not have me mark which. I have used two inks, and marked nothing.

The Captain does not tell. He sits at the end of the bench with his hood up and hears his own deeds told, and says nothing, unless it is to say that it was not like that. On the wall and in the orders he is the Commander, for so the Rite named him. At this hearth he is the Captain still, as he would have it.

When we speak of our fathers we call them Shorelanders, for so they were, and we call ourselves we. The folk behind the grey we call the Mystaeri, the mist-folk. That is our word for them, and not theirs.

Where a carving was broken I have said so, and have not mended it with guesses. Where the tales do not agree, I have kept both.

This Book is ink. The Bonded mean it to be cut into the granite of this hall when there is peace enough to cut it. Until that day it is kept in the chest I carried, with the mute stones.

THE INVOCATION

Spoken at the Long Hearth before the first tale of a night, and set here before the first tale of the Book.

IN MEMORY OF OUR HOME, OUR FAMILIES, OUR GOD, OUR FREEDOMS, OUR PEACE.

Sit, if there is room, and stand if there is not. The guns are cooling, and the mortar will not set before dark.

Here is the hearth, and here is the stone that fell from the inner gate. Whoever holds it tells.

We tell these tales because we forgot once. Our fathers forgot a keep, and it crumbled. Their fathers forgot a wrong, and it came back to us under grey sails. So we tell all of it: the building and the breaking, and what we did as well as what was done to us.

Some of these tales are ours, and they are cut plain, like the stones of the wall. Some are the wood’s, and the wood tells slowly, and will not be hurried. Where the two do not agree, we have kept both.

Hold the stone. Lay the tale. When it is laid, the hearth will answer.

And the hearth said: We remember.

BOOK ONE · THE BOOK OF THE WALL

How we came to stand.

I.1 · The Torn CloakStone leaf

Laid by Sergeant Kael, Kael the Counter, oath-keeper of the True Men, on the first night this hearth asked for a tale. He would not let me change a word of it, and I have not.

Give me the stone. No, I will stand. I have sat enough this winter.

I swear on the lintel of the western stair, where the old wardens cut their names, that I will tell this plain, for I have no other way of telling. I was on the gate that day. I am a counter of things and not a singer, and what I did not count I will not tell you.

The column came in at dusk, and I counted it as it came: old men, and women with bundles, and children asleep on their feet, and soldiers of six broken companies who no longer knew their officers. Two thousand and some hundreds. We had walked a month to reach a castle, and we reached a heap of stones. The outer gate hung from one hinge. The ward behind it was grass to the knee. Where the songs had put a great hall there was a roof fallen in, and snow on the hearth where you sit now.

Some shook. Some wept. Some fell and would not rise.

Then the crying began, and it was not one cry. Some cried that we should send to the grey sails and beg for terms, though no one had ever understood a word the grey sails said. Some cried for the high passes and the snow beyond them. Some cried that we should go down into the cellars of the Keep and bar the doors and wait, as if the sea would forget us. I had heard men break before. I had never heard a people break. It is a sound like ice going out on a river.

Now I will tell you what the Captain was, and first what he was not. He was no general, and the generals were slain. He was no politician, and the politicians were forgotten. He was no nobleman, and the nobles were scattered. He was no Stonewright, and the Stonewrights sat in the grass with their chisels in their laps and despaired. He was the captain of a small company, and we were his company. He had found us one by one, in garrisons and on roads, and bound us by nothing but trust. We were soldiers who kept our oaths, who built what we promised, who fought only when the cause was just. That is all a True Man is. I say it so you will know it is not much. It is not little, either.

He did not want to lead. I swear to that as well. All through the long retreat he waited for a greater man to rise, and none rose, for the greater men were dead.

The True Men looked to their Captain. The Captain looked at the wall.

Then he climbed it. He had taken a coal from the dead hearth as he came through the hall, and I did not know why. He went up the broken face of the inner wall alone, by the stones that stood proud of the mortar, and we stood at the foot and let him, for he had not told us to follow. At the top, where the wall still stood whole, he took off his cloak. It was a plain soldier’s cloak, crimson once and gone to the colour of old brick. He tore it down the back. He took a pike out of the rubble and bound the half of it there. Then he knelt on the capstones with the coal, and wrote, slowly, as a man writes who has not written much.

I was the first up after him, and the first to read it. I read it out, and my voice was not steady, and I will not pretend it was:

IN MEMORY OF OUR HOME, OUR FAMILIES, OUR GOD, OUR FREEDOMS, OUR PEACE.

He set the pike in a crack of the wall and gave it to the wind. That was the last unbroken wall of the Shorelands, and that was the banner above it.

Now hear who answered, and in what order, for I counted. The True Men answered first; I could have counted us on my two hands, and did. Then the soldiers of the six companies, who had walked the long retreat beside us. Then the people of the column, who had carried their children on their backs through the passes: one voice, and a dozen, and then hundreds, until I could not count, and I stopped trying. It is the only time I ever stopped. It was not by command, for he gave none. He had only written down what we had left, and it was enough.

That is why the pennant on every keep of ours is crimson. It is the colour of that cloak.

He is sitting there now, at the end of the bench, with his hood up. He will tell you it was not like that. It was like that.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

Then the True Men sang, as they sing it still:

The gate was broken, the lords were scattered, the grey sails gathered along the shore; one man climbed where the stones lay loosened and tore the cloak his shoulders wore. He wrote five things a man might die for, and none of us has needed more.

And the hearth said: We remember.

The facing leaf shows only the grain of the wood, and one line: Unread. The Bonded cannot yet hear this deep.

I.2 · The Cry of the BondedStone leaf

Laid by Seren Reader-of-Wood, apprentice to the Bonded of the Keep and scribe of this Book, on the night after the first assault the Rivenmen held. She was a child of the guild on the day she tells of, and sat in the courtyard with the rest.

“Hold the stone,” the Bonded told me when I was small and afraid to speak at this hearth. “Hold it, and it will hold you.” So I hold it.

I was in the courtyard that day among the Stonewrights, for I had been called to the guild already, though I had no craft yet but carrying water. We sat in the old ward where it had gone to grass, some hundreds of us, masters and prentices, and the bonded pairs a little apart. We had come out of ten havens. We had watched every one of them die, and we had walked away from each with our chisels in our packs, and not one of us had lifted a hand, for our creed forbade it. Build, do not destroy. Protect, do not attack. No Stonewright ever bore a weapon. Mortar and chisel were our only arms, and they had not been enough.

The faith that had sustained us through twelve generations was on us still, but it sat on us like a wet cloak. I think every elder in that courtyard was turning over the same hard thing and would not say it aloud: that walls built for peace must sometimes be defended with blood. And if that was so, whose blood? And whose hands?

Then the Captain came down from the wall. I had seen the cloak go up, and heard the soldiers shout, and did not know what it had to do with us. He walked into the middle of the courtyard. He was not tall. There was coal-black on his fingers. He looked at the elders, and at the broken gate behind them, and he asked one question. It is the only speech of his that anyone at this hearth can repeat:

“Your walls are falling. Will you build, or will you watch and die?”

No one answered him. I remember the wind in the grass.

Then the Bonded of the Keep stood up. You know them now. I did not know them then, save as two elders who sat apart and spoke to no one, for they had no need. She was Elderess Rhyna, Rhyna of the Inner Gate, whose bonded forebears had laid the capstone of that very gate in the age before the havens. He was Elder Halvard, Halvard Stone-Warden, of the quarry-wardens of the high ridge, whose fathers had cut that same stone. They were husband and wife, as the bonded are, and had been one since the cradle. They looked up at the torn cloak, and they bowed to it in the same breath, for the bonded do not wait on each other. Then their two chisels went up as one and took the last of the light.

The cry came out of them both, and it was one cry. She began it:

“The mortar cries out from the ground. Rise, Stonewrights! Lay stone upon stone. Stand every wall to the very end.”

And he ended it, with his chisel beside hers:

“Rise! Rise! Rise!”

Some tell it that she called and he answered, as though there were two cries and a silence between them. There was no silence. It was one cry in two throats, and hers came first only because a cry must begin somewhere.

Then the whole guild was on its feet, and I with them, though I do not remember standing. We took up the shout, and the soldiers on the wall took it from us, until the walls of Rivenkeep itself seemed to tremble. Old stone does not tremble. I felt it do so under my feet.

The terms were made that night in the ruin of this hall, and the Bonded made them. The Stonewrights would not take up arms. They never had and never would, and the Captain did not ask it. But they would build between the volleys, and mend a breach while the guns still spoke, with the speed and precision that only Aetherbonded pairs could achieve, for a bonded pair works as one body with four hands. One of them said the first half to the Captain and the other the rest, and I could not tell you which. It has been the guild’s word ever since: “Not the sword, but the stone. Not the killing, but the keeping.”

So the siege day was shared out, and this hearth has names for its hours. There is the laying, when the Stonewrights raise and mend. There is the hauling, when the guns are dragged to where the Captain wants them. There is the holding, when the grey sails come in and the wall must stand. Every gun that is hauled is a course of stone not laid, for it is the same hands on the ropes; and the Captain knows it, and moves a gun as a careful man spends silver. And wherever you see a Stonewright on the wall in the holding, look again. You will see two.

We sang that first night by torchlight in the gap of the inner gate as we laid, one voice to the comma and the other the rest, as the Bonded taught us:

Stone upon stone, and the stone keeps faith; hand over hand, and the hand holds true; two at the gate when the grey sails gather, two at the gate, and the gate stands through.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

And the hearth said: We remember.

I.3 · The Captain’s Long BreathStone leaf

Laid by Private Hale, Hale the runner, who carries word along the wall in the holding, in the first year of the siege. It is a saying of the hearth, and he tells where it came from; Kael the Counter mends it at the end, as he mends most things.

Give me the stone, then. I am not a teller. I run.

There is a saying at this hearth that you will hear before you have been a month on the wall. I think it began with me, though Voss says it is older.

A siege day has eighteen hours in it, from the first grey to the last. In the laying they go slowly, for stone is heavy. In the hauling they go slowly, for guns are heavier. But in the holding, when the grey sails come in, the hours go by like breaths, and a man cannot hold one of them still long enough to think in it. So it was in my first holding. The sails came out of the grey faster than I could count them, and the guns spoke, and the smoke came down, and afterward I could not have told you whether an hour had gone or a morning.

Every gun on this wall stands where the Captain set it, and none moves but by his word. So in the holding the word must run, and I am the one who runs it. That day the wall-warden of the east battery sent me with a question, and I ran it along the wall-walk with my head down, and came to the Captain.

He was standing still. That is what I remember first. Everything on that wall was moving but him. He had his hand on the rail of the battery, and he was watching one hull among a dozen, a grey-sailed thing coming in on the tide, and he did not look round at me.

And the hour began to stretch. It did not stop short, as a cart stops against a stone; it slowed, as a cart slows on a long hill, until it hardly moved at all. I have no better way to say it. I stood at his shoulder and the hour went long around him, and I could see the water sheet off that hull’s bow, and I could count the ripples going down its sail, one and two and three, as you count the rings on a cut log. I had time to be afraid. Then I had time to be ashamed of it. Then I had time to stop.

Then he gave the battery its word, and the hour let go of us slowly, as a held breath goes out, until the world was back at its own pace. The guns went. The hull broke up in the one stretch of that water where it could hurt no one, where he had meant it to.

So the men say: For the Captain, an hour lasts as long as a day.

They say it as though it were a gift the Stonewrights laid on him. I said it so myself, the first winter. I know better now. It is not that he is quick. It is that he will not be hurried.

Kael said it better, from across the fire, and I give it to you as he said it: “It is not the world that slows, lad. It is the Captain who will not hurry. The moment he chose was the moment that came.”

This I lay as it was laid for me.

And the hearth said: We remember.

I.4 · The Rite of the CornerstoneStone leaf

Laid by the Bonded of the Keep, Elder Halvard and Elderess Rhyna, in the first spring of the siege, and set in two inks as all their tellings are. They speak of themselves by name and from far off, as the annals speak of the dead; when I asked them why, one said that a tale is not a boast, and the other said that a wall does not know whose hands laid it.

We hold the stone together, as we hold all things. Hear how the Rite of the Cornerstone was found again, and what was done with it.

Beneath this hall there are vaults, and beneath those vaults are others, cut down into the living ridge before ever a haven was raised on the coast; and the deepest of them no Stonewright had entered in the memory of the guild. On the third night after the cry, Rhyna of the Inner Gate rose from her blanket, and Halvard Stone-Warden rose in the same moment and took up the lamp. They went down stair after stair, until the stairs ended at the lip of a shaft where the first builders had let their stone down on ropes. She knew where to look, and he went down, and she paid out the rope, and neither had said a word. What she knew and what he found were one thought, as their thoughts are.

At the foot of the shaft lay a chest of black stone, with the mark of the first builders cut in its lid. In it were the texts of the Rite of the Cornerstone, cut small on thin leaves of slate. It was the most ancient ceremony of the guild, the rite by which the first Stonewrights had consecrated the leaders of Rivenkeep to build Rivenkeep itself.

Halvard and Rhyna read the leaves by lamplight in the vault, and what they read troubled them. For the texts held the secrets of building for battle: walls sloped back to turn a shot, gates set crooked so that no ram could run at them straight, courses laid so that a breach would fall outward into the ditch and not inward on the men behind it. The first builders of the Keep had known war. For twelve generations the guild had taught that walls were raised for peace and weather, and had believed it, and had been proud. The first fathers of the guild had known better, and we had forgotten them.

The new capstone of the inner gate lay in the ward, cut and dressed and not yet raised, and over it the Rite was done, before all who had come up the mountain, in the shadow of the Title where it flew above the gate. The Captain stood where the leaves said the overseer of the work must stand, with his hand on the new stone and his face to the people. He did not like it. He stood there.

There were three oils, as the Rite requires, and Halvard and Rhyna held each flask together and tipped it with both their hands. One named the oil and the other its meaning, for so the Rite is spoken. Cedar, for endurance. Granite-bloom, for strength. Myrrh, for the burden of command.

Then they laid in his hand the staff of the guild: a rod of polished wood capped with the finest polished ashlar, borne by every overseer of a great Stonewright work since the founding. Four hands laid it in two. By it he was named Commander of Rivenkeep.

When the people had gone to their fires, Halvard and Rhyna knelt by the new capstone and cut a Stonwryt into its underside for his work, which was the wall, and the war. The one set the chisel and the other struck. It was the first Stonwryt cut in Rivenkeep since the founding, and the first the guild ever cut for a soldier. The stone was raised into the gate that night.

He did not carry the staff into battle. He did not wear the oils beyond that day. He led as he had always led, from the wall, beside his True Men, with mortar upon his hands and a soldier’s plain cloak upon his back. The staff leans in the corner of the gatehouse where he left it that night. No one has moved it. If you doubt the tale, go and look at the dust on it.

One leaf more lay in the chest, at the very bottom, and Halvard and Rhyna did not find it until the Rite was done. It was in a smaller hand than the rest. It told how the first builders of the Keep ended their day: that when the stone was set and the tools were cleaned, they sat at the hearth, and one of them laid a tale, as a course is laid upon a wall, one course each night, so that the wall and the telling should rise together. So this hearth is older than the havens. We did not make its custom. We forgot it, and found it again at the bottom of a chest.

The people called him Commander. In his own heart he was still only the Captain.

This we lay as it was laid for us.

And the hearth said: We remember.

I.5 · The Naming of the RivenmenStone leaf

Laid by Corporal Voss, Voss who asked, on the eve of the going-down to the havens. He asked his question in the first winter of the siege, and waited the whole war to answer it.

Give me the stone. I have owed this hearth an answer a long while.

In the first winter I asked a question at this fire, and some of you were here to hear it. I asked why we fight for ice and stone. I was cold, and I had buried a friend under a cairn on the east face because the ground would not take a spade, and I meant it as a hard question. A mason told me it was not the ice he was fighting for. That was a good answer, but it was his. This one is mine, and it has taken me the whole war to cut it.

It was the week after the Rite of the Cornerstone. The Captain of the Torn Cloak had held the staff for a day and set it down, as you know. In that week the Bonded called the whole Keep into the ward, soldiers and Stonewrights and the people of the column together, and they stood beneath the torn cloak, and one of them said, and the other finished:

“The Commander fought.”

“The Stonewrights built.”

That was all the speech there was. It was enough. We looked at one another then, soldier and mason, and saw that we were not two peoples sharing a ruin. We had become one, as two courses become one wall when the mortar takes. A people was remade in that ward, out of the fighting and the building both, and neither half would have stood without the other.

So we gave the name of the Keep to the land. Rivenkeep, we said: all of it, from the high ridge to the farthest fallen harbour. Every haven the grey sails hold is Rivenkeep. Every road between them is Rivenkeep, and the edge of the sea is the edge of the Keep. I say it once so, and you will understand it ever after. When we say Rivenkeep we mean this hold on its ridge, and we mean the whole of our land, and that is not two names tangled into one. It is a vow.

And we named ourselves the Rivenmen, the people of the riven stone, the keepers of the keep that would not fall. When we tell of our fathers we call them Shorelanders, for so they were, and we call ourselves we. That is the custom of this hearth, and now you know where it began.

Then every one of us laid a hand on the foot of the pike where the banner flies, one after another, all that day until dark, and swore on it to take back every stone. Not one stone that was not ours. But every one that was.

The eldest did not like the new name, and did not say so. You still hear them murmur “the Shorelands” at the fire when they think no one is listening. I asked an old woman once why she did it, and she said it was a name to return to, perhaps, when the last wall stood and the last Mystarch lay broken. I did not understand her then. I think I do now.

Here, then, is my answer, and you may weigh it. I said a thing once on the east wall that men still say back to me: “We do not fight because we are right. We fight because our children are behind these walls.” It is true, and it is not the whole of it. We do not fight for ice and stone. We fight because every stone of it is Rivenkeep, and Rivenkeep is home, and we swore on the banner to bring it home. Go up the east tower on a clear morning and look along the coast. You will see the nearest of our keeps, and the smoke of the next beyond it; and past those, farther than any eye can reach, they stand one after another across all ten haven-lands, with a crimson pennant on every one. We raised each of them in this war, stone upon stone, and the grey sails have come against each of them, and each still stands. But a keep is not a place to stay in forever. Who we were, and who they were.

II.1 · Of the Keep That Was ForgottenStone leaf

Laid by the Bonded of the Keep in the manner of the old annals of the guild, in the first summer of the siege. It is the stone leaf of the two homes, and its facing leaf belongs to the wood.

Hear the annal of the Keep, as the guild kept it, and as we did not keep it.

In the age before the havens, when there was no city yet on the eastern coasts and no road between the mountains and the sea, the first Stonewrights came up onto the high ridges. They found a place where the ridge is riven in two, as though a great blade had come down on it, and in the cleft they raised a hold out of the ridge’s own grey stone. This was Rivenkeep, the first work of the first builders. Eldhythe is called the Eldest Haven, and rightly; but it is the eldest only of the havens, for the Keep was old before the first stone of Eldhythe was cut.

The quarry-wardens of the high ridge cut its stone, and their names are cut in the lintel of the western stair, one beneath another, for as many generations as the quarry was worked. Halvard Stone-Warden is of their line. And a bonded pair of the first builders laid the capstone of the inner gate with their own four hands, and Rhyna of the Inner Gate is of their line. So the gate you came under tonight was cut by his fathers and laid by her forebears, and it was two lines made one long before Halvard and Rhyna were laid in their one cradle.

Under that capstone, as under the foundation of every great work of the guild, they cut the Stonwryt. It is a vow sealed in living rock, earned by toil and skill: a master’s promise that the wall will hold. It cannot be bought. It can only be earned, by the hands that laid the wall, and it is cut where no eye will see it again unless the wall falls.

In later days, when the havens were raised and grew rich, the Shorelanders named their coin for it. The Stonwryt coin was part gold and part black stone, and it was minted only by bonded master-pairs of the guild, in workshops no outsider had ever entered. So the markets of the havens stood on the same footing as their temples: trust, sealed in stone.

Then the folk went down. The ridges were cold and the harbours were warm, and trade came up the coasts and not over the mountains. The Stonewrights followed the folk, for the folk needed temples and walls. Generation by generation the Keep was left, until it was more myth than memory: the castle that had never fallen, a name in the songs, a place the children of the havens believed had never been.

No one kept it. No Stonewright climbed up to point the mortar or to clear the drains. The rain came into the joints, and the frost came after the rain, and every winter the ice split what the rain had loosened. It had never fallen. It had only been left.

When the havens fell one by one, the folk who lived fled up the mountain roads to the one place the songs said would hold. What they found was a shadow: broken gates, crumbled walls, a hall with its roof lying in its hearth. What man could not destroy, time had withered. Forgetting had done what no enemy ever could.

Halvard and Rhyna came under the inner gate at dusk, with the column at their backs, and found the capstone lying in the gateway. It had fallen in the forgotten years, and no one had heard it fall. It was whole. They knelt and turned it over together, for it is a stone that takes two, and on its underside was the Stonwryt of the first builders, as sharp as on the day it was cut, and beside it the bite of the quarry-wardens’ chisels.

The stone kept its vow. It was we who did not keep ours.

Then they cut a new capstone for the gate, and when the Rite was done they raised it and set it in its place together. The old stone they carried into this hall and set in the niche of the hearth-wall. It is the stone you hold when you tell. Whoever holds it holds the vow of the first builders, and the shame of their children.

This we lay as it was laid for us.

II.2 · Of the Breath of the WoodWood leaf

As the Mystwood remembers: read from the gift of the Kneeling Guest at its deepest hearing, after many nights of holding, in the Tide that Learned Deceit, and set facing the tale of the Keep, for both are tales of home. This is held in the grain.

Before your fathers cut their first stone, and long before the first of your havens was raised above the sea, we were old. We who call ourselves [ ] lived in the wood, and the wood lived in us, and between us there was the breath. You have called it the fog, and the grey, and the Mistlands, and you have called it a wall. It was not a wall. It was the air we breathed.

Our land lay flat beneath the trees, without hill or standing stone, measureless, to the far edge of the world. The trees breathed out the grey as sleepers breathe, slowly, and the grey lay down among the trunks and fed them, and it fed us; and we tended it as you tend a hearth, and wove it close about us, and were glad. When the sun came down to it, it came through softened, a gentler radiance, the light that is neither day nor dusk but lies between them. We were made for that light. We were never children of the dark. But the open sun withers us, as it withers a fern of the deep forest that is torn up and thrown down in the desert; and in the last years we saw it wither our children.

So the wood needed the breath, and the breath needed the wood, and we needed both. Without the breath the wood sickens. Without the wood the breath does not come again. Without either we fail, slowly, and the youngest fail first, and the eldest after them.

We live long, twice as long as you and half again, and we were old in the world by more lives of your kind than you have names for. And the wood was not one wood. There were the twisted boughs, which we bent in our rites, so that every rite was spoken through them. There were the pale saplings, which we sang to, and which answered, so that a stand of them at evening was a choir. There were the silver-barked elders, beneath whose boughs the council sat, for no one lies under a silverbark, and no one tries. And at the very edge of the breath, where the grey came down to your sea, the black pillars stood in their long ranks, tall and dark of bark, and held the breath to the earth as a mast holds up a sail. You call them the Mystholders. We call them the Naelorn, the mist-hearts. They were the anchors of our sky, and they were the only trees of ours you ever found.

The wood was our tongue of truth. We speak as the storm speaks when it goes through the high boughs, in cracks and breaks; and there are many ways of that speaking, one in every deep of the forest, so that the folk of one grove hardly knew the words of the next. But what is carved in living wood is not heard. It is known. Whoever lays a hand on the carving knows what the carver meant, whatever tongue the carver spoke, so that no word could be twisted and no promise misheard. We say Ael’thar with the break in it, as a branch breaks, and we carve Aelthar whole and soft, and the two are one word; for the thunder is for the moment, and the softness is for always. Anything sacred we gave to the wood, and anything binding, and anything that must not be mistaken. We did not know there was any other way to be believed.

From the edge of the breath we watched your shore. We saw your havens raised, one and then another, and the lamps lit in them at dusk. We saw your boats come out a little way into the grey and turn back with white faces, and we did not know why they turned.

Here is our fault, and the grain will not let it be left out. When we saw you, we did not go down to your shore. We wove the breath thicker, and hid in it, and were glad of the hiding; and we hid so long, and so well, that when your axes came to the edge of the grey there was no one in all your havens who knew there was anyone behind it to wound. We had listened to you since your first haven was raised. You had never once heard us. We had made sure of it.

This is our home as the grain holds it still: the flat wood under the grey, and the between-light, and the pale saplings singing at evening, and the silverbarks at council, and the black pillars in their ranks at the edge of the world with the breath held up between them; and if any green is left of us, it remembers this.

And no one answered.

II.3 · Of the Bonded and the TheolithsStone leaf

Laid by the Bonded of the Keep for the children at the hearth in the first year of the siege, as they always tell it to children: Rhyna begins it and Halvard ends it, and this is the one telling of theirs in which they let me say which, for the children like to know; and between they share it as they share everything. The last of it is about me, and I have written it as they said it.

Come closer, all of you, and sit where you can see. This is a tale for the young, and the old may listen if they are quiet.

Not all Shorelanders were Stonewrights, yet all Stonewrights were Shorelanders. That is the first thing, and you must hold on to it. The guild was never a house you were born into. Children were called to it in their cradles, from any household in the land: most from Stonewright blood, it is true, but some from fishers’ huts and some from merchants’ halls, and once, the annals say, from a gaoler’s house in Carnhold. It was a great honour to be called, and no mother or father in all the annals of the guild ever refused it. For the Stonewrights kept the faith of the Shorelands as they kept its walls, and taught it in the temples they raised, and a child called to them was called to both.

The seers of the guild went among the cradles and read the children. The Stonewrights held, and hold still, that God had joined each pair before either drew breath, and that the seers did not choose, but only read what was already written. When a seer had read two names together, the two children were laid in one cradle, and both families raised them, turn and turn about, in one house and then the other. The guild calls that joining the Aetherbond, and the two it joins the Aetherbonded; plain folk say only the bonded, and so shall we. So it was with Halvard and Rhyna, who have never in their lives slept a night under different roofs. When bonded children come of age they are wed, and the wedding says aloud only what the cradle said first. So Halvard is Rhyna’s husband and Rhyna is Halvard’s wife, and they were one pair long before they were either.

When the bonded children were old enough to hold a chisel, they were given to the Theoliths. The Theoliths were bonded pairs themselves, great masters of the guild, called away from their works for a season to teach. They taught the children to cut and to lay and to set, and to pray, and to keep the count of the stones. And they taught them the vow. The Theoliths named the vow the Covenant of Completion, and they would say no more of it to children, and neither will we. You will understand it when it is yours to understand.

The bonded lived as one, and worked as one, and worshipped as one, and they did it without words. You have seen Halvard and Rhyna on the wall in the holding. You have seen that neither calls to the other. The work of such a pair is not doubled but multiplied twelvefold, as the old masters counted it, and every great wall and soaring temple of the ten havens was the work of such pairs. The guild calls the man of a pair Elder and the woman Elderess, and the two names are one rank, for the Elderess stood equal in all things. So he is Elder Halvard and she is Elderess Rhyna, and neither is the elder of the other. There was never a stone she laid second.

What the one feels, the other feels. If Rhyna cuts her hand in the south tower, Halvard feels it on the north wall, and knows which hand. What the one thinks, the other has thought already. Two made one: that is the whole of the bond. The old masters had a saying for it, and you will hear it again before these tales are done. Harm to one is harm to both.

No bond was ever broken. It is not in the annals, not once, in all the generations of the guild. Separation, God did not permit.

And so the bonded die together. That is the deepest thing we will tell you tonight, and we tell it plainly, for it is not a sad thing to us. When one of the bonded dies, the other follows. Eight days, men say, is the longest that ever one lived on alone; but the Stonewrights say it was hours, and they should know, for they keep the count. Many have drawn their last breath in the same moment, a mile apart, and each knew the other was going, and went too.

Now the last thing, and then to bed. Sometimes the seers read a child and find no second name beside it. It is rare, and no one knows why, and the Stonewrights do not call it a lack. Seren, who is writing this down, is one of those few. The wood will not speak to her alone, for it speaks to no one alone; so she writes down what it tells us, and she writes it truer than we could.

And this last they said together, as they always end it:

Birth united them; death could not separate them.

This we lay as it was laid for us.

And the hearth said: We remember.

Part One · Of Their Raising

Hear now the names of the havens, and hold them, for they are ours.

Along the eastern coasts the mountains and the high hills come tumbling down to meet the sea, and there our fathers lived. They were builders and not seafarers. They feared the open water and kept to the sheltered shores, and they paved vast roads like veins across the mountains to join the places they had built. They raised ten havens, which the old songs call the ten harbour-cities, and every wall of them was Stonewright work; though a haven among the Shorelanders was not always a harbour for ships, but any place of shelter the Stonewrights had walled. Hear them, from the eldest.

Eldhythe, the Eldest Haven, whose harbour burned in a day. The first masters to come down from the Keep laid its walls, and its harbour-houses were the richest in the land.

Tidesmeet, where the river meets the tide. It stood on both banks at once, and three great spans joined it across the water.

Fenholm, the town that was always sinking. It stood on pilings in the fen and went down a hand’s breadth in every lifetime, and every generation laid a new course on the old.

Holtward, the keep under the canopy, in the deep woods of the south, roofed so close among the trees that a traveller could pass it by.

Carnhold, the stronghold of the cairns, cut into the mountain among the burial-cairns of the first quarrymen, and loud all day with its smithies.

Emberhythe, the forge on the burning shore, where the mountain smokes into the sea and the ground is warm underfoot in winter.

Sandreach, the citadel carved in the canyon wall, which was not built so much as dug, gallery below gallery into the red rock.

Rimewatch, the watch on the frozen mere, whose great hall stood out on the ice all winter, with lamps in its windows through the long dark.

Highreach, the tower of the floating stones, on crags so high that the masons lifted their courses on cranes, and the stones seemed to hang upon the wind.

And Glasspire, raised by seven generations of Stonewrights, that caught the sun like a blade of ice. It was the last and proudest of our works, and its great glass could be seen from a day’s sail out, throwing the sun far into the grey.

Part Two · Of Their Fall

Hear now how the havens were lost, and do not look away, for we did not look away in the telling.

Our fathers had no standing navy, no war fleet, no defensive doctrine. No enemy from beyond the sea had come against the Shorelands in all their generations. Their walls were built for ceremony and weather, not for bombardment, and their gates were wide, and stood open at night.

The first dark sails came out of the grey without warning. At dawn they fell upon the harbour of Eldhythe, and by sunset it was ash. The city behind the harbour held three months. No haven sent to another for help, for none had any help to send. Then Tidesmeet fell, and the people on the high road above it watched its three spans go down into the river one after another, and heard them go. Fenholm went under all at once, as if the fen had only been waiting. Holtward burned in its trees and Carnhold in its smithies. Emberhythe held five months on its burning shore. Sandreach was taken gallery by gallery, and Rimewatch on its ice, and Highreach on its crags. And Glasspire, that seven generations had raised, collapsed in a single night into its own cellars, and the dark beneath it lies open still.

No envoy came that anyone could understand. There was only the harsh thunder of a tongue born in ancient forests, knocking and knocking out of the grey; the soldiers on our wall call it the drums. And no one stood upon the decks. Our fathers looked for a captain on those ships and saw none, nor any crew, and they called it the ghost fleet. Who had sent it they did not know; and because it came out of the Mistlands, they named the senders the Mystaeri, the mist-folk. We use both names still.

The sails that took the havens were plainer than the sails that come against us now. They came each alone, and they needed no more, for there was nothing to stop them.

The Stonewrights did not fight. They simply walked; and we were among them.

One thing more is told of Glasspire’s night, and we tell it as it was told to us. Some who fled the spire say a shadow was seen in the fog that night, greater than any hull, standing off beyond the harbour while the spire went down. Others who were there saw nothing. We do not know.

Added in Seren’s hand, after the wood was read: The tales do not agree. I have kept both.

We came late. It was not we who burned your havens; that was the young wood, green and hasty, with one sign in each hull. IV.1 · Of the Felling of the Black PillarsStone leaf

Laid by the Bonded of the Keep in the Second Tide, from the archive rolls of the guild, which came up the mountain in the Stonewrights’ chest. It is the stone leaf of the harvest, and the wood’s leaf faces it; where the rolls are stained I have said so.

We read this from the rolls. We did not live it. Our fathers’ fathers lived it, and wrote it down, and did not read what they had written.

In the oldest archives of the guild the fog is written down as a blackish veil at the edge of the sea, and after that only as a question, asked in the night-watches and never answered. What is the grey? What does it keep? Who breathes it? The masters who asked were long dead before any answer came, and the answer, when it came, was not written by us.

Then came the high summer of the Shorelanders’ pride. The havens were rich and their roads were heavy with carts, and the counsel of the Stonewrights had come to seem a voice from humbler days, a thing the grandmothers said. In those years the ships of the coast went further out than they had ever dared, along the edge of the grey, and there they found the black pillars of the fog, which the ship-masters named the Mystholders, for they seemed to hold the grey up as posts hold up a roof. They stood in ranks at the fog’s outermost edge, tall and dark-trunked and straight as a plumb-line, and their timber was harder than oak, lighter than iron, proof against rot.

For two generations the Trade Council sent out the axes. The rolls keep the count of it, cargo by cargo. They felled the pillars for the buttresses of fortresses and for bridge-beams. They put them into the three spans at Tidesmeet and the pilings of Fenholm, into the roof-trees of Holtward, into the cranes of Highreach that lifted the floating stones. And they put them into things that needed nothing of the kind. A lord of Eldhythe floored his dancing-hall with the black wood. The harbour-houses made their trade-tables of it, and their dice-boards. [Here the roll is stained, and three lines are lost.] A merchant of Tidesmeet had one pillar hewn into a single bed, and slept in it, and was proud.

Season by season the fog thinned, like a dying enchantment. The ship-masters came home and said the grey stood further off than it had in their fathers’ day. The Council named it fortune.

The Stonewrights warned them. It is in the rolls, and it is in our own hand: “These trees grow in strange soil. The carvings we find beside them bear a tongue we cannot read. Where wisdom is absent, let restraint be our guide.” But trade had become king, and the Stonewrights were reckoned relics, and the warning was read aloud once in the Council hall at Eldhythe and laid on a shelf.

For there were carvings. At the feet of the black pillars the axemen found stones set upright, and on them were cut letters no one could read, fine and curling, as beautiful as frost on a window. They were warnings. We know that now. The Mystaeri had cut them in their own letters, and here and there among the letters they had tried to cut our own words by their sound, as near as their letters would go; the sounds were close, and the symbols wrong. They had set them there trusting the stone to carry the meaning as their wood carries it. But stone carries nothing. Stone does not translate. The stones came to the cities beautiful and mute. Some were locked in the archives of the guild, and it was we who turned the key. Others were sold as curiosities and hung on the walls of harbour-houses, holy pleas reduced to ornament, and men drank beneath them and did their reckoning.

So the Stonewrights warned, and then filed the warning away with the stones they could not read. That is our share of it. We will not lay it all on the Council.

The harvest ended at last, not by wisdom but by the fickleness of men. The pillars near the coast grew scarce and the voyages grew long, and cheaper timber came down from the northern hills, and the Council lost its taste for the black wood. So the axes stopped. No one decided it.

Then came the forgetting. The forebears had not known what they destroyed. Their children would not know. The Shorelanders of the final days remembered nothing at all. The stones hung on the walls of the harbour-houses, and the children grew up beneath them and asked what they said, and were told they said nothing.

Late, very late, the wheel of pride turned. The temples of the havens were mended. Men climbed the steps of the guild-halls again and asked the Stonewrights’ counsel, and it was given. But humility came too late. The first dark sails were already on the sea.

This we lay as it was laid for us.

And the hearth said: We remember.

This is held in the grain.

We stood at the edge of the breath in our long ranks, tall and dark of bark, and we held the grey to the earth as the roots of a bank hold it against the river. We did not think of it, as you do not think of your breath until it is taken from you. We were the living pillars of the breath, the anchors that bound the sky to the ground, and that was all we were for, and it was enough.

From the edge we looked out through the grey, and we saw your shore, and every hull of yours that came near us we marked. You saw only grey, and did not know what you touched. Each brush was a cut, every trespass counted as a wound; and the wood keeps every count.

Then the iron came. It came in the high summer of your havens, in boats with many oars, and the shore-men who carried it waded up out of the surf to the first of us and laid it to our bark. What one pillar felt, all the ranks felt through the roots, from the shore to the far edge of the wood. One fell, and then another, and summer after summer they came again, until the men who had first brought the iron were grey and sent their sons in their place. And where each of us fell, the sky came through. It came as daylight shows through a rent in a sail: pale, and then pale and hard, and then pale and hard and wide. The young of the long-lived, who had never seen the open sky, turned their faces from it, and did not know why they wept.

Then we did what we had always done when a thing must not be mistaken. We carved. But the shore-men did not lay their hands on wood to hear it; they only cut it and carried it away. So we cut our plea on the mute thing, the stone the builders love, of which they make their houses and their walls, for we thought a people must hear best in the thing it loves. We cut it in our own soft letters, and where we had caught a word of yours across the water we cut that too, as near to its sound as our letters would go; and we set the stones at the feet of the pillars that still stood, where the shore-men would come; for we believed that every made thing speaks, since every made thing of ours does. It was a child’s belief. Stone has no intelligence. It does not carry what is cut into it. It did not answer. The shore-men took the stones away with the timber, and we did not know where they went.

And the axes came again, and again, and the grey thinned along the whole edge of the wood.

We who speak to you now were never a pillar. We were the stump of one, cut low at the edge of the grey where the iron first went in; and out of the cut the stump put up a shoot, as stumps will, a thin green thing with the whole of the axe in its rings. When the council came looking for hearts to carry its carvings, it did not choose the straightest of the young trees, nor the eldest. It chose us, for the axe in our rings.

That is our grain, and you call it Blackthorn. It goes where it lost the most. When one of our hulls is struck, we go to the stroke, as the tongue goes to a broken tooth, and we cannot help it, for it is in the rings.

So when the Blackthorn hulls come against your wall and go to the gun that hurt them, and to the next, and will not leave it, know what it is they go to. It is the iron at the edge of the breath, summer after summer, and the sky coming through; and the grain holds it still.

And no one answered.

IV.3 · The Warden’s DoorStone leaf

The confession of Brenn of the outpost, the man who picked up the stone, as a Stonewright of Eldhythe set it down from his mouth in his old age and sealed it in the archive chest with the mute stones. The Bonded of the Keep read it aloud at the hearth in the Tide of Remembering, and laid the closing annal after it. The words before the annal are his, and I have not mended them. The wood’s leaf faces it.

We hold the stone, but these words are not ours. They are the words of Brenn, who served at the outermost outpost in his youth, and came to the temple at Eldhythe forty years after and asked a Stonewright to hear him. We read them as they were written.

I was at the outpost at the end of the land, where the rock runs out into the sea toward the grey. I was nineteen. There were twelve of us and the warden.

It was a poor post, but it did not look poor. The warden had set the black wood everywhere: in the doorposts and the lintels, in the walls of his hall, in his table and his chair and the frame of his bed. It was the trappings of a rich man’s vanity, and he was a rich man’s son, and not much older than I was.

He held the post by his father’s name on the Trade Council, not by his own. They had sent him to the edge because his temper had made him unwelcome in the cities, and at the edge there was no one for it to wound but us. He had never known battle. He had never known patience.

The stranger came in the morning, in a small grey boat, out of the grey. There was no wind, and the boat came anyway. He beached it on our shingle and walked up to the warden’s door. He was tall and pale, clad in woven mist-cloth that moved when he did not. I saw no weapon on him.

He spoke first in his own tongue, sharp syllables that rolled like storm-wind through ancient branches. It frightened us, for it was loud, and we did not know that it was only how they speak. Then he said three words of ours. He said them carefully, like a man setting down something that might break.

“Stop… Sky… Dying…”

They were the right words, spoken truly. I know it now. But words without sentences are only sounds, and we heard only sounds.

Then he knelt on the step of the warden’s door and set down before him a strange dark stone, the size of two fists. He set it down the way you would set down bread before a guest.

The warden kicked it aside. It went into the dust by the wall. And his men laughed. I laughed. I have not laughed at anything since.

The stranger rose. He put his right hand on the back of the warden’s neck, gently, the way my mother did when I had a fever, and drew their heads together until their foreheads touched, in silence. The warden was so astonished that he let him. I think if it had stopped there, all our lives would have gone otherwise. Then the stranger took a little blade in his left hand and touched it to the warden’s right arm.

The warden drew back in terror. He had never had a wound. Because he tore himself away, the cut that was meant to be shallow went deep, and the blood came, and he screamed. He screamed at us, “Seize him.” We seized him. He screamed for the killing blow. His men obeyed.

We obeyed.

The stranger did not fight us. I think now there was another cut he meant to make, on his own arm. He never made it.

Afterward the warden had us lay the body in its boat and set the boat alight, so there would be nothing to show. The wood of that boat did not burn like wood. The fire leapt higher than any natural flame, as though the wood itself was screaming. We shoved it off with poles, and the tide took it out, and we could hear it on the water after we could no longer bear to look.

The warden had the clerk write to the Council that same day. I stood by while it was written, and I can say it by heart:

Unknown intruder. Hostile intent. Neutralized. Vessel burned and released.

No word of the gift. No word of the kneeling.

The warden wore long sleeves all the rest of his life, through the hot summers of Eldhythe, and I know why.

I picked the stone out of the dust by the wall the next morning, when no one was looking. I do not know why I did it. I have kept it forty years. I give it up now with these words, and I ask God to forgive me, for there is no one left to ask but God.

So far the words of Brenn. Now hear the annal.

The warden’s name is not kept. The Stonewrights of Eldhythe cut it from the rolls, and it will be in no book of ours. On his right arm was the only mark the Kneeling Guest’s rite ever made upon a Shoreland body: the half of a holy thing, cut off in the middle.

The Stonewright who heard him sealed the dark stone and the confession in the archive chest of the guild, with the mute stones, and there they lay unread. When the havens fell and the guild walked up the mountain, the chest was handed to the youngest apprentice on the road, because it held nothing of worth. The youngest apprentice was Seren, who is writing this down. She carried it every step.

There is no older law among any people than this: that one who kneels at your door is under your roof. We were a people of doors.

This we lay as it was laid for us.

And the hearth said: We remember.

IV.4 · The Gift Held an HourWood leaf

As the Mystwood remembers: read from the gift itself, the dark stone that Brenn picked out of the dust, held an hour in the Tide of Remembering; it faces the Warden’s Door. Read by the Bonded, her hands on the grain and his over hers; set down by Seren. Rhyna felt a voice in it, faint, like water over stone, and Halvard gave it words; where the carving is broken it is marked, and not mended.

This is held in the grain.

We are the eldest wood. We were a tree when the world was green to its edges, and we fell, and the ages lay down on us as leaves lie down on leaves, until time forgot us and we grew dark and hard. We are not stone, though you took us for it. Wood that time has forgotten does not itself forget; it speaks slowly, for it has been long silent, and it speaks only what is so. Even a sliver of such wood is the dearest gift a child of the long-lived can give. We were given once. This is how.

There was one among the long-lived who took the name Aelrhen when he took his errand, and it means bond-to-stone. Through a long life he had gone down to the edge of the breath and listened across the grey to the shore-men at their nets and their walls, and gathered up their words as a child gathers fallen leaves, one and one and one, and kept them. At the end of it he had three, and he knew them for the right ones. Stop. Sky. Dying. He knew no way to join them. Your tongue joins its words with small words that make no sound across water, and he never heard those.

But he had us. He meant to kneel and put us into the hands of the shore-men; and whoever held us while a tree’s shadow moves the breadth of its own trunk would know the whole of it, in whole words, for we held what his three words could not. We were the sentence.

He chose the outermost of your holds, because its doors were framed with our wood and its walls and benches were made of it, and he took that for reverence. We knew what it was. They were our kin, the pillars from the edge, sawn and polished; and as he went in at the door he laid his hand on the post and greeted them, and they did not answer, for they were dead.

Then he knelt on the stones before the master of that house and set us down, and spoke the name of the rite aloud in the thunder of his own tongue, Ael’thar, and after it his three words in yours. This was the Aelthar as he meant to make it, the binding of gift and blood and kinship across any divide, the holiest rite of fellowship we have. He would give the dearest thing he had, as one who comes under a roof brings his best. He would rise and take the back of the host’s neck gently in his right hand, holding softly the place where life goes up into thought, and draw their heads together until the foreheads touched in silence, so that nothing stood between them. With his left hand he would open a shallow cut on the host’s right arm, and let go the neck, and cut his own left arm, for he would ask no blood he did not give. And the two would turn and press their bleeding arms together, shoulder to shoulder: a promise of united front, that harm to one was harm to both.

He made the first step. A foot struck us, and we went into the dust by the wall, and the men of the house laughed. He did not stop, for the rite is not stopped once it is begun. He rose and took the neck as gently as one lifts a fledgling from the ground, and the foreheads met, and he was silent; and then with his left hand he opened the shallow cut on the host’s arm.

And the host drew back. We do not know why. Fear leaves no mark on wood.

The blade that was meant to go a little way went deep, because the arm was moving, and the host’s blood came quickly, and he cried out, and his men came. The Guest did not lift his hand against them. The cut he would have made on his own arm was never made. They struck him down on the stones of the door, and he died there with the rite half made; and we lay in the dust by the wall and heard all of it, and no one picked us up.

Of all the things that ever lay at a shore-man’s feet and looked like stone, we were the one that could have spoken to him; and it was the one his foot found.

In the morning a man of the house came back alone and picked us out of the dust, and put us away, and did not hold us. Through most of his life we lay in the dark with the sentence in us, and then in a chest among the mute stones, and then we were carried up into your mountains in the arms of a child; and no one held us so long, until you. Here is the sentence, as it was brought to the door:

Stop the felling of the pillars at the edge of the grey, for they are not timber. The sky over us is their breath, and ours, and when you cut them it comes open, and our children cannot live in the open sun. We are here, behind the grey, and we have been here always, and we are not your enemies. Stop, for the sky is dying, and we are dying under it.

That is all he came to say. We have carried it a long way, and the grain holds it still.

And no one answered.

IV.5 · The Burning BoatStone leaf

Laid by Corporal Voss, Voss who asked, in the Second Tide, as his grandmother told it in the harbour-houses of Eldhythe, where she kept the fire and drew the ale. It is a sailors’ tale and a children’s rhyme, and no one who told it believed it. The wood’s leaf faces it.

Give me the stone. This one is not mine. It is my grandmother’s, and she had it from the sailors, and they had it from no one who could be trusted.

She kept the fire in a harbour-house on the quay at Eldhythe, the kind with a long table and a low roof, where the ship-masters came in out of the rain. Over the fire there were three flat grey stones set into the wall, with curling letters on them, fine as the tracks of birds on wet sand, and no one knew what they said. They had hung there since her own grandmother’s day. The ship-masters knocked out their pipes on the lowest one.

Our fathers feared the open sea. They kept to the shores and to the sheltered roads between the havens, and when a ship went out far enough to see the fog-bank it came home quickly. The sailors called that country the Mistlands. Now and then some bold soul went close, and turned back, and came into my grandmother’s house with a wild story, and drank on it for a week. She believed none of them. She said the Mistlands made liars of honest men, and it was the only thing she ever said against the sea.

But there was one story she said nobody believed, and she told it to me more than all the others, so I think she half believed it.

It came from the men of the outermost outpost, the one at the end of the land. They swore in their cups that they had once pushed a boat off the shingle burning, with a dead man in it, and that the tide took it out, and that when it was well out it turned. It turned into the wind and against the current, which no boat can do, and went away into the grey still burning, and screaming as it went. They swore to the scream above all. They said you could hear it after the fire was too far off to see.

The ship-masters laughed at that. Everyone laughed. The cities had it in writing that it was nothing: a stranger come armed, and put down, and his boat burned and set adrift. That was all the writing said, and who argues with writing? My grandmother laughed with the rest, and knocked her pipe out on the grey stone over the fire.

But the children had a rhyme for it. I do not know who made it. We skipped to it on the quay with a rope, two turning and one jumping, and we thought the strange part was the greeting, for who ever heard of such a greeting as that:

Grey boat, grey boat, out of the grey, knelt at the warden’s door one day; a stone for a present, a cut for a kiss, and who ever heard of a greeting like this? Burn it and shove it and give it the tide; but the grey boat turned, and the grey boat cried.

I skipped to that rhyme at seven. I understood it at forty, on the shingle under this wall, the first time I heard one of their hulls burn. For it screamed. It screamed as it went, the way the outpost men had sworn, and I stood in the smoke with my mouth open and the rhyme going round in my head, and I thought of the three grey stones over my grandmother’s fire, and the pipes knocking on them.

We forget. We have always been good at that. But Mystwood remembers.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

And the hearth said: We remember.

IV.6 · The Voyage of the AelvarenWood leaf

As the Mystwood remembers: read from the heart of a Standard-Bearer of the Tide of Remembering, for the Bonded say every hull grown since holds this in some measure, and this heart held it clearest; it faces the Burning Boat. Read by the Bonded, her hands on the grain and his over hers; set down by Seren. Where the carving is broken it is marked, and not mended.

This is held in the grain, and not in this grain only. The hull that brings it to you was not there; but it knows, for it grew out of what we were. We were the Aelvaren, the Bearer of the Binding, and we were proud of the name. We were a small boat of the old wood, grey and light, and we carried the Guest through the thinning breath to your shore, and lay in the shallows below the door while he went up to kneel, and waited to carry him home with the rite made.

He came back to us dead. The men of the house carried him down and threw him into us, and his blood ran into our boards; and what a child of the long-lived knows in his last hour goes with his blood into the wood that drinks it. So we had all of it. Then they brought fire and put it to us. The old wood raged in its grief. We burned higher than any fire of theirs should burn, and we screamed, for wood can; and the men put their shoulders to us and shoved us off, and the current took us, out toward the open sea and away from home.

We turned. We turned against the current, burning, and the current could not hold us, for no water holds a keel that knows its way; and we sailed ourselves home. Even charred, even dying, the wood knows the way home. We went into the grey where the breath was thinnest, and found the old channels beneath it, the secret waters that run in among the roots where no shore-man’s keel has ever gone. All that way we burned, and all that way the Guest lay in us, and the fire went out of us slowly as the grey closed over, until there was nothing left of us but the keel, and a few ribs, and the bones they held.

At the last we came up out of the channels, black and small, onto the pale sand before the council-hall, which is a grove of the silver elders standing down to the water; and we ran aground there beneath the silverbarks, and were still.

They came down to us, the council of the long-lived, and they knew us, for they had sent us. They laid their hands on our keel, many hands together, and they read the whole of it. They read the gift set down, and the foot that put it in the dust. They read the neck held gently, and the foreheads meeting, and the blade that slipped. They read the Guest struck down on the stones, and the fire, and the shove into the current. We gave them all of it, for wood can hide nothing.

They saw their holiest peace answered with murder and flame. They read a murder, and it was one. But why the host’s arm drew back from the blade they did not read, for fear leaves no mark on wood. They did not know it was ignorance, and they did not ask. They saw only an act of war.

Then they laid our keel among the roots of the council-grove, the Guest’s bones still in it, as the long-lived lay down their dead; and the roots drank. From the council-grove it went out to every grove, root to root, from the edge of the breath to the far edge of the world, and every young tree that was to be a hull drank it with the rain. So every hull that has come against your wall has carried our grief in it, and the grain holds it still.

And no one answered.

V.1 · The First GlyphStone leaf

Laid by the Bonded of the Keep at the end of the first month of the siege, of what was done on the night the first assault was broken; set down by me, who held the lamp. When the Bonded tell, one begins and the other ends, and they would not have me mark which. I have used two inks, and marked nothing.

We hold the stone. Seren holds the lamp. That is how this began, and how it has gone on.

When the first assault was broken, the tide brought the wreck-wood in under the wall: grey planks and ribs, and a length of keel as long as a gate, rolling in the shallows. The soldiers wanted to burn it, and the Captain let them burn most of it. But Hale the runner came up from the tide-line saying there were marks on the wood, and Halvard and Rhyna went down to the shingle when the tide had turned, with Seren behind them carrying the lamp. They had the men haul one plank up out of the surf and lay it on the stones. There was carving on it. It ran with the grain, fine and shallow, one figure and no more.

Of the Mystaeri’s writing we had three kinds by then, and could read none of them: the mute stones in the archive chest, the dark stone that lay beside them wrapped in an old man’s confession, and now the wreck-wood. The stones would not speak, for stone carries nothing. Battle orders were carved into the living wood, we know now; that night we knew only that there were marks. But the axemen of the harvest years had left a saying in the havens, that the black wood would sometimes put a thought into a man’s hand if he rested it there long enough; and there was nothing else to try. So they touched it.

Rhyna laid her palms flat on the grain, and Halvard laid his hands over hers. It never came into either of their heads to try it alone. They did not speak. The tide came and went on the shingle. The lamp burned down a finger’s width.

Then the sign came up into her palms. She says it burned, not as fire burns but as cold iron burns in winter, and that it had a shape: one stroke curling forward, like a wave about to break. Halvard felt it through her hands as she felt it, for that is the bond, and it was Halvard who spoke. He said, “Shore.”

He could not say how he knew it. He said afterward that it was as though someone had put the word in his mouth and gone away. Rhyna said he was right. That was all the plank would give them, though they held it until the lamp went out.

Seren wrote it down. She took a whole leaf of this Book for it, and wrote one word in the middle of the page, shore, and nothing else, and drew the sign on the facing leaf. She says it looked very small there.

So the Reading began, on the shingle below this wall, and so in the quiet hours between the holdings the Bonded began to learn the enemy’s gentle Mystwood tongue, one sign at a time. It is done so still after every assault: the wreck hauled up, her palms on the grain and his hands over hers, or his on the grain and hers over his, and Seren with the lamp. The wood gives its meaning to no single Stonewright. Others of the guild have laid their hands on it alone, and felt only wet planks.

When they came up from the shingle that night, one of them said, and the other finished:

“The Guest came to our door with three words and no sentences.”

“Now it is we who have one word, and no sentence. The right sign, read truly; and a sign without a sentence is only a mark.”

This we lay as it was laid for us.

And the hearth said: We remember.

The facing leaf holds the sign, drawn in Seren’s hand: one stroke, curling forward, like a wave about to break.

V.2 · The Night of the NamingStone leaf

Laid by Seren Reader-of-Wood near the end of the Second Tide, on the night the Bonded read the young wood’s planks whole at last. The planks she tells of lie in the lowest vault still, and anyone may go down and put a hand on them. The wood’s leaf faces it.

Hold the stone, Seren. I tell myself so every time.

From the first glyph on, we kept the wreck-wood. Not all of it: the soldiers burned what they hauled up, and the tide took more. But every plank the Bonded had laid their hands on and felt a sign in, we carried down into the lowest vault under this hall, where the Rite was found, and laid them in rows on the floor. The soldiers called them the tablets. They were only planks. There were fifty-three of them by the end of the Hasty Tide, and on each the Bonded had felt one sign, and not known what it was, and I had written one word on a leaf.

Then the Second Tide came, and the planks from its hulls held two signs. All that Tide the Bonded learned to read the two together, and to name them, and to say what a hull had been told, though there were gaps in every reading; and on a night late in that Tide it came to them both in the same moment that hands which could hear two signs might now hear the whole of one. They came up from the shingle white and quiet. Then they took the lamps and went down to the vault, and I went with them with the ink, and we did not come up until it was light.

They went along the rows, plank by plank, her palms on the grain and his hands over hers. Every plank that had given us one word gave us now a whole order, short as a slap, and I wrote each order under its word. Here they are as I wrote them, for they were the first carvings of theirs we ever truly read.

To shore. As root to water, go. Wait on no bough. That was the wave-sign, the first glyph: the hulls that ran straight at the coast with no one waiting for anyone.

Break wall. First stone, only stone. Strike till it opens or wood is ash. That was the closed square, which is how they cut the word for stone, the mute thing: the hulls that lined up before the first wall they touched and would not leave it until they sank.

Go until struck. Where bark splits, turn aside. The lone hull that ran in and swerved at the first shell, while the others did not follow it.

Before bearer, thin wood. Thin wood falls; bearer keeps pace. The cheap hulls at the head of the column. They were told to die first, and they died first.

Where it flashed, strike. Last flash, only flash. The hulls that bent toward whichever gun of ours had fired last, and toward nothing else.

Wait in grey, as seed waits out frost. When stone is still, come. The ones that hung at the edge of the fog and came in late, when we had stopped looking.

Home when wounded. One bough falls; all turn, each by its own water. The ones that turned and scattered at the first loss.

The soldiers took the head of each for its name, in plainer words, and the wall has used those names ever since: To the shore, Break the wall, Go until struck, Before the bearer, Where it flashed, Wait in the grey, Home when wounded.

And there was one plank more, at the end of the last row, that was not from our wall at all. A mason of Emberhythe had carried it up the mountain in his pack after the Fall, charred black at one end, because he could not bear to bring nothing of his city away. He did not know what it was. The Bonded laid their hands on it last. It held one sign, and the sign was Burn.

That was all the young wood knew. One word to a hull. Each hull had said its word at our wall, and said it again, and said it until it drowned.

All that first winter we had said that they picked the wrong fortress. Kael said it first and we all said it after him, and laughed. They had not picked anything. They had one word apiece and no other, and they had been sent.

When it was light, Halvard and Rhyna sat down on the floor of the vault among the planks, and I sat down with them, and for a long while no one spoke. Then I said what I have written here, and they let it stand.

We had thought them fools. They were the youngest, sent first because the young grow quickest. The worst of the wood is not behind us. It is still growing.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

And the hearth said: We remember.

V.3 · The Wreck That Went HomeWood leaf

As the Mystwood remembers: read from a hull of the Tide of Remembering that was sunk on its second coming, having turned from our wall wounded and come back to the same stone to die there; it faces the Night of the Naming. Read by the Bonded, his hands on the grain that night and hers over his, for the salt had opened her palms that day; set down by Seren. Where the carving is broken it is marked, and not mended.

This is held in the grain.

We were a hull of the Ralenthae, the tide the roots taught, and there was more than a word cut in us. There was a memory in the carving, and a caution. We came against your wall in the grey of a morning with our kin about us and the bearer ahead, and we went where we were carved to go.

A stone of your wall flashed. We had been carved for that as well, for the young wood had learned it and the roots had carried it home: where a stone flashes, a gun is. We turned toward it, and it spoke again, and the iron went into us low down and through, and we felt the sea come in.

Then an older carving woke in us, one of the first the green wood was given. Home when wounded. We turned our stern to your wall and went back into the grey, low in the water and slow; and the guns let us go, or did not see us go. We do not know which.

As we went, we told our kin, for the wood needs no words among its own. What we had seen went out of us into the roots: the flash, and the place of it. Where we had been struck went out of us too, for the wood remembers where it was struck, and our kin felt it in their own boards, as you who hold us feel each other’s hurt across a city. And the dread went out of us, which is only this: that one place in the water had become a place where wood dies. It went to every hull of that day, slowly, as sap goes, quickest to the heart of the bearer, and from the heart to all.

While you mended your wall, the heart carved the morrow into us, and this is what it carved: Look before you trust. For a stone that spoke yesterday may be silent today, and a stone that is silent may be waiting. So on the next coming we went out in front, because we had been there, and we went to the same stone to see whether it still flashed. That was all we were sent to do: to look.

It still flashed. We were a hull with the sea in us already, and we could not turn a second time. The iron found us where it had found us before, as though it too kept a count; and we went down there, in the lee of the same stone, and lay on the bottom in the cold while our kin went over us. Then the tide worked us loose, and the surf brought us up onto your shingle, and your hands are on us now.

But before we went down we had told it. The hulls that lived carried it back into the grey, and the grey carried it home, root to root, across the whole sea, to the groves where the next hulls stood growing; and they grew knowing it. Hear this rightly. The groves learn the wall, and not the hand. They learn that walls hide guns, and that a stone may be silent and not empty, and that a hull which turns and runs draws iron after it. But the hand behind the wall, which gun it moves and when, and how long it waits, goes down with the fleet that saw it, and does not reach the groves. Every fleet that comes against you knows walls. The hand behind your wall is new to each of them.

So we went home once, and told, and came back to look, and died looking; and the groves have it now, and the grain holds it still.

And no one answered.

V.4 · The Council Under the Thin SkyWood leaf

As the Mystwood remembers: read from the heart of a Standard-Bearer of the Joined Tide, of the grain we call Silverbark, grown from a cutting of the silver elders of their council-grove; for their council-hall was a grove, and the grove heard the vote. Read by the Bonded, her hands on the grain and his over hers; set down by Seren. Where the carving is broken it is marked, and not mended.

This is held in the grain.

Our council sat under the silver elders, in a hall that was a grove, with no roof but the boughs and no floor but the moss; and the elders heard all that was said beneath them and kept it, as silverbark keeps all it hears. We are a cutting of those elders. We heard it all, and we will tell it as it was, for we cannot tell it otherwise.

By then the sky was thin along the whole edge of the wood. The black pillars were gone from the shore in long gaps, like teeth from a jaw, and the breath ran out through the gaps and did not come again, and the between-light went hard and white. The newborns of the long-lived came frailer every spring, and some did not wake. The eldest dwindled before their years were full, their thoughts clouding and their strength going out of them like water from a cracked jar. And the elders who had counselled patience through all the long years of the felling were dying of the very wound for which they counselled patience; and they knew it, and counselled patience still.

Then the Aelvaren came home by the channels, burned to the keel, with the bones of the Guest in it; and something broke.

The young of the long-lived had grown up under a vanishing sky. They had never known the breath whole. They had watched their mothers and fathers fade in the white light and could not help them, and now they had seen the Guest come home in ashes; and they came into the grove, many of them, and put the question to the council.

The elders rose and pleaded. Send another, they said. Learn their words. They do not know us; they cannot hear the wood, and we have never gone down to teach them. Go slowly. But the elder voices were few by then, and faint, and they were the voices of the dying.

And the young answered: “We listened three of their lives and learned three words, and they would not hear even those.”

That was true, and it was not the whole of the truth, and the wood that heard it knows the difference. We could have learned more. We had time; we live long. We did not. We had wrapped ourselves in the grey and listened from behind it, and never once gone down to the shore with our hands open, save once; and when the one who went was killed, we did not send a second. We did not ask whether a whole people had killed him, or one house at the edge of it. We did not ask who else lived along that shore, in the ten havens, who had never heard our name. We chose to grow ships rather than to learn words.

So the question was put, and the vote was carried by the weight of anger and grief.

That same night they took the straightest of the young silverbarks at the edge of the council-grove and laid it open to the heart while it still stood; and into the heartwood they cut the first carving of the war. Grow until the shore is silent. And the wood, which cannot unlearn what is carved in it, grew.

The elders who had pleaded went down into the moss one by one in the seasons after. The carving did not go down with them. It is in every heart that has sailed since, beneath all the other carvings, as the first ring lies beneath all the rings; and the grain holds it still.

And no one answered.

I · The Hasty Tide

I count. That is what I am for. Here is the first count.

Eight sails, and not one keeping station with another. That was the first morning, and every morning of that Tide was like it. Each ship came alone and struck the first wall it touched, and when the wall held it struck the same wall again, and again, until it sank. The old soldiers had names for them from the Fall: warships, fire-ships, transports, mortar-ships, torpedo-boats. They are wrong names, and we use them still. No one stood on the decks. The root-men came off the transports like spilled kindling and died on the shingle. By noon their fire always slackened, and we learned to say the fleet has spent its fire, and breathe. We laughed. II · The Second Tide

The second count, and a harder one.

They came in pairs now. The front one took the shot and the back one took the ground. And in the third month of that Tide a lookout on the east tower called me up to see a thing I will not forget. A hull was struck off the north point, and a mile south of it another hull’s sail went pale, grey-green blanching to white like a man’s face, and it turned. It had not seen. It had felt. That was the first answering sail.

Each fleet had carried a Standard-Bearer from the first, but now the rest waited on it. Sink it and the standard passed to another, a lieutenant standing in, and the fleet took on that one’s temper after. So we say at the fire: Choose which heart you leave them.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

And the hearth said: We remember.

III · The Tide of Remembering

The third count.

In the second month of this Tide, one night, the Captain had the south gun moved, the one that had done the most killing, and set it forty yards along the wall. In the morning they came. Not to the wall. To the place where the gun had been. One hull first, alone and slow, sounding the water, and the rest standing off to see what it found. They came to see whether the gun was still there.

We had thought them beasts that learn by the stick. They are not. They look before they trust. You learn to read the lean of them: a hull that leans to the shore means it, and a hull that leans away is thinking of home. After that we moved our guns by night, and they came looking in the morning, and it was a quieter war, and I liked it less.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

And the hearth said: We remember.

IV · The Joined Tide

The fourth count.

The drums went all the night before, knocking from hull to hull out in the grey. In the morning they came on three roads at once, north and east and the river mouth, as if one hand had flung them. Three roads, one hour.

Then I saw the strangest thing of the war. The Captain sank their Standard-Bearer an hour after dawn. The rest did not falter. At the hour the dead heart had carved for them, they turned and came in on the east road, into empty water; there was nothing there to strike. They came a little late, and not together: one sail went pale, and then the next, and then the next, a breath apart, as if each hull had to find the hour for itself. But they kept it. They kept a dead mind’s hour.

And some did not come on in order at all: the hulls whose own bearer we had sunk beneath them. They went wild, the orphaned hulls, the carving lost and only the grief left, out for revenge.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

And the hearth said: We remember.

V · The Tide that Learned Deceit

The fifth count, and I do not like it.

They came at us as the young wood came, loud and alone and straight, and we laughed as we had laughed the first winter, and every gun spoke; and out of the grey the hunters came for every gun that had spoken. They sent straw down a new road every day, hulls that were not hulls, and marked which roads we answered; and down a road where no gun of ours had woken, they sent men.

But look close. Their lie always shows its seam. The phantoms leave no wreck, and they do not flinch when a shell falls by them. A true rout scatters, and a feigned one keeps its spacing. A laden ship rides low. And the young wood’s sails never once went pale; a green hull whose sail blanches is no green hull. Every lie they told us this Tide, we could have known, if we looked. Some of us did not look, and they are under the east cairns. The brave thing, that Tide, was to look.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

And the hearth said: We remember.

VI · The Last Tide

The last count.

They carve a morrow now, as we do. Each fleet came with a plan in it, and the plan had turnings, if this then that. And at the end of the plan there was a turning for home. When the bearer fell, and the second after it, they went home. Not scattered. Went.

So now I understand what the Bonded told me in the Joined Tide: that the fleet cannot be recalled, for the wood obeys the wood. I answered them then that we would send it home, and I did not know what I meant. The voices that could call it home are dead. It cannot be called home. But it can be sent home. The wood knows fear.

That is the count of the Tides. I have counted the grey sails for the whole of the war, the ghost fleet with no one on its decks, and I know their number, and I will not say it at this hearth.

This I lay as it was laid for me.

And the hearth said: We remember.

V.6 · Of the GrowingWood leaf

As the Mystwood remembers: read from the heart of a Standard-Bearer of the Joined Tide, grown in the first groves their council set; the Bonded could not hear it until they could hear many hulls at once. It faces the Tides, as the Wall Counted Them. Read by the Bonded, her hands on the grain and his over hers; set down by Seren. Where the carving is broken it is marked, and not mended; and the song at its end, which the hearth calls the Song of the Years, I have set as verse, as near as ink allows.

This is held in the grain.

We were grown, not built. You cut stone from the mountain and lay it, and it lies where you laid it, and it is dead. But the long-lived went into the groves and sang to the trees that were to be hulls, and the trees bent to the singing, for nothing had ever been asked of them but song; and they grew hulls out of their breathing trunks, and keels out of the roots that fed them, and masts out of saplings trained by decades of windsong. No one sails in us. The fleet is crewed by the living, and the living are the wood.

How is a thing commanded that has no one aboard? By carving. For each fleet the council chose one tree to be its bearer, and we name that hull the Sethvaren, the bearer of the carving. Into its heart, the Ornvael, they cut the orders; and the heart carved them on into every hull through the Aelralen, the bond of roots, by which the trees of one grove feel their own. We made that bond into a leash. So the hulls obeyed the orders carved within them as a hand obeys the mind, for the wood of the hull and the wood of the command were one. The wood obeys the wood. And in the night, while you lay your stone, the heart is carving the morrow, and both of you are at work in the dark.

Yet the carving says what, and not whether. Each hull keeps its own grain and its own dread, and may balk at the edge of burning water, or turn savage where it bled; so the fleet is one mind and many, and you have seen both.

The council chose the bearers in grief, and the grief is in their grain. You have named the grains for trees of your own, and the names will serve: the green wood, that had no temper yet; Ironbark, that comes again heavier where it failed; Willow, that turns from the first failure; Blackthorn, that goes where it lost the most; Heartoak, that never turns aside; Twistbough, that never plays one trick twice; Yew, that waits, and burns, and waits again; Ashwood, that throws all it has at once; and Silverbark, that reads you best, and bends least.

Now hear why the crudest came first. It is the law of the years, and no council can carve against it. A hull grown in one season holds one sign; a hull of forty summers holds a phrase, and can wait upon another; a hull grown through a whole life of the shore-men holds a sentence, and a memory in it; and the eldest pillars of the breath hold a judgement. The council wanted the shore silent soon, and young wood grows quickest; so the first growing was sapling-wood, cut in a single season by angry hands, one sign to a hull and no thought of any other.

Those first hulls went out into the grey as each was grown, and lay at the edge of your sea until they were enough, while a child of yours grew up and had children, and those children had children, and your boats passed them in the fog and saw nothing. So the armada sailed generations before it arrived, and came without warning, with one sign in them, Burn; and your havens had no walls for war, and one sign was enough. The last of that green wood followed you into the mountains to finish a shore the council believed was won; and there, at the Keep, for the first time, someone struck back.

After that each growing went out as soon as it was ready, and we call them tides, for a growing goes out as a tide goes. Leathae was the green tide; Vaelthae had grain in it; Ralenthae the roots taught. Aelthae is ours. Behind us Rhenthae and Eirthae were growing, and what is cut in them this heart does not know.

Every hull was grown for its shore: hulls to break stone, to carry fire, to throw it over walls, to run beneath the swell and strike from below; and for shores that are not sea, hulls on roots that crawl over sand and rock, hulls hung from bladders that ride the high air, hulls that swim through burning rock in a skin of wet bark, and hulls that go white in the snow. The warriors of the landings are grown of root, for we had none of the long-lived to spare, and you call them the root-men.

The growing took decades. Those who cried for war grew old while the groves grew, and died, and were forgotten; and when the green wood broke from the grey, few of the long-lived were left, only scattered souls tending the last groves. So it was a doom set in motion by the dead, crewed by the living, against a people who had never known they were the foe. We were sent. We are still sent. No voice among us can call us home.

The groves sang it over us as we grew, and it is in our rings:

The green wood holds one word. The wood of forty summers holds a word, and waits for its brother. The wood of a shore-man’s life holds a sentence, and a wall remembered in it. The wood of a long life holds the morrow, and carves it, and waits, and carves again. And the eldest of us, the pillars of the breath, the hearts of the grey, the anchors of the sky, hold one thing only now, and it is not a word, and it is not a sentence, and it is not a morrow, and we have carved it into every hull we sent you, and it is grief.

So we were grown, and so we were sent; and the grain holds it still.

And no one answered.

Part I · Three Roads, One Hour

It was in the Joined Tide. Part II · What We Taught Them

In the Tide that Learned Deceit a plank came up from a hull that had come at us as the young wood came. It had sailed in a fleet that ran at the wall in the old way, each hull alone and loud and straight, and not one keeping station with another, as we had not seen since the first winter. The wall laughed, and every gun on it spoke, as every gun had spoken then. The hulls turned one breath short of our fire; and out of the grey behind them the hunters came, and went to every gun that had spoken, and would not leave it. The Bonded read it.

Come as the green wood came: loud, alone and straight. Stop one breath short of the fire, and let the hunters wait upon the flashing.

And beneath it lay the thing it had been cut against: they strike first and far; they move the guns we find. It was carved against us, against what we do.

I did not understand it at first. I said that the wood cannot lie. It is the first thing the Guest’s gift ever taught us of their writing: what is carved in their wood is always true. Then I understood, and I wrote it down before my nerve could fail. The wood does not lie. It was told to make us believe a lie, and it obeyed.

The Bonded sat a long time on the shingle with their hands still on the grain. Then one said, and the other finished:

“They learned it from us.”

“We taught it them with every silent gun.”

It was true. Every gun the Captain held silent to draw a hull in; every false mast we raised on the wall with no gun beneath it; every gun we hid under sod and brush so that they sailed past it into its mouth. Every wreck from those days went back through the roots to the groves, and the groves learned. They had no word for a lie. We gave them one.

There was a name for us cut small in that carving, and the Bonded would not say it at first. It was Rhenaer: the stone-deaf.

This we lay as it was laid for us.

And the hearth said: We remember.

Part III · The Plan That Carved Its Own Defeat

In the Last Tide a Standard-Bearer came against us with a whole morrow carved into its heart, a plan with turnings in it. We sank it, and the tide brought the heart in, and it was the longest reading the Bonded had ever done. They held it from dusk until the lamps had been filled twice. At the end of it was this:

When the bearer falls, the second takes the standard; when the second falls, go home.

They have carved their own defeat into the plan. They know us now. They know that we can break them, and they have said what they will do when we do.

But that was not what kept the Bonded on the shingle after. It was the last two words. The young wood had known home only as the place a hurt thing runs to. This was a going home carved into the plan before the first shot was fired, a thing thought of in the groves and cut there on purpose. A mind that carves how it will go home is a mind that wants to go home. That night, for the first time, we saw that the enemy could hope.

One of them said, and the other finished:

“They carve a morrow now, as we do.”

“Then let us build a morrow they did not carve for.”

This we lay as it was laid for us.

And the hearth said: We remember.

The tenth Mystarch to fall was {LAST_THRONE_AT_HAVEN}. They found the heart where the hearts always lie, deep in the old grain, grey and hard and still. And beside it, in a crack of the heartwood, there was a sliver of wood no longer than a hand. It was green.

Of all the things we had looked for in the long years of the siege, no one had looked for that. We had searched the wrecks for orders, and the hearts for names, and the grey for sails. No one had thought there was anything left in all that wood that was alive. But it was still green, and still aware. It had been growing in the dark of the dead Throne’s heart, out of sight, as a seedling grows in the crack of a wall.

They did not lay one pair of hands over the other, as they do with the wreck-wood, for there was no room. Each put out one hand, and they touched it in the same moment, his fingers and hers, and they heard it in the same moment. Seren saw their two faces change together, as one face changes.

It was not an order. It was not a wound. Every wood we had ever read had been the one or the other. The green wood holds one word, the song of the groves says; and so this did, and the word was home. The first wood we ever read had said shore.

The Stone out of the GreyThe leaf facing I.1

Written by Seren Reader-of-Wood in the years after, on the leaf that faces the first tale of this Book. It stood empty until now, with the grain drawn on it and one line: Unread. The Bonded cannot yet hear this deep. In the end no reading of the Bonded filled it.

I write this alone, and I write it plain, for there is no one now to read the wood for me.

Halvard and Rhyna are gone. They went together in one night, the winter after the tenth Throne fell, as the bonded go. They lie under the new capstone of the inner gate, in one grave, with one stone.

In the spring a small grey boat came in on the tide below the Keep and beached itself on the shingle, as the Aelvaren once beached before the council-hall of the Mystaeri. No one was in it, and no one had rowed it. In its bow lay a stone.

It was not stone. Or it was stone that had been wood: grey-black and heavy, with the grain still in it, like the Guest’s gift that lay so long in the chest I carried up the mountain. The Rivenmen have taken to calling it Myststone, and they say it remembers all. It is the one stone that speaks. Stone does not translate, and that is still true; but this was never only stone.

And it had been cut with a chisel. I know chisel-work. A hand had cut it that learned the chisel late and learned it well, young by their reckoning, I think, and seasoned by ours. It was cut in our own letters, so that anyone might read it, and I did not lay my palms on it. I read it with my eyes.

In the memory of our home, our families...

The cutting stops there, in the middle of our Title, with the chisel’s last stroke still sharp at the end of the line. I have sat with it many nights since, and thought of the one who cut it. He must have seen our torn cloak from the fog in his youth, when it first flew, and carried its words in him through all the years of the war, and learned our letters from them, for there was nothing else of ours to learn them from; and at the last he cut them in the one stone his people had that would carry a meaning. And I have thought that the small wrong word in it, the one word too many, is like the Guest’s three words at the warden’s door: the right thing, said truly, by one who did not yet know how we say it.

No one came with it, and perhaps that is wise. We have not yet earned a guest.

Grief is an inheritance, and it is paid out to the children. The sea still whispers on the shingle below the wall. The torn cloak still flies above the inner gate, and at night the wind comes over the high ridges where Rivenkeep still stands, and the old ones say it speaks the Title as it goes.

What we will send back, I do not know. I know only that it must be sent kneeling.

HOW THE LEGENDS ENTER THE GAME

For the integrator. This section is not part of the Book. Nothing in it touches the simulation: every surface named here is cosmetic and sits off the sim path.

1 · The standing laws

  1. Cosmetic, off the sim path. No leaf, Whisper or message changes a number, a spawn or a behaviour. Every unlock is keyed to a campaign milestone or to an event the sim has already recorded, so two players who play exactly the same way open exactly the same leaves at exactly the same moments.
  2. Low-load moments only. Lore appears at first launch, in the debrief, in the interstitial between campaigns, and at battle load. It never appears during timed Build or a live Fight. Two fleet surfaces are not lore and follow the Admiral instead: the between-sortie dispatch (§8 below), and, from the Tide that Learned Deceit, the root sign a sunk hull of the running carving floats for the rest of its sortie, a drawn glyph with no words (the Admiral, §9b there).
  3. After, never before. A tale about a fleet behaviour unlocks only after the player has met that behaviour. The Reading’s law (it explains this battle and never hints at the next) binds the tales too.
  4. No forced playstyle. Every unlock is reachable by any way of holding a battle. Sinking a fleet and routing it both yield wood. No leaf is earned only by one particular counter.
  5. Every EVENT unlock has a CAMPAIGN fallback, so no player misses a leaf.
  6. One new leaf per debrief or interstitial. Any others queue for the next. A ripening (a fragment filling in, or a caption added) shows as a small mark and does not count toward the one.
  7. The Book only adds. It never removes or changes a word the player has already read.

6 · Battle messages

6.1 · The style rules

  1. When, and for how long. At battle load or briefing, or during the 3-2-1 transition, for three seconds. Never during timed Build or a live Fight.
  2. The draw. Each battlefield has a pool of 5 to 8 messages, and a replay shows a different one. The draw is cosmetic and never touches the battle’s seed. Because of rule 6, any draw gives every player the same information.
  3. Who speaks. Wall-voices: Hale, Voss, Kael and the other True Men, wall-hands, file-leaders, masons. Seren and the Bonded speak rarely. Two further voices: a Trade Council lord who fled to the Keep, whose old pride is turning, and an old Theolith, who takes the long view. The Captain never speaks. There are no admirals, generals or modern ranks, because there was never a navy and the generals were slain.
  4. Length. At most 16 words, in one or two sentences.
  5. Register.
    • The plain speech of their world. Humour comes from character, never from anachronism.
    • No design vocabulary and no mechanic named (no AoR, no piece names, no campaign numbers). “Sortie” is allowed, in soldiers’ mouths only.
    • At most one oath in a pool, and faith is never the butt of a joke.
  6. Hint discipline. If one message in a pool hints at the battle’s carving, every message in that pool carries the same hint in a different voice. Otherwise none of them does. A hint may name a tell that fits the Tide. It never names the counter.
  7. Truth.
    • Never say what the fleet will do.
    • What the fleet remembers lasts one siege only. The wood remembers the wall, not the hand.
    • A Throne line may count groans.
    • Use fear-names only, until a true name has been read.
  8. Flavour.
    • The Tide sets the fleet’s behaviour: one-word mobs, then pairs, then looking, then three roads, then seams, then morrows, then groans.
    • The theater sets the texture: salt, river, fen, canopy, cairn, ember, sand, rime, crag-wind, dark crystal.

7 · Command Time in the fiction

  • Your ruling. The siege clock runs 1 day = 18 real seconds = 18 game-hours, so one game-hour is one second and one sim tick. When the player starts an order in a Fight, time ramps down (it does not snap) to 18 real seconds per game-hour, which is 1/18 speed and feels stopped: “an hour lasts as long as a day”. When the order is committed or cancelled, time dwells a moment at that floor, so a second planned order can follow without racing the ramp, and then it ramps back up. Orders are stamped in sim ticks, so wall-clock speed never changes an outcome. The numbers (ramp-down, dwell, ramp-up) live in the Admiral, §12.
  • How the Book names it. I.3, The Captain’s Long Breath, is the only place it is named, and it is named as something men say, never as a rule: “For the Captain, an hour lasts as long as a day.” Hale’s picture follows the ramp. The hour slows the way a cart slows on a long hill, not the way it stops against a stone, and it lets go the way a held breath goes out.
  • The law, told as character. Kael’s correction states determinism: “It is not the world that slows. It is the Captain who will not hurry. The moment he chose was the moment that came.” The stamp is the tick, not the clock. “A second earlier or later” becomes a deliberate choice, never a reflex. Strategy, not skill.
  • The rules. The Book gives no numbers and names no mechanic. No text of any kind appears during a live Fight.

10 · Waiting on Jack’s call

  1. The sending (VI.1; recommended). The Bonded build the first Stonewright boat from the wreck-wood and send the green sliver home with a Stonwryt coin, kneeling. If you decline it, cut from the RECOMMENDED marker to SENDING END. The leaf then closes on “she was weeping for them both”, and the closing lines stay as they are.
  2. The Bonded’s death (the Epilogue; recommended). They go together in one night and lie in one grave under the new capstone. If you decline it, cut the two opening paragraphs, and the leaf opens at “In the spring”.
  3. “The stone-deaf” (Rhenaer; V.7 Part II; optional). This is the late carvings’ name for the Rivenmen. If you decline it, cut the one marked paragraph.
  4. The Last Carver. If you adopt him, he is the one who cut the Stone out of the Grey. As written, the Epilogue fits either choice.
  5. Proposed names: the ten havens; the Throne griefs (III.2); the emissary Aelrhen; the Mystaeri’s own name Naelaer; the outpost man Brenn; the Bonded’s lines (her forebears laid the gate and his cut it); Seren unbonded.

If you would rather the warnings carried no Shoreland words at all, remove one clause each from IV.1 and IV.2, and revise the Whisper to “The Bonded say they cut their warnings in their own letters, and trusted the stone to carry them.”
  7. Smaller calls. The torn cloak is crimson, and so is every pennant. The Mystaeri live two and a half times as long as the Shorelanders. The UI currency sounds like “Stonewright” when spoken.
  8. Rulings the Book already assumes (each reversible; say the word):
    • The Leviathan is out of the Fall. Your legend had the Leviathan Mystarch bring down the Crystal spire. The Book keeps the spire’s single night but brings the Thrones in last, after the Fall, so the greatest ships come last (the Ender’s Game order) and the Finale keeps its weight. What survives is a rumour, “a shadow in the fog greater than any hull” (III.1), which the wood denies (III.2, Silvaere). To restore the Leviathan, name it in that III.1 paragraph and cut Silvaere’s denial.
    • No Mystaeri sail. It is a ghost fleet, grown and carved, and the landings are made by root-men. The only living exception is the optional Last Carver.
    • Myststone is petrified Mystwood, so “Stone does not translate” and “the Myststone that remembers all” both stand.
    • Where the battles are. The campaigns are fought along the Rivenmen’s line of keeps across the ten haven-lands; the havens themselves are retaken only in the Finale (I.5).
    • Rhyna’s cry is kept whole. She begins it, and Halvard ends it with “Rise! Rise! Rise!”, as in your legend; I.2 insists there was no silence between them, so the two are one cry.
    • The deceits are the Admiral’s. V.7 Part II and Kael’s fifth count (V.5) now tell Come as the young wood came, the Admiral’s first decoy carving (Cam 29), and Straw, then men. The earlier line Show them fear in the east matched none of the Admiral’s forty-six carvings, and a Reading is always a carving that ran. To restore it, the Admiral would first need a carving that shows fear where it is strong.
    • The dead heart’s hour is kept late. Kael’s fourth count now has the hulls keep it a breath late and one sail after another, not “to the very moment”, because the Admiral fires a dead heart’s links three ticks late and a tick apart (its CALL-OUT 39).
    • “But Mystwood remembers.” It now closes Voss’s stone leaf (IV.5), in a Rivenman’s mouth, instead of opening the wood’s leaf that faces it. The wood never uses the Shoreland word “Mystwood” of itself.
  9. Two Mystaeri roots sit too close to Tolkien’s Elvish (flagged, not changed). The lexicon’s orn (“heartwood”) is also the Sindarin word for “tree”, and its sil (“silver”) is the Elvish root for “shine white”. Both are near in meaning as well as sound, which the story bible’s rule zero forbids. They reach Naelorn, Thaelorn, Ralenorn, Vaelsorn, Ornvael and Silvaere. The names are shared with fiction_hooks §9 and the Admiral docs, so the Book keeps them for now. If you want them changed, change the roots in fiction_hooks first, and the Book will follow in one pass.
