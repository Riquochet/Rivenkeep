Design Intent — A Thinking Game

This is the Sudoku of its genre: a thinking game, hard focus, one problem you reason your way through. Not a reflex game, and not a numbers treadmill. The cognitive demand is the point — the rest of this document is the machinery that makes that demand rewarding rather than exhausting.

Real strategy, not a treadmill

The failure this design refuses is the tower-defense treadmill: bigger enemies → build bigger and more cannons → bigger enemies. That is stat inflation in a strategy costume. Here the enemy gets smarter, not tougher, and you beat it by outthinking it — by tricking a thinking opponent, not out-scaling a dumb one. Difficulty is Cognition, never hit points; the entire model exists to make that literally true.

The loop is the debugging loop

The core loop is the one every engineer knows: write a little, watch it run, it fails, read why, fix it, run again. Deploy is where you write the plan; the Fight is where you watch it run; the next Deploy is where you fix it. The Fight is for monitoring and adapting — reading what the fleet does and timing your fire-control; the structural moves (re-tasking an AoR) land in the next Deploy, not mid-Fight. You are not fighting at twitch speed — you are iterating on a system. This loop is why determinism is load-bearing: you cannot debug a system where the same input gives a different result. Determinism is what makes the Fight a diagnosis instead of a coin-flip.

The load is the point — but only the right kind

A thinking game should demand hard focus. But there are two kinds of load, and only one is the game:

  • Reasoning load (Sudoku, debugging) — the effort is in your thinking: hypothesis, feedback, refinement. This is the game.
  • Bookkeeping load (a cargo manifest) — the effort is clerical tracking: holding a hundred cells and ledger entries in your head. This is the failure.

The whole discipline is keeping the load on the first side, and three things do it: (1) the three channels live at three fidelities — a soft haze (fear), a few hard glints (contact), and live vision — so you reason about shapes, not cells; the fidelities summarize so you never hold a spreadsheet. (2) The Fight must be diagnostic — legible enough that you see why a plan failed, so the loop stays hypothesis→test→refine (reasoning) and never memorize-everything (bookkeeping). (3) Structural action is deferred to Deploy, so the Fight is read and Deploy is act — never both at once. If the game ever feels like a spreadsheet, that is bad load leaking in, and the fix is always more summarizing and clearer diagnosis — never more to track.

Why this document exists before a single ship

Ships are the most complicated part of the game, and everything else fails if the fleet's mind is wrong — so we proved the mind first, with real game theory, before designing one ship. We already built 16 ships once, in the GDD, on an unproven foundation, and threw them all away. Not twice. This is the foundation, deliberately ahead of the roster; the roster comes after it is solid, and we expect the roster to stress-test and edit this document. That is the foundation doing its job — not a sign it was incomplete.

What A Ship Is

A ship is not a scripted mover and not an omniscient one. It is a deterministic policy that acts on what it has perceived — nothing more. Everything in this document follows from that one sentence.

Four decisions from the design conversation, and the single model they collapse into:

The four decisions → one architecture
  • Blindness. Ships and troops are as blind to the defender as the defender is to them. They never read the true cannon roster, AoR radii, loaded domains, HP, or fire-control stances. — This is a restriction on the input.
  • Determinism. Play the same battle the same way and the fleet reacts identically, shot for shot. — This makes the policy a pure function.
  • Learning. The fleet gets smarter inside a battle through contact, and carries that knowledge across sorties within the battle. — This is the state the function reads.
  • Counter-web. The roster exists to make the defender's levers matter, and grows in many directions as the player descends. — This is what the lineages are for.
The collapse

Fleet behavior = a pure deterministic function of (seed + that unit's observation history). No wall-clock, no unseeded randomness, no peek at ground truth. Blindness restricts the input; determinism makes it a pure function; learning is the accumulated observation state. Three of the four decisions are the same decision viewed from three sides — and the fourth (counter-web) is delivered by the lineage matrix that rides on top.

Legibility is a hard constraint (fairness)

Every hidden-information mechanic in this model must be one the player can reason about. The fleet's blindness and its knowledge are governed by physical, visible rules — line of sight, terrain occluders, distance, walls — never by opaque bookkeeping. A bait only counts if the player could have predicted it would work. If a mechanic ever makes the fleet look dumb rather than blind, the fix is always to make the reason for its blindness visible — never to let the fleet cheat.

The inversion — and why it is the game's soul

The player and the fleet both learn, on opposite clocks:

Who learns when
  • The player learns across loops. Each replay of a battle, the human folds last attempt's crises into a better plan. Knowledge is the only thing that carries between replays.
  • The fleet learns within a battle, and forgets between battles. Inside one battle it sharpens sortie over sortie (the battle is the loop — see The Battle Is The Loop). On the player's replay, the seed resets and the fleet forgets everything it "knew" — because if the puzzle shifted under the player between attempts, knowledge would stop being the thing that carries.

That is Edge of Tomorrow exactly: Cage remembers across resets; the enemy fights adaptively inside each reset and starts fresh next loop. The four decisions, followed through honestly, land the design directly on its own north-star touchstone. Nobody set out to build that — it fell out of the constraints.

The Battle Is The Loop — Sorties & The Tension Ramp

A battle is the loop. It is long — potentially an hour — so it is broken into sorties: Build → Deploy → Fight cycles the player can pick up and put down without committing to one marathon session. The sortie is a pacing device, not a reset; the roster and its deployment can end a sortie early, but the tension is meant to build across the whole battle, and the battle's end is the respite — the celebration.

The persistence boundary
  • Across sorties, within a battle — everything persists: unsunk ships remain (carrying their damage), cannon health / veterans, walls (damaged), boss HP, and the fleet's memory — the Contact ledger and the Effect fear-grid (line of sight is live, so it re-acquires each Fight).
  • Troops are the exception — cleared at every sortie end. They are the ephemeral wave, redeployed fresh from surviving transports.
  • The battle is the only reset. On the player's replay of a battle the seed resets and the fleet forgets. A won battle simply ends — the celebration is the release.
Memory outlives its gatherers — but the network degrades

Stored knowledge is fleet-wide: what has propagated survives the units that gathered it, and clearing troops or losing hulls doesn't wipe the shared fear map or the contact ledger — next sortie's fresh hulls inherit it. But the fleet's living intelligence is a network, and sinking ships degrades it three ways, all emergent and all intuitive: (1) you lose whatever the sunk ship had observed but not yet shared — propagation is slow, so kill the knower before it radios; (2) you blind the sector it was watching, so you can rearrange your fort there unseen until the fleet regains eyes; (3) fewer hulls means a thinner net that spreads and refreshes everything slower. So old facts persist, but new facts come slower and staler the more you sink — and sinking the observer (a scout) hurts most. You can't erase the fleet's mind, but you can starve and blind it.

Assessed & set aside — full hive-mind memory loss

Considered: a hard hive mind where sinking a ship erases fleet memory. Recommendation against, three reasons. (1) It cannibalizes the signaling game — if erasing by sinking is reliable, players skip the subtle baiting that is the whole point; erase beats mislead. (2) It inverts the tension ramp — attrition would make the fleet dumber across the battle, flattening the climb to the climax. (3) Legibility / fairness — the player can't predict what a sink erases unless the memory-to-ship map is visible, and a hive that forgets everything on one loss is too fragile to be a fair adversary. The soft hive mind above already delivers the part worth having ("they think my gun is where it was, but I moved it" is a stale belief, not erased memory). The adopted alternative is the Flagship (next): coordination-decapitation, not knowledge-erasure.

The Flagship — the coordination hub (adopted)

The fleet's top propagation node: a single, visually unmistakable command ship, screened in the back line. While it lives, the network coordinates fast — fear and contact spread quickly and the next-sortie plan is tightly synchronized. Sink it and coordination collapses: propagation reverts to slow and local, and the fleet can no longer spread new information or act as one plan. Critically, it keeps everything it has learned — the ramp holds; stored knowledge is untouched — so this is a coordination lever, not a knowledge switch. The payoff: a decapitated fleet is more baitable (stale, divergent local beliefs), so killing the flagship rewards the signaling skill rather than replacing it — the exact opposite of the rejected hard-hive version. It is a legible, hard-won decapitation play (you must fight through the screen), it gives the back-line-targeting cannon personality its reason to exist, and it makes the fleet protect its flagship (screening behaviour). The coordination collapse must read on screen — the fleet visibly fragments and slows.

The tension ramp — the fleet is dumbest in sortie 1 and smartest at the climax

This is the engine that makes a battle build. Sortie 1: the fleet walks in with empty maps — naive, easily baited, blundering into your cores. By the final sortie it holds a rich fear gradient and a full contact ledger — it knows your fort, has found your breaches, fears your kill-zones. Difficulty within a single battle escalates purely through accumulated cognition.

The same curve at two scales — the design's recurring signature

The intra-battle ramp (memory accumulating sortie over sortie) is the same "smarter fleet → harder" curve as the campaign-scale Cognition axis (lineages evolving across 190 boards). One principle expressed at two scales: the fleet gets smarter within a battle and across the game. Coverage-vs-concentration recurs down the layers; so does this.

Partial decay — so the ramp is a ratchet, not a sawtooth

Fear cools a notch at each sortie boundary (the "lost sight of the threat" gap), which is what keeps baiting alive — a bluff can fade, so a flank becomes reusable. But decay is partial and slow relative to the battle, so net fear ratchets upward as the battle wears on; the map trends hotter. Deliberate silence still cools one specific region (the bait); the whole map does not reset. Decay rate is the Cognition knob — forgetful early fleets, stubborn late ones.

Build/Deploy is simultaneous planning — both minds think at once

The between-sortie phase is not just the player's planning time; it is the fleet's. While you build and deploy, the fleet consolidates memory, propagates across the network, and computes its next-sortie plan — deterministically, from its (last-observed, possibly stale) knowledge. Neither side sees the other's moves before the Fight. The Fight is the resolution of two committed plans. This is a thinking-game structure (plan → commit → resolve) and a performance win — the heavy AI compute runs in the low-intensity phase, off the real-time path (which matters on mobile). Guardrail: the plan must stay legible (see the Legibility law) — the player has to anticipate what the fleet will do with its thinking time, or the puzzle breaks.

…which makes Build/Deploy the bait window

Because both sides plan blind and the fleet plans from last-observed state, this is where you invalidate its plan: repair the breach it intends to pour through, relocate the exposed gun it is hunting, wall in a veteran to turn it back into a ghost. The fleet opens the next Fight executing a plan built against a fort you have already changed — and corrects only on fresh observation (and later still, if the sightline is blocked). Baiting, moved up to the planning layer.

The Fight is mostly holding fire — and holding fire is a memory move

Plan-primary means the fight verbs are deliberately expensive (their costs live in the Cannon & Tower doc): re-tasking an AoR and switching a domain are costly recovery tools, so the plan dominates — yet a poor plan is not an automatic loss, because those verbs are a paid safety valve. The verb the commander actually leans on is fire-control: mostly holding, releasing at the right moment. And holding is not passive — a silent battery stays off the fleet's fear-grid, so the fleet routes as if that ground is safe. The primary live action is therefore a belief move: withhold the signal to keep the fleet mistaken, then spend it — reveal + alpha — the instant a valuable target has committed into your core. "Hold until the right moment" = manage the fleet's fear in real time, then cash it in. The Fight is for monitoring and adapting; the structural fix — re-tasking an AoR — lands in the next Deploy, which is the loop's "fix" step (see Design Intent). (Verb costs and the fire-control mechanics themselves are the Cannon & Tower doc's to specify; this is only the memory-side consequence.) The full lived experience of this — the sortie's tempo, the assembled grid-read, and reading the ending — is the Fight doc's.

How a battle ends — Resolve & the castle win

The number of sorties is not fixed. A battle runs until the fleet resolves — and the player's whole job is to keep the required castles alive until it does. Castles surviving to the fleet's resolution is the win.

Resolve is another manipulable belief

The fleet carries a resolve — its will to keep fighting (the morale/rout concept). It is computed from the fleet's perceptions, not ground truth: its own losses, its progress (contact — breaches made, distance to the castles), and the danger it meets (the fear grid). So resolve is another belief the player manipulates — the signaling game pointed at morale. "The ships think they are losing" is exactly that: a belief that can be true, or bluffed. Mechanically, resolve is a per-ship network — each hull carries its own, pulled by peer contagion and a flag anchor broadcast; this document owns the belief/perception framing, while the full network model, the tell, and the flagship spec are authoritative in Ships §4.

Three ways a battle ends — battle-level
  • Retreat — resolve collapses; the fleet withdraws. Win, if the castles held. (It "thinks" it is losing — whether or not it truly was.)
  • Annihilation — a committed fleet is ground to the last ship. Win, if the castles held. ("Surviving the onslaught is enough.")
  • Breakthrough — a required castle is destroyed before either. Loss.

These are the battle-level ends — not the sortie-level three (Held / Repel / Breakthrough) in the Victory doc. A sortie Repel is one wave\'s rout-cascade (it flees to reload, banking time); battle-level Retreat is the whole fleet quitting once its network can no longer field a wave that holds. Repel is the currency of break-their-will — each one ratchets the fleet toward Retreat.

Two strategic paths fall straight out, and the player reads the fleet's resolve (a behavioural tell, like the fear tells) to choose between them:

Break their will vs grind them out
  • Break their will — convince the fleet it is losing so it retreats early: sink ships visibly, keep castles unreachable, punish pushes hard, project a scalding defense. A psychological win — they give up though they might have won. Rewards efficient, punishing defense and the deception skill.
  • Grind them out — a committed or unbluffable fleet can't be broken; you must survive until it is annihilated. Pure endurance — hold the castles through the full onslaught. You can even feign weakness to keep an aggressive fleet overcommitted so it grinds itself down (risky — it might actually break through).
The stalemate valve — why "surviving is enough" works, and why battles always end

A fleet making no progress loses heart: resolve decays under stalemate. So a purely impregnable turtle still wins — by outlasting the fleet's will, not by sinking a thing. This is the mechanism behind "sometimes just surviving is enough," and it also guarantees battles terminate: a finite fleet, attrited each sortie and bleeding resolve when stalled, must eventually break through, break morale, or run out of hulls. No infinite stalemate.

Resolve is an intensity dial on the flag — set per battle

The spread of resolve keeps both experiences alive. Skittish fleets break easily (satisfying early bluff-wins); fanatical fleets and bosses are near-unbreakable — they fight to the last ship, so the pure survival test always exists and the player can't just bluff a cheap retreat every battle. It is one dial — the flag's anchor-broadcast strength — and a strategy-selector, not a power knob (fanatical fleets ignore the fear grid and drive into your kill-zones: easy to slaughter, impossible to bluff). Killing the flagship craters resolve and forces a field promotion — a survivor inherits the flag with no buffs and the fleet takes on its personality, so decapitation is a succession you manage, not a single kill. Full model authoritative in Ships §4.

Precedent — the rout, made manipulable

This is the Total War morale model: units break and flee when they believe the battle is lost, a general's death craters morale (the flagship), and you win far more often by breaking the enemy's will than by killing every soldier — which is also how real battles actually ended (the rout, not the massacre). The one new turn is making that morale a belief the player manipulates, which is just this game's signaling core reaching the battle-resolution layer.

The honest tuning risks

This model is only as good as its numbers, now mostly sanding magnitudes: legibility — RESOLVED (the definitive tell is the motion signature in Ships §4 — lateral = fear, fore-aft = nerve; no morale bar); balance — if bluffing retreat is too easy the grind path is vestigial, too hard and "surviving is enough" never fires; pacing — a fleet that breaks too early feels anticlimactic, too late feels grindy. The resolve spread across lineages is the lever, and getting it right is real work, not a given. Seam: the belief-and-manipulation nature of resolve is this document's; the win-condition specifics — castle degradation, which castles are "required," enclosure as the means of protection — are owned by the Victory doc (integrated there, not forked here). This also evolves the old "enclose to win" into "enclose to protect, survive with them intact" — keep the idea, refine the definition.

1The Genre Is Signaling, Not Shooting

Name the structure and the rest of the design becomes derivable rather than a matter of taste. This is a deterministic signaling game:

  • The player is the sender — every cannon that fires (or holds) is a signal.
  • The fleet is the receiver — it updates a belief about the board from those signals and acts on the posterior.

So the player is not fighting the fleet. The player is shaping the fleet's beliefs about the board. Baiting is textbook deception: feed evidence that pushes the receiver's posterior away from the truth, in a direction you exploit.

Why determinism is a requirement, not a preference

You can only deceive an opponent whose inference rule you can predict. A random fleet cannot be baited — there is nothing to model, so there is no skill in feinting at it. Deception is only well-defined against a stable, learnable update rule. Poker bluffing collapses against a pure-random caller; it works because opponents update on bets predictably. The same law holds here: determinism is the precondition for the entire endgame skill to exist — replay-fairness is a bonus, not the reason.

Precedent — telegraphing is depth, not easy-mode

Into the Breach telegraphs exact enemy intent a turn ahead and is fully deterministic; it is celebrated as a thinking game, not a shallow one. Telegraphing doesn't reduce difficulty — it relocates it from execution and luck to reasoning. (Honest limit: ItB has no hidden state and no learning, so it validates determinism-as-depth, not our belief layer — that is what the stealth precedents below are for.)

Why this does not degenerate into Pac-Man

The stock objection: a deterministic, learnable opponent, once solved, is solved forever — Pac-Man's ghosts collapse into memorized routes. That happens to Pac-Man because it has one tiny maze and a four-direction action space: a single optimal line exists to memorize. Rivenkeep does not degenerate because the player's decision space per board is combinatorially vast — 8 archetypes × 3 domains, placement across a large board, group/AoR shapes, wall layouts, domain loadouts, and the whole bait-and-timing layer. There is no single "the solution" to route around; there is a space of viable strategies, and the deterministic fleet is a responsive opponent inside it. Honest distinction: a perfectly copied playthrough of one board does replay identically — but that is reproducibility (the feature that powers replays, leaderboards, and the very possibility of baiting), not degeneracy (every player converging on one trivial memorized line). The branching factor prevents the latter. 190 boards and the Cognition axis are reinforcement on top, not the primary defense.

2Three Channels — The Fleet's Entire Mind

The fleet learns three different kinds of thing, with different properties. Keeping them separate is what makes the model both fair and legible — and it maps cleanly onto how a fog-of-war RTS separates live vision, explored-and-remembered ground, and danger you can only infer.

ChannelSourceNatureLocalizes?Persists?Answers
Line of sightWhat a unit can see now — gated by occluders (trees, rocks, cliffs, walls) and distanceLive, current visionYes, while openNo — live only"What can I see right now?"
ContactWritten when LOS, a connecting shot, or a troop touch confirms somethingHard, confirmed factYes — until contradictedYes"What is there, and where?"
EffectIncoming fire the unit survives — a coarse bearing/area per shellNo — general area onlyYes, decayingYes"Where does it hurt?"
LOS is the sensor; Contact is the memory it writes

Vision and memory are different things. Line of sight is live — a unit sees a grid only while the sightline is open, and loses the view the instant it closes. Contact is what that vision leaves behind — once LOS (or a landed shot, or a troop) confirms a wall / castle / gun, the fact persists even after the unit can no longer see it (the "ghost building"), revised only by a contradicting observation. Effect is separate from both — never seen, only felt, inferred from being hit. So LOS feeds Contact; Effect stands alone.

The asymmetry law

The fleet sees and confirms what it faces; it only estimates what hits it. Walls, castles, and exposed cannons get found — via line of sight, a landed shot, or a troop touch. AoRs and enclosed cannons get guessed — they are the source of fire, occluded from sight, and a source is non-identifiable (see Occlusion). What it can reach is knowable; what reaches it is guessed.

Breaching reveals the next layer

The fort is concentric and walls block sight, so the fleet sees only the wall it is facing. Break through, and line of sight opens to the next wall behind it — now confirmable. A deep fort reveals itself progressively, layer by layer, as it is breached; the inner sanctum stays invisible until the fleet is inside the outer one. Concealment is layered by construction — a gun behind two intact walls is doubly a ghost.

This split does three jobs. LOS + Contact give the fleet enough hard information to drive at an objective (the anti-wander guarantee); Effect keeps the defender's guns behind a fog that survives arbitrary sampling; the layered LOS gate keeps your interior hidden until it is breached. The cheating fleet and the flailing fleet are both closed by the same structure.

Precedent — the RTS fog rule, three-for-three

Every fog-of-war RTS separates exactly these three: the lit vision around your units (LOS), the greyed-out explored map that remembers seen terrain and buildings after you leave (Contact), and the enemy activity you infer from pressure and contact (Effect). Shipped for thirty years. Kill the scout and the lit area shrinks, the greyed map goes stale, and you fall back to inference.

3The Effect Channel — The Fear Gradient

The fleet's read of the defender is emotional, not analytical. It does not hold "an AoR of radius 6 centred near grid 20,14." It holds "the north shore is dangerous, the south feels open." A fear map, not an intel map.

How the fear map is built

Recorded on a medium-resolution grid — about 10×10 fear cells over the play area (not per-cell, not blobby regions): the location of incoming hits, weighted by how often a unit crossing a cell is hit and at what percentage. Each incoming shell gives the fleet a general direction and area it came from — never a point — and those accumulate into the grid. Constant, high-% hits → scalding. Occasional, low-% → warm. Never hit → cold. Formally this is a Monte-Carlo estimate of the defender's aggregate falloff field — the fleet is sampling your coverage by walking through it and tallying how often it bleeds. Overlapping AoRs smear together into one field; the fleet never resolves the individual circles.

Named tiers, not a number — the fear map is a stealth alert level

The single most important framing decision: build the fear map as a stealth alert system, never as an exposed heat value. The stealth genre solved "make a hidden AI belief legible" twenty-five years ago and shipped it repeatedly.

Region tierFleet reads it asBehavioral tell (nerve vs confidence)
COLD"Open water. Go."Straight, confident approach; delivery commits here.
WARM"Something's out there."Hesitant, skirting; hugs the region's edge, probes.
HOT"That flank kills."Actively avoids; routes delivery around it.

Precedent — the stealth alert-state playbook

Metal Gear's Normal / Caution / Alert; Thief's light gem; the "?" / "!" over a guard. Three moves make a hidden belief readable: (1) quantize into a few named tiers, never a raw number; (2) give each tier an unmistakable behavioral + audiovisual tell; (3) animate the decay — the guard walks to your last-known position, finds nothing, gives up, returns to patrol. Map straight on: a region's fear tier is an alert level; the fleet committing into stale-soft ground is the guard walking to last-known-position; per-sortie decay is the search-then-give-up.

Decay

Fear fades between sorties if a region stops hurting. A flank that terrified the fleet last sortie, left un-punished, cools — and the fleet edges back in. Decay rate is a Cognition-axis knob (fast-forgetting early fleets, stubborn late ones). Crucially, decay is shown as behavior, never as a bar (see Commander UI).

Why ~10×10 is the right resolution — on theory, not taste

A ~10×10 fear grid is simultaneously the more legible choice (a readable heat overlay — "they're avoiding the northeast near the point," which per-cell noise and blobby regions both fail to convey), the more exploitable choice (a cell can be baited), and the more tunable choice. It is also what defeats counter-battery localization: a cell spans many possible gun positions, so a fear field at this resolution cannot be inverted to a gun. Medium-coarse-but-visible is the stealth sweet spot, chosen deliberately, not a compromise.

The grid stays 10×10 as the board grows — and that helps new ·

The board is being resized upward (128×80 derived, Victory §3), and the fear grid does not scale with it. It stays about 10×10 over the play area, so each cell grows from roughly 6×5 grids to roughly 13×8 grids.

That is an improvement on all three grounds this resolution was chosen for. A larger cell spans more possible gun positions, so the field is even harder to invert into a counter-battery fix. It reads more clearly as a heat shape at overview zoom, where a cell becomes a substantial patch rather than a smudge. And it is more baitable, because one silent battery now shapes a bigger region of the fleet's belief — which makes hold-fire a stronger information weapon rather than a weaker one.

It also turned out to be one of the requirements that sized the board. For fear navigation to be visible at all — a hull skirting a hot cell, probing a cooling one, committing through a cold one — a contact must cross two or three cells on its approach. At ~13 grids per cell that is 26–38 grids of approach depth, which independently reproduced the figure the player-side band arithmetic gave. The fleet's mind and the player's reading time wanted the same board.

4The Contact Channel — Confirmed Targets

The anti-wander guarantee. Without it, a perfectly-fogged fleet just mills around — and "the enemy flails" is as dead as "the enemy cheats." The fix is a hard-fact channel running alongside the soft fear map.

What confirms a target
  • A ship's shot connects with a wall, cannon, or castle → that structure's location is a confirmed fact, not a guess.
  • A troop ashore reaches and strikes a structure → same. Troops ashore are close-recon: they see local ground truth and feed it in.
  • Confirmed facts persist into the next sortie as hard map annotations. The fleet walks in already knowing the perimeter geometry it touched — no re-scouting the walls it already breached.
Contact never fades — it is revised, not decayed

A confirmed fact is a permanent per-grid ledger of last-observed truth; it does not lose confidence on a timer. It is overwritten only by a contradicting observation — a later shot that lands on a grid the fleet believed was walled and finds it empty updates the entry to empty. No fuzzy decay parameter, and fully deterministic. Two consequences fall out:

  • Stale positives (ghost structures). Abandon a wall — let it break and don't rebuild — and the fleet keeps shelling the ghost until a shot re-observes the gap.
  • Stale negatives (the hidden breach) — the stronger bait. Remove or relocate a wall the fleet confirmed, and it still believes that grid is blocked: troops path around an opening that is actually clear, and ships don't exploit it, until the fleet re-observes. You can carry an open breach the fleet is blind to — the contact-channel twin of the hold-feint.

Line of sight (channel 3) writes contact, and sight is physical. A ship confirms or refreshes a grid it can see. Line of sight is broken by trees, rocks, cliffs, and walls, and capped by distance — each lineage has a sight range; a piece too far to make out isn't confirmed even in the clear. Fog is one more occluder on top. So the hidden breach is not fog-only: it lives anywhere the sightline is broken — behind terrain, behind a wall, far to the rear, or in fog. Fairness (see the Legibility law): the player can always see why a piece is or isn't visible to the fleet, because every occluder is a thing on the board. Sight range is a Cognition-axis knob — short-sighted early fleets are easy to keep blind; long-sighted late fleets (and the recon lineage) are hard to hide from.

This deepens the loop rather than easing it. The fleet remembering the breach it found last sortie and massing there is your overcommit-punish setup — you quietly reinforce the remembered breach into a kill-box (see Baiting).

Searching fire — spending a reveal to buy a contact

Some units convert fog into facts on purpose: they fire semi-randomly to probe for walls, castles, and exposed guns. Two rules keep this honest:

Rules for searching fire
  • "Semi-random" means seeded, not random. A deterministic search pattern, weighted by the current fear gradient — the fleet probes where it suspects a soft breach, not uniformly. Same battle → same probes. (Unseeded randomness here would break determinism, which breaks baiting, which breaks the game.)
  • It must cost. A unit that fires to search reveals itself (your own muzzle-flash rule, pointed back at it) and burns a reload. So probing is a real gamble: aggressive lineages probe; cautious ones wait for scouts. Searching fire is a Cognition-axis behavior, not a universal one.

5Enclosure Occludes The Flash

The keystone that resolves "static cannons never move, so won't the fleet eventually fix them?" — the counter-battery-radar problem. The answer: the fleet maps where it gets hurt (effect), never where the gun is (cause), and a cannon's channel membership is decided by how you place it.

The occlusion rule
  • Enclosed cannon — its muzzle flash is hidden behind its own walls. The fleet sees the shell arc down but never sees the origin light up. It is a pure source, so it only ever feeds the effect channel. Permanently a ghost — felt, never found.
  • Exposed cannon (a Lone Wolf shoved forward for reach; an un-walled gun) — its flash is visible, so it can be fixed exactly as a wall is fixed, and it crosses onto the contact channel: once seen it is a confirmed target that persists across sorties and can be hunted and baited.

The three locks — why "static" doesn't leak

  1. Broken sightline (mechanical, the hard lock). A wall blocks line of sight — to the muzzle flash, to the gun itself, and to anything behind it. Enclosure breaks every sightline into the interior, so the fleet gets neither a flash-bearing nor a visual fix on an enclosed gun. (The shells still lob up and over — the fleet feels them as incoming fire; it just can't trace them home.) Flash-occlusion and visual-occlusion are one rule: walls stop sightlines. This also makes walls a deliberate concealment tool — screen a gun, a relocation, or a breach by putting a wall between it and the fleet's eyes.
  2. Disc, not point (structural). A hit proves only that some AoR reaches this spot — and an AoR is a large circle, so it localizes to a whole disc of possible gun positions, never a barrel. The inverse problem is non-identifiable: many cannon layouts produce the identical hurt-map. Bearing is available but coarse by design: a shell gives the fleet a general direction and area — feeding the ~10×10 fear grid — never a point, so even perfect bearing math lands in a cell spanning many gun positions. And an enclosed gun lobs its shell up and over, so the bearing points at the wall, not the gun behind it. Coarse-by-design is the feature, not a leak.
  3. The AoR is fluid though the gun is fixed (the actual answer). The fleet was never chasing the cannon — it chases the danger, and danger geography is the AoR, which you move live (re-task drag), hide (hold-fire — the group never enters the map), or relocate (Deploy, at a nurture cost). The bolted-down object is simply not the thing the fleet is trying to find.
Enclosure now pays a fourth time — and placement becomes a two-axis gamble

Enclosure already paid three ways: protection, territory/victory, and veteran nurture. Now it pays a fourth: concealment. And the placement decision the player reads live becomes two axes crossed — coverage vs concealment (push a gun forward for reach, or wall it in for invisibility) × the veteran you're risking (expose your 200% gun and it becomes a named target the fleet remembers across sorties; enclose it and it's a phantom that still bleeds them). Exposure carries a persistent cost, not a per-sortie one.

6Baiting — Play The Model, Not The Fleet

The endgame skill, and the reason the whole architecture exists: memory is a trap the player springs. Because the fleet acts on a belief about the board, and beliefs go stale, the deep move is to poison what the fleet thinks it knows.

Three trap patterns (all deterministic, all readable)
  • Bait-and-relocate. Fire from a spot, then move the AoR (or the gun in Deploy). The fear map still reads that region HOT and cools only slowly, so the fleet routes into your relocated guns. It can't tell "the gun moved" from "the gun went silent" — a spatial field can't distinguish them, and that ambiguity is the exploit.
  • Hold-feint. A battery that held fire last sortie never entered the fear map — that ground reads COLD. Route delivery there, then spring the alpha. Withholding a signal is itself a move; the receiver updates on absence.
  • Overcommit-punish. The contact channel remembers a breach; the fleet masses delivery on it next sortie. You've quietly reinforced it into a kill-box.
  • Hidden breach. Because contact is revised only by contradiction (never decayed), a wall you remove or relocate stays "walled" in the fleet's ledger — so it won't path troops through the opening or exploit it, until it re-observes the gap. Open a breach the fleet is blind to; in fog it may never find it (see Contact).
Three systems, retro-justified

This single skill gives independent reasons-to-exist to three verbs that were otherwise "nice to have": the cannon-move verb (relocate to bait — you asked for exactly this), hold-fire (silence keeps a battery off the fleet's map — so it hides the gun and wins the crazy-Ivan alpha window), and enclosure (you protect the veteran you're about to bait with). Fire-control becomes information warfare on both sides, which is why it is the strongest of the three Fight verbs.

Precedent — this is the stealth "search the last-known-position" loop, and poker slow-play

The guard walking to where you were while you slip behind him is bait-and-relocate. Alien: Isolation makes an entire game of luring the creature away from your last-known area, and it reads as tense and fair. Checking a monster hand to keep an opponent's model of you wrong (slow-play) is hold-feint — a canonical strategic tradition, not a bolt-on.

7Propagation — One Fear Map, Spreading Slowly

Decision: the fear map is fleet-wide with slow propagation — one shared belief, not per-ship islands. It reads cleanly as a single overlay, makes information a contested resource, and creates its own baitable texture.

How knowledge moves
  • Contacts and fear flow ship→ship and troop→troop on a lag — reuse the troop "command-confusion" batching (more units → slower coordination), the same signature one layer down.
  • Scouts are the premium accelerant — wide vision, fast propagation into the shared map. Kill the scout and the flank re-blinds: fear stops spreading there and stale beliefs linger. (Straight StarCraft: the info-gatherer is a priority kill.)
  • The Flagship is the network's top node — while it lives, propagation is fast and coordinated; sink it and the whole network reverts to slow and local, though nothing already learned is lost (see The Battle Is The Loop).
  • Troops ashore are close-recon, feeding hard ground truth into the contact channel from inside the perimeter.
  • Propagation lag is itself a texture: "the north's fear hasn't reached the freshly-arrived southern ships yet" — a real, legible, baitable seam.
The population this layer needs — and why it is available from Campaign 1 corrected ·

Propagation, contagion, batching and the rout cascade are statistical mechanics: they need bodies to run through. A cascade across three hulls is not a cascade, and an intensity dial with two hulls to act on degenerates into a per-hull toughness stat, which is exactly what it was designed not to be. The layer wants roughly 6 hulls to function and 8 or more to read well — the specified feel is a wave visibly unravelling over ~2–3 s (Ships §4), and that needs enough hulls for the unravelling to be sequential rather than a single step.

That population is available immediately, and the reason is the contact/hull distinction. The fleet-sizing model (Victory §3) caps the player's attention in contacts, not hulls, and a formation holding shape is one contact whatever its hull count. So an opening battle can field 2 contacts made of 3–4 hulls each — six to eight bodies for the network, two objects for the eye. The wave is trivially legible and the resolve layer is fully alive, because the coherence that makes it easy to read is the same coherence that gives fear and panic somewhere to spread.

So resolve is online from Campaign 1, at full strength, and it needs no staging. What it does constrain is early formation authoring: opening waves must arrive in coherent formations rather than as lone hulls. A pair of unescorted singletons is both a thinner read and a dead resolve network — worse on every axis, and the kind of "simple" that is really just empty. Loose, sloppy, easily-shredded clumps are the right early shape (that looseness is also the Cognition tell — a shallow fleet holds its shape badly), not sparse ones.

This box corrects an earlier pass which computed this floor in hulls, compared it against a contact count, and concluded the resolve layer could not come online until the mid-War. Those are two different quantities and the conclusion was wrong. Nothing about the resolve design changes; only the schedule claim, which is withdrawn.

RESOLVE IS QUEUE-WIDE, not per-sortie — and PURPOSE is one of its inputs new ·

Two rulings from Jack extend the resolve network beyond the hulls currently on the board, and they change what the player is actually working on.

1 · The network spans the whole queue

"This is all about the resolve. The resolve is gone, no ships will continue to fight — BUT that is for the whole queue of ships, not just one sortie."

So resolve is not a property of the wave in front of you. It is a property of the fleet, reserves included. A rout cascade in sortie three lowers the baseline of hulls that have not sailed yet; an anchor killed in sortie two is an anchor the reserves no longer have. The consequences are worth being explicit about because they reframe the player's job:

  • Resolve damage accumulates across the battle rather than resetting each sortie. It is the one quantity the player builds up over the whole engagement, which makes it the closest thing in the game to a progress bar — and, correctly, one that is never shown.
  • Breaking their will is a campaign against the fleet, not a trick per wave. Repels are the deposits; the fleet Retreat (Victory §4a) is the withdrawal. That is why repels were always called the currency.
  • Reserves can be demoralised before they ever arrive. A late-battle formation drawn from a battered queue comes out already shaky, which is the fading fleet expressed in nerve rather than in hull count.

2 · Purpose holds resolve up — and a hull without purpose has nothing holding it

Resolve was previously fed by own-experience, peer contagion and the anchor broadcast. Jack's carrier example adds a fourth and more structural input: a hull needs something to be for. Sink every carrier and the escorts are not merely frightened — they have no objective, so there is nothing for their resolve to rest on, and it collapses queue-wide (Victory §4a; the core/screen structure and the coreless residual are in Ships §9).

This is the best thing that has happened to the resolve layer, for two reasons. It gives resolve a cause the player can attack directly rather than only a set of pressures to apply — you can reason about what the fleet is for and remove it, which is a strategic act rather than an accumulation of chip damage. And it means resolve is not purely psychological bookkeeping: it has a structural spine that the player can see (which hulls are cores, which are screens, and which formations still have one).

Reading it, unchanged in kind: still no bar, still the motion signature (lateral = fear, fore-aft = nerve). What purpose collapse adds is a distinctive read at the top end — a coreless residual moves like hulls with nowhere to be, and that is a shape the player learns to recognise as the battle's end approaching.

3 · Purpose is removed, never inverted v2.4.0

The obvious temptation, once purpose is a resolve input, is to let its removal spike resolve — the “out for revenge” reading Jack flagged. That temptation is refused, and the effect is kept anyway. Making one input both hold resolve up and spike it on removal is a sign inversion on a single term: it would need its own state, its own tell, its own teaching moment, and it would make the resolve layer arguable at the edges for the first time.

Instead, revenge is a behaviour swap, not a morale event (owned by Ships §9). When a formation's last core dies, its surviving screens stop escorting and start shooting — they drop station-keeping and drive forward. Resolve keeps falling the whole time. So the board gets more dangerous while the fleet gets closer to breaking, which is both halves of Jack's ruling with no contradiction and no new machinery.

It reads through the existing signature unchanged. Hulls no longer holding station on a payload develop a forward net motion — the Committed read, which the player already knows as the fanatical tell. Same signature, different cause, and it feels like fanaticism without being it. It is also transient by construction rather than by timer: it ends at reconstitution (absorbed into a formation with empty screen slots → resumes screening) or at rout (resolve was already sagging when it started).

The orphan fork — and why resolve is the right arbiter

Jack ruled that orphaned screens might turn wolfpack or might retreat, having lost their purpose, and asked for those to be balanced. They balance themselves, because the resolve network already holds the deciding quantity:

  • Resolve above its rout threshold when the core dies → turns wolfpack. Fresh hulls, live anchor, neighbours steady.
  • Resolve below it → breaks off and retreats. Worn down, anchor dead, neighbours already wavering.

So the player's own prior pressure decides which one they get, deterministically and with no weight to tune. A clean early decapitation on an untouched formation buys an angry wolfpack; the same kill on a formation already ground down buys a retreat. A surgical kill is more dangerous than a worn-down one — which inverts the usual expectation, costs nothing, and finally gives the grind line a payoff of its own instead of being strictly the worse route.

A FOURTH REGISTER — escort tightness reads the fleet's confidence in its own map new ·

Ruling (Jack): “An escort screen maybe loose for unknown waters, but tighten up when it thinks it knows the player's AoR.”

This is a genuinely new read of the fleet's mind, and it belongs to this doc because it is a belief display rather than a formation detail. The Contact and Effect channels were previously legible only through the fear-haze and the exposure glints. Now they are also legible in the shape of the escort:

What you seeWhat the fleet believes
Loose, wide escort — hulls spread, spacing generousUnknown waters. It has no confident model of where your guns are, so it is hedging across bearings.
Tight, closed-up escort — hulls drawn in on one aspectIt believes it has your coverage pinned, and it has committed its shields to the bearing it expects fire from.

Why this is the best kind of tell: it is a belief, therefore it is baitable — the whole point of §6. Feed the fleet a read, let the escort tighten against a zone you have already moved, and the shields are now committed to the wrong aspect while your actual AoR centre sits on the soft one. That is the bait-and-relocate loop operating on formation geometry instead of on a single hull's pathing, and it is the first bait that pays off against a screened target.

It also makes the propagation lag visible in a new place: a formation arriving from a spawn whose sector has not yet received the fleet's updated fear map comes in loose while its neighbours are tight. “The north's fear hasn't reached the freshly-arrived southern ships yet” now has a silhouette.

8The Commander's UI — Fog Of The Enemy's Mind

This is the payoff, not a readout. The tension you want has an exact source, and naming it says what to draw.

The dramatic engine

You never get certainty — you get a belief about their belief, and acting on a wrong guess is the drama. It's the sensation of every stealth game: not "am I hidden" but "do they think they saw me." So the UI's job is to show enough to create dread, never enough to remove doubt. A literal, accurate readout of the fleet's memory is the wrong design even though it's the obvious one — it converts a signaling game into open-information and kills the bluff the instant the receiver's cards face up.

Three registers — mirrored from the two persistent channels (Contact and Effect)

RegisterChannelFidelityRender
Exposure glint — which of your pieces the fleet has confirmedcontactHard, reliableRendered as saturation over your own fort (Jack-specified): unconfirmed ground sits slightly desaturated (twilight); the instant the fleet opens a sightline to it or lands a hit on it, that area jumps to full colour (noon-day sun). The twilight→noon gradient is the exposure read — a found wall / castle / exposed gun glows confirmed; an enclosed gun stays twilight (a ghost). DMZ / area-denial grids are always noon (the fleet always knows impassable ground). Two properties fall out (Jack): the fort's overall saturation is an at-a-glance battle-health gauge (mostly-twilight = still hidden; creeping-noon = being found), and a breach fires a sun-ray — when a troop breaks a wall, the sightline it opens to the next wall behind lights instantly like a shaft of noon into the twilight interior (the “breaching reveals the next layer” rule above, given its render).
Fear haze — where the fleet is scaredeffectSoft, uncertainA dim tiered haze over the enemy's approach lanes — "they seem to fear the north." Coarse, never numeric.
Decay — a bluff fadingeffectBehavioral onlyNo timer. The feared region visibly loses the fleet's nerve — ships edge back in. You read "my bluff is fading."
The honest risk — two fogs can read as noise

The player already reasons under board fog; now they also reason under enemy-mind fog, and stacked uncertainty reads as noise rather than tension unless the player feels agency over it. The mitigation is derivable, not empirical: make exactly one register hard and reliable — the exposure glint — so the commander always has a firm floor of "here's what I know they know," and let fear-haze and decay float soft on top. One solid rail, two atmospheric layers. Wrong toward all-soft → noise; wrong toward all-hard → the bluff dies. This is the same reason stealth games give you a reliable light gem and an unreliable sense of suspicion. The two fogs render in visibly distinct visual languages — now settled (Jack). Board fog (what you can't see) speaks in opacity: fully opaque where blind, opened by a transparent-centre / opaque-edge reveal lens that holds then collapses (fog-theaters only; see Cannon & Tower §5). Mind fog (what the fleet knows of your fort) speaks in saturation: twilight where unconfirmed, noon where confirmed. Opacity vs saturation are read by different channels of the eye, so the two never conflate — the distinct-languages requirement is met by construction. (The fear-haze and decay layers stay soft on top of the saturation rail, as below.) This whole risk is the bad-load risk (see Design Intent): stacked fogs become bookkeeping unless the fidelities summarize them into shapes the player reasons about. The one-hard-rail-plus-soft-haze structure is exactly that summarizing — it is what keeps the load reasoning-flavored, not clerical.

Precedent — the motion tracker

Alien: Isolation's tracker tells you something is near and pointedly not exactly what or where; the dread is entirely in the imprecision. Stealth detection meters (the filling eye/gem) are the exposure-glint at register 1 — a legible commitment state, never a coordinate. RTS minimaps that remember seen buildings but grey out unscouted ground are the contact-vs-effect split, already shipped. Nothing here is novel UI — it's proven UI pointed at a new target: the enemy's mind.

9The Cognition Axis — Difficulty As Estimator Quality

How the roster gets "harder as you descend" without being "more hit points." Difficulty is a monotonic decrease in how exploitable the fleet's inference is — the quality of its guess rises; the fog never lifts.

Early fleet (shallow boards)Late fleet (deep boards)
Fear decayFast — forgets quicklySlow — stubborn beliefs
Fear resolutionLow — treats a HOT region as uniformly deadlyHigher — partially separates overlapping AoRs
VerificationNone — trusts the map, blunders into coresCross-checks remembered fear against current vision; a region gone quiet gets probed, not trusted
PropagationSlow, patchyFast, coordinated
Sight range (perception)Short — forward pieces stay unseen; easy to hide fromLong — the recon lineage sees deep; hard to conceal anything
Searching fireRareDeliberate, gradient-weighted
Player must…run one trick — baits land easily and teach the signaling languagecombine tricks (relocate + hold-feint + reinforce) to fool a receiver that verifies
The structural floor

Every estimator, at every tier, slams into the same non-identifiability wall and never crosses into a gun-fix. Difficulty raises the quality of the guess; it never removes the fog. That is a designable, theory-grounded curve from board 1 to board 190 — and it is Cognition, not HP. This is the anti-treadmill promise from Design Intent, made mechanical: the enemy gets smarter, never tougher. This is the "more than more-hit-points" evolution, and it is exactly a less manipulable inference rule in signaling terms.

10Two Anchor Lineages — Named Strategies Over The Channels

Both fall directly out of the memory model; both get harder by converting effect-guesses into contact-facts better, not by gaining HP. These are the first two entries of the future roster, and they double as the minimal test cast's most interesting members.

The Hunter — punishes exposure

Operates purely on the contact channel: it can only meaningfully attack a cannon it holds a confirmed fix on — an exposed, flash-visible gun, or one a scout/troop has pinned. Against a fully-enclosed fort it is nearly inert, shelling danger-guesses for scraps. Its whole design pressure is make the player afraid to expose a gun. It is also the perfect bait victim: feed it a fix, relocate the gun, watch it pound dead ground. (Your GDD Mortar/Heavy, reborn as a channel-native unit instead of a perfect-information cheater.)

The Provocateur — forces the exposure

A durable, aggressive nuisance whose job is to draw fire: push into a soft flank so hard the player must open up on it — and every gun that fires while un-enclosed reveals a flash the fleet banks as a contact. It weaponizes the hold-fire dilemma: engage it and you light up your batteries; hold and it breaches. It turns "should I fire" into "should I reveal," the signaling game at its sharpest. (The Decoy-Mast logic, pointed outward — the enemy running a decoy on you.)

11The Evolution Matrix — Lineage × Axis preview · roster sweep is next session

Ships rhyme within a lineage (a fleet role) and diverge along axes — the same generating structure the cannon matrix uses (role × ammunition), one layer out. A small set of base lineages × a handful of evolution axes is the big combinatorial generator; the vast list gets built next session and paired down against the cannons the session after.

Base lineages (fleet roles)Evolution axes (growth directions)
bombard-walls hunt-cannons deliver-troops deliver-air support screen / tank area-deny recon 1. Durability — armour / regen / shields (the one HP-ish axis)
2. Evasion — sharper crazy-Ivan, speed, unpredictable jukes
3. Cognition — better fear-estimate, sees through fog, learns/propagates faster
4. Coordination — escorted formations, deliberate clump/space/file, screens its support
5. Firepower — harder/faster hits, AoE on shots

Two properties this buys
  • The evolution axes are the counter-web axes. A ship's growth directions are the escalation of the defender's levers — one structure expressed twice. Introduce them in roughly the order the player earns the answering lever (Introduce → Isolate → Combine).
  • "Easy early, hard late" runs mostly on axes 3 and 4, not 1. Early ships are blind blunderers that path into your bright cores and die cheap; late ships are sharp, coordinated hunters that avoid cores, find seams, and screen their support. Durability is just one of seven levers — the escalation is perception and coordination.
Minimal test cast (the spanning set, not the bestiary)

The fewest units that exercise every mechanic once, agreed as the starting place: Warship (control unit — the fire→reveal→fear loop, non-juker), Scout (recon + propagation node; kill it, flank re-blinds), Transport (memory-routing + primary bait victim + sea→land switch-moment), Carrier (sea→air switch-moment + air-density question), Support (the silent hider — never enters the fear map, gives back-line targeting its reason), and optional Hunter (the trap against cannons specifically). A test instrument, deliberately not a pair-down.

12Determinism Ledger

Every place the model touches state, and the confirmation that reproducibility holds. This is the contract the eventual SDD implements against.

MechanismDeterministic because…
Grid-scoring movement (kept from the GDD's shape)Pure function of board state; ties broken by lowest grid index (row-major).
Fear map (effect channel)Updated only from observed hit events, which are a deterministic function of player inputs. Same inputs → same field evolution.
Confirmed targets (contact channel)Recorded only on connecting hits / troop touches — deterministic events.
Searching fireSeeded search pattern weighted by the fear gradient — never live randomness.
Line of sight (channel 3)Geometric visibility computed from board state (occluders + distance) — deterministic. Opening a sightline (e.g. a breach) writes contact facts deterministically.
Between-sortie planningThe fleet's next-sortie plan is a pure function of its memory at sortie end; computed during Build/Deploy, off the real-time path. Same memory → same plan.
Crazy Ivan / hit responseDeterministic flee-impulse on the deterministic hit event (kept from the GDD).
Propagation lagBatching by spawn order (deterministic group assignment), same as troop command-confusion. Flagship alive → fast batching; sunk → slow/local. Deterministic either way.
Cross-sortie memoryPersists within a battle; resets on the player's replay (seed reset). Fleet-wide: propagated knowledge outlives sunk ships and cleared troops, but sinking a ship deterministically loses its un-propagated intel, blinds its sector, and thins the propagation network. Contact facts are revised only by contradicting observation — including line of sight, blocked by terrain/walls and capped by distance — never time-decayed.
Unit persistence across sortiesUnsunk ships persist (with their damage), boss HP persists, cannon veterans persist; troops are cleared each sortie. All deterministic given identical play.
Resolve (per-ship network)Each hull's resolve is a deterministic function of its own losses/fear/progress + peer contagion + the flag's anchor broadcast (rides the propagation net; Ships §4). Per-hull rout is evaluated continuously mid-Fight (a rout cascade = a sortie Repel, banking time); the battle-level Retreat is evaluated at the sortie boundary (can the re-formed wave hold cohesion?). Same battle → same cascades → same break points.
The invariant

Fleet behavior = pure function of (seed + observation history); observation history = pure function of player inputs. Therefore: play the same battle the same way → identical fleet reaction, shot for shot, sortie for sortie. Deviate → different, but fully reproducible.

13Keep The Idea, Throw The Definition

Reconciling this model with the pre-redesign GDD ship/troop content, per the session's mandate.

Keep (load-bearing ideas)
  • Deterministic per-type grid-scoring shape; tie-break by grid index; recalc cadence.
  • Per-type target priorities and fallback chains (walls → cannons → castles, etc.).
  • Fleet shield (ships hiding behind hulls); ship stacking; crazy Ivan.
  • The three delivery layers (sea ships / land troops / air bombers) = the three defender domains.
  • Transports, carriers, kamikazes, support ships; A* troop pathfinding + formation stacking; fog + smoke.
Throw (superseded definitions)
  • The fixed 14-ship roster and all absolute stat tables (rebuilt next session as lineage × axis).
  • Ship range and range-based accuracy falloff — replaced by AoR-avoidance: ships score for low incoming hit-probability, loitering in weak fringes, gaps, and behind shields.
  • "Tap a ship to focus fire" and anything assuming the player aims.
  • Perfect-information scoring — the old functions read dCannon, cannon HP, and true AoRs. Every such input is replaced by an inferred one from the effect/contact channels. This is the biggest single change.
